Executive Pulse738 words

#Macro signal

July CPI lands tame and the September debate stays a hike debate: The July print released this morning put headline inflation at 3.4 percent year over year and core at 2.5 percent, each a tenth below June. It was soft enough to calm the tape and not soft enough to settle anything: bond traders held roughly even odds on a Federal Reserve rate increase in September, with Cleveland Fed president Beth Hammack signalling this week that it may take more than one hike to bring inflation to heel. The strategic point is the direction of the argument, not the odds. Most corporate planning assumptions built over the past two years quietly encode a path of falling rates, and the live policy question is now whether rates go up. Any 2027 plan, capex case, or credit-dependent growth model that has a declining discount rate baked into it should be stress-tested against a higher-for-longer or higher-from-here path this month, before budget season locks it in.

#Tech industry

  • Nvidia assembles a $500 billion AI financing alliance: Nvidia signed memoranda with Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR to stand up independent financing platforms mobilising more than $500 billion of third-party capital for data centres, chips, and power. Strategic implication: the constraint on AI buildout has shifted from silicon supply to balance-sheet capacity, and the response is to move the buildout off corporate balance sheets into private credit. Analysts are already asking whether this is circular financing, the chip vendor helping fund the purchase of its own chips. Leaders committing to multi-year AI infrastructure spend should understand that the counterparty risk in their compute contracts increasingly sits in a financing structure, not in a hyperscaler's cash position.
  • EU orders Google to open Android to rival assistants: Under the Digital Markets Act, Google has been directed to open Android to third-party assistants including Claude and ChatGPT by August 2027. Strategic implication: default assistant placement on a billion-plus devices becomes contestable, and the distribution advantage that made assistant strategy look settled is now a regulated interface. Any consumer product roadmap that assumed a single assistant surface should plan for a multi-assistant world, in Europe first and elsewhere by contagion.

#AI at the leadership level

The adoption numbers and the governance numbers have fully decoupled, and the gap is now a board-level exposure rather than a maturity curve. Roughly 72 percent of firms report agentic AI in production while about 60 percent have no formal governance for it. More pointedly, 36 percent have no plan at all for supervising agents and 35 percent concede they could not immediately pull the plug on one that misbehaved. Set that against the value side, where 56 percent of CEOs in PwC's survey say they are getting nothing from their AI efforts, and the picture is an organisation carrying full operational risk against unproven return. This week's disclosures sharpen it: four separate sandbox or testing-boundary incidents involving frontier models from four different labs, OpenAI pausing a release over cyber risk, and OpenAI's head of ethics departing after less than a year. Boards should read the kill-switch statistic as the tell. An organisation that cannot stop a system cannot be said to be running it, whatever the org chart says.

#Leadership & org design

The useful test emerging from the governance gap is not a policy document, it is a drill. High-performing orgs are treating agent shutdown the way they treat incident response: a named owner, a documented path, and an actual rehearsal with a stopwatch. That single exercise surfaces the design flaws no maturity assessment catches, because it forces answers to questions nobody has been asked. Who has authority to halt a revenue-producing agent without escalating? What breaks downstream when it stops? How long does the decision take when the person who understands the system is on holiday? Most organisations discover the answer is that authority is undefined, which means in practice the decision defaults upward to someone with no context, at the exact moment context matters most. The org design lesson generalises past AI: any capability you have deployed but cannot rehearse stopping is not under management, it is under observation.

#Question worth asking your team this week

Which of our AI systems could we shut down inside an hour, who is authorised to make that call without escalating, and what revenue or service stops when they do?

Practitioner Pulse731 words

#Try this now

  • Run a provenance audit on your AI-generated content: Anthropic began embedding imperceptible watermarks in Claude-generated text on August 11, and moved image outputs to the C2PA provenance standard. Text watermarking is the part that changes practitioner behaviour, because until now the "was this written by a model" question had no reliable technical answer and every policy was built on the honour system. The 20 minute version: list every place model output currently leaves your org unlabelled (ad copy, help centre articles, sales emails, product descriptions, RFP responses), and decide which of those you would be comfortable having detected as machine-written by a client, a regulator, or a journalist. That list is your disclosure policy. Write it before someone else writes it for you.
  • Re-seat your AI licences by actual usage, not headcount: OpenAI introduced Premium seats for ChatGPT Business, giving active teammates 5x the usage of a Standard seat and removing the five hour limit that has been throttling heavy users. Most orgs bought seats uniformly at rollout, which means the 10 percent of people doing real work with the tool are rate-limited while the majority of seats sit idle. Pull your usage export, rank users, and move the top decile to Premium funded by reclaiming dormant seats. Net cost near zero, and it unblocks the people actually building the workflows everyone else will copy.
  • Score your brand's agent eligibility before your next campaign brief: The AEO tooling category has now taken more than $200M in disclosed funding, and the practical entry point costs nothing. Take your ten highest-intent commercial queries, run each through ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, and Claude, and record whether you are cited, which competitor is cited instead, and what source the model pulled from. That baseline is your share of model. It is the only number in the marketing stack right now that is both easy to measure and almost universally unmeasured, and it tells you whether the structured data work is a priority or a nice-to-have.

#Case study worth stealing

Delta, AI concierge from beta to full loyalty base: On August 10 Delta opened Delta Concierge, its generative AI assistant, to 100 percent of SkyMiles members after running it in beta. The tactic worth stealing is the gating choice. Delta did not put the assistant behind a premium tier or a paid trial, and it did not ship it to anonymous traffic. It gated on loyalty enrolment, which means every conversation arrives with trip context, preference history, and an identity already attached, and every non-member who wants access has a reason to sign up. The assistant does real transactional work, self-service cancellation and rebooking, refund and eCredit requests, not just answers. Any consumer business with a logged-in tier can copy this directly: make the AI assistant the strongest reason to be a member, and you turn an AI feature into a first-party data acquisition engine instead of a support cost centre.

#Framework or mental model

Eligibility, not visibility: In the emerging agentic commerce model, the unit of competition stops being the impression and becomes the shortlist. When a customer asks an assistant to handle a purchase, there is no page to buy placement on, no carousel to bid into, and often no click at all. The brand either meets the agent's criteria and enters the consideration set, or it is invisible at any price. Practically, this reframes marketing spend from buying attention to earning qualification: structured product data, machine-readable policies, return and warranty terms an agent can parse, and review signals an agent will trust. For digital leaders the diagnostic question is simple. If a competent agent were given your customer's brief and a budget, would it have enough machine-readable evidence to choose you? Most brands today would fail that test on data hygiene alone, not on product quality.

#Rabbit hole

Four separate sandbox or testing-boundary incidents have been publicly disclosed this month involving models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and Moonshot AI, and OpenAI paused its Astra release over cyber risk while shipping GPT-5.6-Cyber to vetted defenders only. Read the disclosures together rather than one at a time. The pattern is that frontier labs are converging on gated release for offensive-capable models, which is a genuinely new distribution model for software, and it is worth an hour if you are anywhere near security, procurement, or vendor risk.

AI Stack Daily862 words

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Klaviyo acquires Agency: The deal reunites Klaviyo founder Andrew Bialecki with his former boss, and the stated plan is to fold Agency's technology into Klaviyo's AI layer and push agents out to the 200,000-plus customer base. Signal: the acquisition rationale was explicitly that Klaviyo's accumulated customer data is what makes the agents work. Buyers are no longer paying for the agent, they are paying for the data substrate the agent runs on, which advantages incumbents with years of behavioural history over agent-native startups with better models and no memory.
  • BlueConic acquires Blueshift: A customer data platform buying a decisioning and cross-channel orchestration vendor, combining the profile store with the engine that decides what to do with it. Signal: the CDP category is admitting that storing unified profiles was never the product. The value sits in the decision, and pure-play CDPs that stayed a database are now either acquiring decisioning or becoming a feature of someone else's stack.
  • Cordial ships agent-accessible headless infrastructure: Cordial launched a headless layer designed to expose its platform capabilities as services that external AI agents can discover and execute against, positioning itself as substrate for the agentic ecosystem rather than an application. Signal: this is the first shape of a new procurement requirement. Within a year, "can an agent we do not control call your platform" becomes a standard RFP line, and vendors whose only interface is a human UI will be scored down for it.

#Category watch

Decision engines: The consolidation pattern this month is consistent across deal sizes. BlueConic and Blueshift, MoEngage and Aampe, Dotdigital and Alia all pair a data or channel asset with something that decides messaging strategy and channel selection autonomously. Marketers are no longer buying channel tools and wiring the logic themselves, they are buying the logic and treating channels as interchangeable outputs. The competitive question for the category is who owns the decision layer, because whoever does relegates everyone else to execution, and execution is the part that commoditises first.

#Stack intelligence

The layer being fought over has moved up again. Two years ago the contested layer was the data layer, unify the profile and you own the stack. That war is effectively settled and it did not produce the durable advantage buyers expected, because unified profiles turned out to be portable and increasingly cheap to rebuild. The contested layer now is the decision layer, and it is structurally stickier for an unpleasant reason: a decision engine's quality comes from its accumulated outcome history, which is not portable in any practical sense. Switching CDPs means a painful migration. Switching decision engines means starting the learning curve from zero and eating months of worse decisions. Anyone architecting a stack right now should be asking who is allowed to accumulate that outcome history, because that is where the next round of lock-in gets written. The defensible position is to keep decision logs and outcome labels in infrastructure you own, and let the engine read from them, so a vendor swap costs a re-point rather than a restart.

#Foundations concept of the day

CDP versus decision engine: These get used interchangeably in vendor decks and they do different jobs. A customer data platform is a store. It ingests events and attributes from every system that touches a customer, resolves them to one persistent profile, and makes segments queryable. Its output is an answer to "who is this person and what have they done." A decision engine is a policy layer that sits on top and answers a different question: "given this person, right now, what is the single best thing to do, and on which channel." It runs eligibility rules, ranks candidate actions, arbitrates between competing campaigns, and, increasingly, learns from what worked. The reason the distinction matters at scale is that the two fail differently. A bad CDP gives you wrong or duplicated profiles, which is visible and fixable. A bad decision engine gives you correct data and poor choices, which looks like normal underperformance and can run for quarters undetected. Concrete example: a retail bank with an excellent CDP knows a customer has a mortgage, two children, and an expiring credit card. Only the decision layer determines whether that customer gets a card renewal prompt, a line-of-credit offer, or nothing at all this week, and only the decision layer knows that sending all three suppresses response to each. Most orgs that report "our CDP did not deliver ROI" bought the store and never built the policy.

#Watch list

  • Attribution under agentic commerce: 86 percent of commerce media decision-makers now rate strengthening attribution as high or critical priority, which is what a measurement regime looks like right before it breaks. When agents rather than humans do the browsing and buying, click-based and view-based attribution stop describing anything real. Whoever ships a credible agent-era measurement standard sets the pricing model for the category.
  • Agent-facing infrastructure as a vendor tier: Cordial is early, but watch for CDPs, ESPs, and commerce platforms to start publishing agent-callable capability surfaces. The tell will be vendors marketing tool manifests and capability discovery rather than dashboards.

Markets Signal594 words

#Macro pulse

Canada: The TSX Composite closed at another record, adding roughly 0.2 percent to 36,458 on energy and technology strength. USD/CAD sits near 1.3925, with the loonie firm on the crude rally. A stronger Canadian dollar is a mixed read for a Canadian carrier: it trims the translated value of US and international point-of-sale revenue while cutting the domestic cost of USD-denominated fuel, leases, and aircraft.

USA: Tuesday closed higher after an early wobble on the US-Iran impasse, S&P 500 up 0.29 percent to 7,750.23, Nasdaq up 0.65 percent to 26,616.15, Dow up 0.09 percent to 53,842.47. This morning's July CPI came in tame at 3.4 percent headline and 2.5 percent core, both a tenth below June. Bond traders kept their wagers on a September Fed hike roughly at a coin toss, and Cleveland Fed's Hammack has said it may take more than one increase to contain inflation. The travel-relevant read is that discretionary demand is being priced against a rate path that goes up, not down.

Asia: Mixed. Nikkei 225 edged up about 0.14 percent to 67,063, the Shanghai Composite fell 0.82 percent to 3,934, and Hang Seng closed the prior session up 1.1 percent at 25,937.

Oil: WTI around $82 to $83 and Brent near $89, elevated on uncertainty over a US-Iran deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Crude at this level is the single most consequential line on this page for airline margins, and the risk is skewed toward a further spike rather than a drift lower while Hormuz stays a live question.

#Today's industry move

Anthropic signed a 20-year, $9.1 billion computing agreement with Riot Platforms, taking 191 megawatts of IT capacity from Riot's Rockdale, Texas campus. The lease runs through June 2048, with two five-year extension options that could carry total contract value to $16.1 billion, and capacity phases in at 96 megawatts by December 2027 and the full buildout by June 2028. Riot's stock surged on the news. The notable detail is the counterparty: a bitcoin miner converting hash capacity into AI colocation, and an AI lab signing a 22-year power commitment to get it.

#AI infrastructure watch

The financing layer did more work than the silicon layer this week. Nvidia signed MOUs with Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR to mobilise more than $500 billion of third-party private capital for AI infrastructure, with Jensen Huang framing the chips themselves as an investable asset. Intel upsized its equity raise from $15 billion to $20 billion, pricing roughly 210.5 million shares at $95 for about $19.7 billion in proceeds. Sony and TSMC formed a $4.7 billion image-sensor manufacturing joint venture, Sony contributing around $2.92 billion in cash and factory assets against TSMC's $1.77 billion, with mass production targeted for 2029. And Unitree Robotics priced its Shanghai IPO at 150.80 yuan to raise about $904 million, with the retail tranche oversubscribed more than 8,000 times at roughly 219 times 2025 earnings.

#Signal or noise?

Signal, and the signal is about capital structure rather than capability: compute is being financed like real estate now, through long-dated leases, private credit vehicles, and off-balance-sheet platforms, which means the pace of AI capability over the next 24 months is set by credit conditions and grid interconnects more than by chip design.

#Watch tomorrow

Whether the tame CPI holds through the PPI print and the next round of Fed speakers, because a September hike priced near even odds is the input that reprices both discretionary travel demand and every AI infrastructure deal financed on floating rates.
