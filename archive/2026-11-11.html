#Macro signal

A hot October CPI has made a December Fed hike the base case, and a half-point move is now a live debate: US consumer prices rose 0.4 percent in October against a 0.3 percent forecast, with core up 0.3 percent against 0.2 percent expected, the third straight 0.3 percent core print. September headline had been 0.6 percent. Energy rose 0.7 percent, food 0.5 percent, transportation services 0.8 percent, shelter 0.3 percent and accounted for more than 30 percent of the headline gain, and electricity rose 0.9 percent, which CNBC tied to AI infrastructure demand. No year-over-year rate was published because of the data gaps left by the 2025 government shutdown. Governor Christopher Waller had said beforehand that a core reading "around 0.3" should put a half-point hike on the table and that a quarter point could risk credibility, and the print landed exactly on his threshold. The two-year Treasury yield jumped roughly 15 to 17 basis points to about 4.73 percent, its biggest one-day rise since April 2025, the 10-year rose about 10 basis points to 4.98 percent, and the 30-year reached 5.35 percent. CME FedWatch put a quarter-point hike at the 8 to 9 December meeting at about 88 percent, with odds of a half point moving from near zero to around 13.6 percent. The policy rate currently sits at 3.75 to 4 percent. The dollar index rose 0.58 percent, gold fell about 2 percent, and the S&P 500 slipped 0.38 percent.

The strategic read is that the rate path most 2027 plans were built on, a slow glide lower, is no longer the consensus. It is a tightening cycle with a credible risk of acceleration. Budgets now being finalized should be stress-tested against a cost of capital that rises into the new year, not one that falls. For technology, product and marketing leaders the immediate exposure is the hurdle rate on multi-year platform investments and any initiative whose business case depends on cheap financing for customers, partners or the company itself. The electricity line is the less obvious tell: AI compute demand is now showing up in the consumer inflation basket, which means the AI build-out is becoming a monetary policy input, not just a capex story.

#Tech industry

  • Lenders are trying to offload AI infrastructure debt, and GPU loans are taking the worst discounts: ING put a package of twelve data center loans, about $500 million, out for competitive bids, with roughly $25 million backing the Stargate project and bids closing 19 November. Société Générale is shopping up to $300 million of Stargate loans, split between a campus in Shackelford County, Texas and a project in Port Washington, Wisconsin, asking 95 cents on the dollar after rejecting a bid in the 80s. Semafor reports whole portfolios of GPU financings being shopped at steep discounts on worries that loan terms outlast the useful life of the chips, while Goldman Sachs and Apollo market a $40 billion chip financing for SpaceX. Ares Management CEO Michael Arougheti said he personally does not know "how to underwrite the decline and depreciation curves on that type of technology." 9fin attributes part of the pressure to the Democratic sweep in the midterms and a slew of ballot votes to limit data center development. Strategic implication: the credit market is now pricing what equity markets have mostly not, that AI hardware is a short-lived asset and that community and political resistance to the build-out is a real risk. Any enterprise AI business case that assumes compute keeps getting cheaper because capacity keeps getting financed should carry a scenario where financing tightens and capacity growth slows. It is also a useful lens for vendor due diligence: ask your AI infrastructure providers how their own capacity is funded.
  • Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into its CEO, nine days before earnings: The board said on 8 November that a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside counsel, will investigate reporting that Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. It stated it has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised" and that it has confidence in Huang, who will keep leading the company. The shares fell 5.47 percent on Tuesday, a second straight decline, ahead of third-quarter results on 17 November and a closed-door briefing House committee chairs have requested for 18 November. Strategic implication: the most important single supplier in the AI stack now carries visible key-person and geopolitical risk at the same time, and Bloomberg reported back in January that the company lacks a clear successor. Few enterprise AI roadmaps name single-supplier concentration at the silicon layer as a risk. This is a reasonable week to add it to the register, along with the question of what happens to your plans if export policy around that supplier tightens again.

#AI at the leadership level

Deloitte released a global survey on 10 November of 806 board members and C-suite executives across 53 countries, and the headline number is how little of the AI conversation in boardrooms touches the workforce. Only 16 percent of organizations have an established strategy for the workforce impact of technology transformation. 49 percent say their boards do not consistently bring human capital into AI discussions, 49 percent report limited or no oversight of how investment is split between technology and people, and only 12 percent report full oversight with active monitoring. 42 percent say board AI discussion centers on productivity outcomes, and 38 percent lack confidence that their current HR and IT structures fit an AI-enabled workforce. A WTW board survey published 4 November found the same gap between AI ambition and oversight of talent, skills and culture.

The implication is that most boards are governing AI as a technology investment with a productivity return, while the actual risk sits in the redesign of work that the return depends on. A productivity number reported without a workforce plan underneath it is a forecast nobody owns. The practical fix is structural rather than rhetorical: put technology spend and workforce spend for AI programs in the same board paper, with the same owner, so the trade-off between them is visible rather than inferred.

#Leadership & org design

PepsiCo and Boeing both announced cuts on 9 November, and the stated reasons are worth separating. PepsiCo North America CEO Ram Krishnan told employees in a memo that about 1,000 jobs, under 1 percent of roughly 125,000 US staff, will go over the coming week, explicitly to remove layers of management and speed decision-making, not to shrink manufacturing. It follows an October warning that North American sales and margins were recovering more slowly than expected, with Elliott holding a $4 billion stake. Boeing Commercial Airplanes CEO Stephanie Pope announced a roughly 5 percent reduction, about 2,500 jobs through layoffs and attrition with notices in December, because slowing the production ramp "puts us in an overstaffed position."

These are two different diagnoses. Boeing is matching headcount to a lower output plan. PepsiCo is saying the organization is too slow for the market, and that the cost of slowness sits in the management layers rather than on the line. That second diagnosis is now the most common rationale for cuts in 2026, and it is the one leaders should audit most carefully in their own organizations, because removing layers only speeds decisions if decision rights actually move down with them. If approvals simply concentrate on fewer, busier executives, the org gets flatter and slower at the same time.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If our cost of capital rises another 50 basis points by March and the AI compute we are counting on gets more expensive rather than cheaper, which of our 2027 commitments still clear the bar, and who would make that call?

#Try this now

  • Claude Data certified queries, to end the "whose number is right" meeting: Anthropic moved Claude Data to general availability on November 10 on paid plans, with connectors to Redshift, BigQuery, ClickHouse, Databricks, Snowflake, and Salesforce. It builds dashboards, data stories, and analyses from plain-language questions and writes and runs the SQL. The part worth 20 minutes is in beta: certified queries, which are approved metric definitions that carry a badge, plus live sharing so colleagues can view without holding source credentials. Pick the three metrics your leadership team argues about most (conversion, active members, revenue per visit), write the definition once, certify it, and point every dashboard at the certified version. Enterprise plans get Data switched on by default November 19, so the definitions are worth settling before people start building their own.
  • Claude Whiteboards for turning a planning conversation into a board with owners: Launched in beta the same day on all paid plans, alongside Claude Sheets. A typed or spoken plan becomes a layout with a timeline, owners, and dates, which can be converted into a doc or deck or sent to Miro, Lucid, or Zoho Vani. Sheets is deliberately light: 100,000 cells, 144 functions, no pivot tables or macros, exports to .xlsx and CSV. The practical test this week: paste the notes from your last quarterly planning session into Whiteboards and compare the result against the board someone spent an afternoon building by hand. If it gets you 80 percent of the way, the planning ritual just lost its most tedious step.
  • Attentive's MCP server, for running SMS and email segments from the assistant you already use: Attentive announced its MCP server as generally available on November 9, letting ChatGPT and Claude read approved Attentive data, build segments, and create or edit campaigns and journeys. The connection can be set to read-only or write. Start read-only: ask the assistant to describe your ten largest segments and the journeys that touch each one, and see whether the answer matches what the team believes is live. That audit alone usually surfaces overlapping journeys and dead segments. Turn on write access only once someone owns review of what the assistant changes.

#Case study worth stealing

United Airlines, letting agents shop but not check out: United said this week it will let third-party AI agents research routes, compare schedules, and surface fares, but will not let them complete bookings on their own, citing its existing Terms of Use against unauthorized automated activity. A human finalizes the purchase, or the booking runs through a direct partnership with United. The booking APIs exist, but access is keyed, and agents acting on their own are not being handed keys. Delta, JPMorgan Chase, and Amazon set similar limits in October. The tactic any digital team can adapt is splitting the funnel into a discovery layer you open up and a transaction layer you gate. You stay visible to agents, and checkout, where the data capture, upsell, and loyalty enrollment happen, stays on your own property. Decide which of your flows belong in each layer now, while the policy can still be a choice rather than a reaction.

#Framework or mental model

The trust-capability gap in AI service: Twilio's Customer Insights Series, surfaced again this week, lays the gap out in three pairs. 63 percent of consumers want easy escalation to a human and 47 percent of brands offer it. 56 percent want a human to approve AI actions and 42 percent of brands require it. 49 percent want transparency over their data and 19 percent of brands offer consent-based access. The same research found 81 percent of brands say their agent identifies itself as AI upfront while only 22 percent of consumers say that happens, and 78 percent of consumers have tried to get past an AI agent to a human. Use the three pairs as a scorecard on any customer-facing agent before launch: escalation path, approval threshold, data consent. Each one you can't answer is a reason customers will go around the agent.

#Rabbit hole (optional)

Propellic, Agentic Travel Booking in Muse and Instinct: A behavioral study of how Meta's Muse and the invite-only Instinct handle travel, built on 176 scripted booking requests for hotels, flights, and tours, surveys of 167 Muse and 50 Instinct users, and 22 recorded interviews that included a live booking task. In 57 percent of flight runs the booking would have gone through Duffel, the booking API behind both agents, even when the user named an airline. Only 28 percent went direct to the supplier and 14 percent through platforms like Booking.com and Expedia. On the consumer side, 66 percent of real conversations stopped at research or shortlist, about half of users stepped in before payment, and 36 percent said they would let an agent pay outright. Read it next to the United policy: one shows suppliers gating checkout, the other shows agents rebuilding an intermediary layer anyway. Worth the hour for anyone who owns direct booking or loyalty. https://www.propellic.com/research/agentic-travel-booking-in-meta-muse-and-instinct

#Vendor & platform moves

  • The Trade Desk posted its first year-over-year revenue decline since its 2016 IPO: Q3 2026 results, reported 9 November, showed revenue of $736 million against $739 million a year earlier, a 0.5% drop, with net income down 67% to $38 million and adjusted EBITDA down 16% to $266 million. Q4 guidance of at least $755 million sits roughly 11% below Q4 2025's $847 million, and full-year 2026 is tracking near flat on 2025's $2.896 billion. The release leaned on Kokai, its Audience Unlimited data marketplace and measurement work, and did not address Amazon's fee competition, Safari's iOS 27 blocking of its ad domains, or the non-exclusive Walmart renewal (per PPC Land). Signal: the largest independent DSP is no longer growing while the platforms that own both the audience and the inventory keep taking share. The independent buy-side pitch was always "objectivity across the open internet." That pitch is now being priced against walled gardens that undercut on fees and agents that compress the trading desk labour a DSP seat used to justify. Expect every independent DSP renewal in 2027 to be a fee negotiation, not a feature review.
  • Viant bought Waymark, a generative AI video ad platform built for connected TV: Disclosed alongside Q3 results on 9 November, in which revenue rose 29% to $110.4 million and CTV passed 50% of advertiser spend, growing nearly 40% year over year. AdExchanger reported the price at $20 million. Waymark turns brand inputs into finished video ads with scripts, visuals and voiceover, and Viant says it will combine that with its audience, content and attention data to produce audience-tailored CTV creative "in minutes and at a fraction of traditional costs." It follows Viant's $40 million TVision measurement deal. Signal: the binding constraint on CTV growth has shifted from inventory to creative. Programmatic CTV lets a buyer target thousands of audience slices, but most mid-market advertisers own one or two TV spots. A DSP that can manufacture the creative closes that gap and captures the budget that never existed because no ad did. Buy, measure, and now make: the DSP is turning into a vertically integrated media agency.
  • G2 unified G2, Capterra, Software Advice and GetApp into a single marketplace and ad platform: Announced 10 November at G2 Reach, the combined property covers 6 million+ reviews across 2,000+ categories and 200 million annual buyers, with one campaign setup across all four sites, a pay-per-click product, a "Clicks Plus" tier that de-anonymizes clicks with firmographic data, and an expanded agent evaluations program that now includes Voice AI Agents. G2's CPTO framed the thesis directly: "AI is the curtain, and all that gets through is the evidence." Signal: B2B review sites are repositioning from lead-gen directories into the evidence layer that LLMs cite when they recommend software. Consolidating the four largest properties concentrates that layer in one owner, which turns third-party proof into something closer to a toll road for anyone selling software into an AI-mediated buying process.

#Category watch

Ad inventory inside AI answers: AdExchanger's 11 November roundup tracks the formats multiplying at once: ChatGPT "visual ads" shown while images generate, Google multi-turn ads in AI Mode across 10+ markets, Bing ads placed atop AI responses, and Yahoo Scout leaving beta with ad support. The same roundup reports Google's stock fell more than 10% after Q3 on a search ad slowdown attributed to its own AI Mode cannibalizing classic results, with six advertiser and agency executives describing shifts of budget to Meta. The pattern is that answer engines are creating scarce, untested inventory faster than anyone has built measurement for it. For a martech org, the practical issue is not whether to test, it is that none of these formats yet plug cleanly into existing attribution, frequency or brand-safety tooling, so early spend will be judged on platform-reported numbers alone.

#Stack intelligence

Auction auditability is becoming an architecture requirement rather than a procurement clause. The FTC and 22 states' amended complaint against Amazon, unsealed 5 November, alleges an undisclosed "soft reserve price" added from 2019 that pushed advertisers' costs up to 50% above the auction result on ordinary days and up to 250% on Prime Day and Black Friday, while the auction was still described as second-price. Internally, per the complaint, the reason for non-disclosure was to avoid "irrevocable damage to advertiser trust." Put that next to the agentic buying now spreading across SSPs, DSPs and commerce platforms, and the exposure is obvious: when an agent bids on your behalf inside an auction you cannot inspect, there are two black boxes stacked on each other. The teams adapting fastest are logging bid, clearing price and outcome on their side of every automated buy, through log-level data agreements or server-side pixels, so that the spend can be reconstructed independently of the seller's reporting. If a platform will not provide log-level data, that is now a pricing input, not a footnote.

#Foundations concept of the day

Dynamic creative optimization (DCO): a system that assembles an ad at the moment it is served from interchangeable parts, such as headline, image, offer, price and call to action, choosing the combination most likely to work for that viewer, context and moment. Instead of producing one finished ad, a team produces a template and a library of components. A decision engine reads signals available at serve time (location, weather, device, audience segment, product inventory, past behaviour) and picks the components, then a testing loop shifts traffic toward the combinations that perform. Classic DCO only remixes assets a human already made. The newer generative version, which the Viant and Waymark deal is aimed at, creates the variants themselves, including full video with script and voiceover.

Why it matters at scale: targeting precision is worthless without matching creative. A retailer that can address 500 audience segments but runs three ads is paying for precision it never uses. DCO is how the creative side catches up with the data side, and its real cost is governance, not software, because brand, legal and compliance teams must pre-approve a component library instead of reviewing finished ads. A cross-industry example: a travel brand showing a single "book now" banner can instead show each viewer a fare to the destination they last searched, from their nearest departure city, priced from live inventory, and hide the offer automatically when the fare sells out. The same mechanics run a bank's rate offers or a streaming service's title promos. The organisational question to settle before buying any DCO tool is who owns the component library and who signs off on combinations nobody has seen.

#Watch list

  • Shopify Campaign Autopilot as a buyer of media. Generally available to all merchants since the 28 October Fall '26 Editions, it builds and runs campaigns across Meta, Microsoft Advertising, Snapchat, Shop and Shopify Messaging from store catalog and customer data, with X Ads and ChatGPT Ads announced next. AdExchanger flagged it 10 November as Shopify pushing "further into ad tech." A commerce platform allocating millions of merchants' budgets across channels is, functionally, a new kind of demand-side platform, and it sits on first-party order data none of the channels own.
  • Reddit and Qloo. AdExchanger reported on 10 November that Reddit is using Qloo, a taste and cultural-affinity data company, to strengthen predictive targeting. Interest-graph data from outside the platform feeding a community platform's targeting is a small deal worth watching as a template for how non-identity signals get monetized as cookies and device IDs keep thinning.

#Macro pulse

A holiday session with thin volume and a sharp AI rebound. Equities are open in both countries today. The US bond market is closed for Veterans Day, so there is no Treasury tape to confirm or fade the equity move, which makes intraday numbers louder than usual.

Canada: The TSX fell 0.8 percent on Tuesday 10 November as gold miners sold off on the hot US inflation print. Wednesday intraday it has recovered, up about 0.7 percent near 36,472, led by energy as crude bounces (Canadian Natural, Suncor and Imperial each up around 1 percent) with Celestica up 6 percent on the AI bid. The loonie is the story to watch: USD/CAD at 1.4248, up 0.42 percent, just under the 0.5 percent flag line. The driver is a widening rate gap. The Bank of Canada sits at 2.5 percent after its last 25 basis point hike, while US markets now price roughly 90 percent odds of a December Fed hike. A weaker loonie lifts the Canadian-dollar cost of every US-dollar fuel invoice, lease and debt payment a Canadian carrier carries.

United States: Tuesday 10 November closed lower after October CPI rose 0.4 percent month over month against 0.3 percent expected, the third straight hot month. S&P 500 down 0.38 percent to 7,520.93, Nasdaq down 0.74 percent to 25,065.87, Dow down 157.86 points or 0.30 percent to 52,231.89. Wednesday intraday has reversed hard on CoreWeave's results: Nasdaq up roughly 2 to 2.3 percent, S&P 500 up roughly 1.1 to 1.5 percent depending on the snapshot, Dow up about 0.5 percent. Goldman Sachs now forecasts a further hike in March 2027 on top of December. Rate hikes into a consumer already absorbing three months of hot prices is the discretionary travel risk for 2027 planning.

Asia (11 November close): Nikkei 225 up 1.31 percent to 62,184, led by chip equipment names (Advantest up 5.9 percent, Lasertec up 6.3 percent), though SoftBank Group fell. Shanghai Composite up 0.46 percent to 3,680.64, tech led with Cambricon up 7.4 percent. Hang Seng the outlier, down about 1.0 percent to 24,315 on profit-taking in Tencent and Xiaomi and worry about higher US rates.

Oil: WTI around $86.94, up about 1 percent, bouncing off a 10-week low after falling almost 20 percent from late-October peaks. Brent near $93.07. The supply side is loosening: Saudi output jumped 26 percent in October to 10.56 million barrels a day, and US crude inventories rose 9 million barrels last week. For airline cost structures, the direction of travel since October is favourable even with today's bounce.

#Today's industry move

Anthropic's IPO is moving toward pricing. CNBC reports a range of $70 to $78 per share on 825 million shares, a valuation of up to $1.4 trillion, and a combined IPO plus private placement raise of up to $90.4 billion, with trading possible as early as next week. A listing at that size turns frontier model economics from private-round speculation into a quarterly disclosure regime. For enterprise buyers, that means model pricing, capacity commitments and margin pressure become publicly scrutinised, which tends to make vendor roadmaps more predictable and pricing more disciplined.

#AI infrastructure watch

CoreWeave jumped about 20 percent after Q3 results, raising full-year 2026 guidance. Management said it is 10x oversubscribed on existing 2027 capacity and multiples oversubscribed on 2028, with around $9 billion in customer prepayments and deposits. Prepayments are the important number: customers are funding the build, which answers last week's debt worry more convincingly than revenue growth alone. Chip names followed, with Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD, Intel and Micron up as much as 7 percent.

The counterweight came from SoftBank Group, which posted its first net loss in nearly two years, a 1.12 trillion yen loss including a $3.7 billion impairment on US data-center projects, and fell about 3 percent. Same day, same layer, opposite outcomes: demand for compute capacity is real, but not every developer of that capacity will capture the value.

#Signal or noise?

Partly noise on volume, since a bond-market holiday exaggerates equity swings, but CoreWeave's prepayment and oversubscription numbers are signal that AI capacity demand through 2028 is contracted, not hoped for.

#Watch tomorrow

The bond market reopens Thursday. If Treasury yields push higher on the CPI repricing, the AI rally and USD/CAD both get tested, and a loonie through 1.43 would start to show up in Canadian carrier Q1 cost forecasts.
