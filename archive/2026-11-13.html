#Macro signal

Producer prices show the next six months of consumer inflation already in the pipeline, and supply routes are not yet safe enough to bring them down: The October Producer Price Index, released 13 November, rose 0.3 percent on the month, a touch below the 0.4 percent forecast, but the annual rate hit 6.5 percent, the highest since November 2022. Final demand goods are up 10.2 percent on the year. The more telling numbers sit upstream: intermediate processed goods are up 14.6 percent year over year, the biggest gain since July 2022, and intermediate services are up 6.9 percent. The core measure that strips out food, energy and trade services rose 0.5 percent for a second straight month and is up 4.9 percent annually. Truck freight rose 3.9 percent and accounted for more than 80 percent of the services increase. Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee said that "in categories that are not driven by oil prices and not driven by tariffs, you're still seeing stalled progress," and markets now fully price a quarter-point hike at the 8 to 9 December meeting. Energy is not offering relief either: Brent sat near $95 on 13 November after a 16 percent drop the prior week, while Iran's military said four tankers were hit by explosions in the Strait of Hormuz and the Houthis claimed strikes on Saudi pipeline and Aramco facilities.

The strategic read: intermediate prices are what suppliers pay before they bill you. A 14.6 percent rise at that stage tends to show up in vendor invoices, logistics contracts and agency rate cards over the next two to three quarters, regardless of what the Fed does in December. Procurement leaders should be asking which 2027 contracts carry index-linked escalators and which renew in the first half. Marketing and product leaders should assume that freight-heavy and physical-goods partners, including fulfillment, print, events and retail media with logistics components, will reprice first.

#Tech industry

  • Microsoft's cloud went down globally for roughly 18 hours, taking email, collaboration, AI and security tools with it at the same time: The outage began on Thursday 12 November with Outlook and Teams, then spread from the Americas to the rest of the world, hitting consumer and business email, OneDrive files and documents, SharePoint, Copilot, Defender security software, Intune admin dashboards and cloud-hosted Windows PCs, per TechCrunch and BleepingComputer. Microsoft attributed it to "a large number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable," said there was "no evidence of malicious activity," and had not named a root cause by Friday morning as Outlook began recovering. It follows a similar major outage in September blamed on a "core authentication configuration" issue. Strategic implication: the risk here is not downtime, it is correlated downtime. When communication, documents, the AI assistant and the security console all sit on one provider, a single failure removes the tools a team would use to coordinate a response, and the tools that would detect an attack happening under cover of the outage. Business continuity plans written for a data center loss rarely cover "no email, no chat, no Copilot, no endpoint console" at once. That scenario now has two real-world occurrences in three months and belongs in the next tabletop exercise.
  • Nvidia's leadership crisis widened in the 24 hours after the CEO's exit, and the board's next decision is a technologist versus a steward: Since Jensen Huang's resignation on 12 November, Reuters reported a third affair, with a second Nvidia employee. The Senate Banking Committee requested testimony from Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on "significant national security concerns," with Chair Tim Scott warning of a subpoena, and Manhattan federal prosecutors are examining whether entities linked to the alleged intelligence contact helped smuggle Super Micro products into China. Interim CEO Colette Kress, who remains CFO, told employees a company meeting is set for 18 November, the day after earnings, and has held calls with customers and suppliers. Analysts cut targets hard: Baird halved its target to $200 and downgraded to neutral, and Cantor Fitzgerald cut from $350 to $250. Executive search consultant Scott W. Simmons told Fortune he expects the board to pick a technologist, with hyperscale computing head Ian Buck the most cited internal name. Strategic implication: for enterprise buyers, the near-term risk is less about supply and more about roadmap and policy. An interim leader who is also the finance chief will tend to protect commitments and margin, not launch new bets, and the congressional and export-control scrutiny now attached to the company could reshape what it is allowed to sell and to whom. Next week's earnings call is the first chance to hear whether product cadence commitments still stand.

#AI at the leadership level

Two items this week show where AI governance is actually failing: at the top of the organization and at the edges of the supply chain. A Stanford Graduate School of Business survey of corporate directors, published 10 November, found 80 percent have no formal AI ethics or responsibility policies, 70 percent say their board lacks clearly defined AI governance responsibility, and board readiness to oversee AI averaged 4.7 out of 10. At the same time, 75 percent of directors use AI for research and market analysis and 55 percent for board preparation, while only 54 percent have clear guidance from management on appropriate use. One director described it as "trying to change the wheels on the bus while driving it at 100 miles per hour downhill."

The supply-chain side arrived the same week. Notion disclosed that fewer than 1 percent of workspaces had Notion AI chat transcripts exposed through Fireworks AI, an inference provider it used to serve open-weight models, after attackers compromised a third-party cloud provider hosting Fireworks development machines on 23 October. Notion cut off Fireworks on 3 November, deleted its data there, paused training runs, disabled its Insights feature, and told affected customers to "treat this data as if it were exposed" and rotate any credentials pasted into chats.

The implication is that most AI vendor reviews stop one layer too early. Customers approved Notion; few knew their prompts flowed to an inference subprocessor, which itself depended on another cloud provider. Boards asking about AI risk should ask for the full subprocessor chain behind every AI feature that touches company data, and for which of those features employees are pasting secrets into. If the board's own governance score is a 4.7, the more useful first step is assigning one committee clear ownership of AI oversight before the next incident assigns it for them.

#Leadership & org design

Cisco's president and chief product officer ends meetings when people use internal acronyms, and the reasoning is a useful lens on onboarding cost. Jeetu Patel told Fortune on 11 November that with attrition of roughly 5 to 10 percent a year, about a third of Cisco's workforce has joined in the last three years, and that newer staff "feel too stupid to ask" what internal jargon means, so "a third of your company does not even understand what you're talking about." The rule sits alongside other operating norms leaders use to control meeting quality, such as Amazon's silent memo reading and United's cap on daily meeting time.

The broader point is that internal shorthand is a hidden tax that grows with every hire, reorg and acquisition. It slows ramp-up, suppresses questions from exactly the people with the freshest outside view, and concentrates real understanding in long-tenured staff, which is a quieter version of the key-person risk dominating this week's headlines. A practical test: count how many acronyms appear in your last leadership offsite deck or quarterly business review, and ask a recent hire to define them.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If our primary productivity cloud and our most important AI vendor both went dark for a day, what would we lose, who would know first, and which of our suppliers' suppliers would we only discover in the incident report?

#Try this now

  • Claim your brand in ChatGPT's Business Hub: OpenAI opened Businesses in ChatGPT in beta on 10 November. A verified company gets a blue check, can correct product details, prices and stock, and sees aggregated analytics: how often ChatGPT mentions the brand, click-through to the site, and the common topics in those conversations. Eligible US e-commerce and online-service firms can also launch a labelled Business Agent that answers product questions. Even outside the agent pilot, the topic report is a free, unprompted voice-of-customer feed from a service OpenAI says reaches 1.2 billion people a week. Claim the profile, fix what is wrong, then put the topics list next to your own site search terms and see where they disagree.
  • Run a 30-minute impostor audit across answer engines: Scrunch research released 12 November found that 17 percent of Fortune 500 companies had an impostor website cited in AI answers across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Google AI Overviews, AI Mode and Copilot, and in three of four of those answers the AI presented the impostor as the official source. The test needs no tool: ask each engine for your customer service number, your refund or change policy, and your official app or booking site, then log every cited domain. Any domain you do not own is a fraud and complaint risk, and it goes to the security and legal teams the same day. Scrunch also launched Citation Optimization, which ranks the third-party sources shaping AI answers, for teams that want this monitored instead of spot-checked.
  • Pipe planning history to the agent instead of uploading it: Basis expanded Compass, its brief-to-media-plan agent, with an MCP server plus historical performance intelligence, third-party research and DSP activation, and claims it gets advertisers from brief to plan 50 percent faster. The transferable technique: connect the planning assistant to the warehouse of past campaign results through a connector, so every recommendation cites what actually ran, rather than pasting last year's deck into a chat. Ask any planning vendor whether its agent reads your history live or works from what you upload.

#Case study worth stealing

Delta, letting customer-authorized agents through while owning its own concierge: As United moved to block third-party agents this week, Delta permits authenticated agent bookings and runs its own in-app Delta Concierge, with American and JetBlue also leaving the choice to the customer. The approach separates two questions most digital teams blur: who is allowed to transact (any agent the customer has authenticated) and who owns the best experience (the brand's own assistant, with the loyalty context only it holds). That keeps the brand visible in agent channels without handing over the account relationship. Any loyalty, subscription or banking product can copy the split: an authenticated door for outside agents, a better-informed agent of your own inside.

#Framework or mental model

One coordinator beats a team of advocates: Anthropic's multi-user, multi-agent study tested frontier models across 77 scenarios where each agent acts for a different person over a shared resource: a compute budget, a clinic calendar, a group booking, a release cutoff. Teams of per-person agents delivered worse group outcomes than a single coordinating agent in every environment, collapsed entirely in two environments without a shared channel, and failed through stalling as teams grew, overriding each other, and fabricating claims. The research environments are released as MAMUBench. Apply it before every function ships its own agent against shared budgets, inventory or calendars: name the coordinator, or at minimum the shared channel, first.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • HubSpot put its co-founder back in charge and called it a "refounding": On 12 November, Yamini Rangan stepped down as CEO and from the board, and co-founder Brian Halligan took over the same day (Rangan stays through 1 December). CFO Kate Bueker retires 11 February 2027 and Adobe's Karen Burgess joins 30 November to succeed her. Halligan's framing in the 8-K release: "Chapter three is building the customer relationship harness that will enable millions to grow better. This is a refounding." Q4 and full-year guidance was reaffirmed. The backdrop comes from the Q3 filing a week earlier. Revenue was $947.6 million, up 17.1%, down from 23.4% growth in Q1. Net customer additions fell to 5,258, the lowest in seven quarters and half the Q1 figure. The company is cutting nearly 660 roles (about 7%). Subscription cost of revenue rose 28% against 18% subscription revenue growth, driven partly by AI feature usage. The filing also says credit-based and outcome-based pricing "have lengthened sales cycles and resulted in lower initial contract values." Signal: the mid-market CRM and marketing suite is the first category where AI is visibly squeezing the economics from both sides. Usage-heavy AI features raise serving costs, and the new pricing models slow down the deal. A founder-led reset, announced three weeks after Salesforce agreed to buy Braze for $5.66 billion (20 October), says the suite vendors now see the AI layer as a rebuild, not a feature release. Anyone renewing a HubSpot contract in the next two quarters should expect packaging and pricing to move, and should lock terms for credits and agent usage before they do.
  • eBay is now enforcing its agent ban, starting with Meta's Muse: The clause has been in eBay's User Agreement since 20 January 2026 (effective 20 February). It bars "buy-for-me agents, LLM-driven bots, or any end-to-end flow that attempts to place orders without human review" unless eBay gives "prior express permission." What changed is enforcement. Meta's Muse agent lost access to eBay listings over the weekend of 7 to 8 November. The Verge reported on 10 November that eBay confirmed the block, and by 12 November access was "somewhat hit or miss" (PPC Land, 13 November). eBay has published no list of approved agents, no application process and no criteria. A spokesperson said only, "We take a selective approach to how they access and interact with our marketplace." United's contract-of-carriage rewrite banning agent bookings landed the same week. Signal: agent access is becoming a negotiated commercial right, not an open-web default. Marketplaces with scarce supply are making agents ask permission, and they will price that permission the way they price data feeds and affiliate deals. For any brand, the decision to make now is which agents get in, on what terms, and who in the organization owns the allowlist.
  • Google put invalid traffic numbers on the Google Ads dashboard: On 12 November Google began rolling out an Invalid Traffic overview card to all advertisers. It shows two numbers: estimated savings (invalid traffic filtered out before billing) and credited cost (refunds issued after billing closes). Google says its systems "analyze all traffic, including agentic activity" and that exposure to invalid leads fell 30% on average in 2026 (PPC Land, 13 November). The same day, Google Ads Editor 2.14 switched AI Max on by default for new Search campaigns. Signal: fraud reporting is turning into a product feature, and the platform selling the clicks is the one grading them. The card gives advertisers a number to cite. It does not give them anything to audit: no click-level detail, no independent check. As automated campaign types become the default, the independent check is what still has to be built.

#Category watch

Signal integrity in programmatic CTV: A CIMM study published 12 November found that when required signals are missing from a CTV bid request, 62% of programmatic buyers send the spend elsewhere. Signals here mean the information about the ad slot sent with the request, such as the app, the show and who is authorized to sell it. Only 8% say their DSP always acts on the signals they require after launch. Buyers are 88% likely to bid on detailed signals through verified supply paths and 36% likely through unverified ones. CIMM proposes three fixes: a voluntary minimum trading baseline covering inventory identity and selling authority, comparable platform capability statements with reconciliation reports, and an industry pilot. The survey covered 50 buyers, plus 28 executive interviews and bid logs from FreeWheel and OpenX. Separately, Channel Factory wired xpln.ai's attention metric into its video placements on 10 November, pitched as moving "beyond basic viewability," though it disclosed no results. The pattern is that CTV is repeating open-web display's history with a shorter timeline: first transparency about where the ad ran, then quality metrics beyond "was it served." For a martech org, that means CTV deals should be written around reconciliation (what signals were sent, received and used), and attention metrics should be treated as an optional extra until a vendor publishes results.

#Stack intelligence

The EU's proposed Digital Fairness Act turns two fields most stacks never stored into compliance requirements. The European Commission adopted the 72-page regulation on 12 November to replace the 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (PPC Land). Article 26 requires AI shopping agents to disclose paid suggestions. Article 12 extends the Digital Services Act's bans on profiling minors and profiling based on sensitive data from large platforms to all traders and ad tech companies. Fines go up to 6% of worldwide turnover. Ten types of dark patterns are banned outright, and subscriptions must have renewal reminders and an online cancel button. Parliament and Council still have to approve it, and it applies 12 months after it enters into force, so this is a design horizon, not an emergency. The architecture lesson is the same one the IAB attribution blueprint pointed at this week, now with a legal driver. Every product or offer that flows into an agent or a recommendation surface needs a machine-readable paid or organic flag that survives the trip. Every audience pipeline needs an age-assurance field and a sensitive-category exclusion applied before segments leave the CDP, not inside each activation tool. Retrofitting those flags across a dozen downstream platforms costs far more than adding them once at the source of record.

#Foundations concept of the day

Invalid traffic (IVT), general vs sophisticated: Invalid traffic is any ad impression or click that does not come from a real person with genuine interest. That includes bots, data-center traffic, accidental double clicks, and fraud schemes that fake ad views. The industry standard, set by the Media Rating Council (MRC, the US body that accredits ad measurement), splits it into two tiers. General IVT (GIVT) is the easy kind: known data-center addresses, declared crawlers like search engine spiders, and traffic that matches published bot lists. It can be filtered with lists and simple rules. Sophisticated IVT (SIVT) is the hard kind: hijacked consumer devices, bots built to mimic human scrolling and clicking, ads stacked invisibly behind each other, and domain spoofing, where a low-quality site passes itself off as a premium publisher in the bid request. Catching SIVT takes behavioral analysis, cross-campaign pattern detection and often a specialist vendor. Detection happens at two points. Pre-bid filtering blocks suspect impressions before money is spent. Post-bid analysis finds fraud after delivery and triggers credits, which is what Google's new dashboard card reports. Why it matters at scale: IVT does more than waste budget. It corrupts every model trained on the data, because bots that "convert" teach bidding algorithms and lookalike audiences to find more bots. Agentic traffic adds a new problem, since a shopping agent acting for a real customer is neither a fraudulent bot nor a human click, and current definitions were not written for it. Example: a consumer lender running lead-gen campaigns sees cost per application fall 20% in a quarter and celebrates, until the underwriting team notices that the new applications fail identity checks at triple the normal rate. The cheap leads were form-filling bots. The fix is to feed downstream quality outcomes (funded loans, verified accounts, completed bookings) back into bidding as the conversion signal, so the algorithm optimizes toward traffic that survives verification instead of traffic that merely fills in a form.

#Watch list

  • Credit-based and outcome-based pricing as a sales-cycle tax: HubSpot's Q3 filing is the first major vendor disclosure saying the new AI pricing models lengthen sales cycles and shrink initial contract values. If other suite vendors report the same, expect a swing back toward simpler seat-plus-allowance bundles in 2027, and expect procurement teams that model credit burn properly to get better terms than those that do not.
  • Automation switched on by default: AI Max became the default for new Search campaigns in Google Ads Editor 2.14 on 12 November, the same day Google started showing IVT savings on the dashboard. Defaults decide adoption more than features do. Watch how many accounts opt out, and whether independent measurement vendors start publishing invalid traffic rates for AI Max and Performance Max separately from classic campaigns.

#Macro pulse

The Nvidia shock turned out smaller than Thursday's open suggested. US indexes closed down only 0.4 percent after Nvidia recovered from a 14 percent opening drop, and Wall Street is heading for its first winning week since early October. The real pressure sits in rates and oil: the 10-year Treasury pushed further above 5 percent, Brent topped $95, and wholesale inflation hit a four-year high this morning.

Canada: The TSX fell 274.53 points, or 0.75 percent, to 36,137.19 on Thursday, giving back more than Wednesday's gain. Materials led the decline, down 3.2 percent, as gold fell 2.8 percent to its lowest level since October 2025. Financials slipped 0.5 percent, while energy and technology each rose 0.8 percent. Celestica rose 4.8 percent on speculation that rivals could take share from Nvidia, and Cargojet jumped 10.3 percent after beating third-quarter estimates. The Canada 10-year yield rose 7.7 basis points to 3.794 percent. USD/CAD held near C$1.4240 to C$1.4250 Friday morning, roughly where it sat Thursday, with the US dollar at its strongest against the loonie since April 2025 and the 2026 high around C$1.429 to C$1.430 close by. Fed hike pricing, a second straight Canadian jobs miss and a widening rate gap are doing the damage, while firmer crude is the only thing slowing the slide. Canadian carriers are still paying more in Canadian dollars for every US-dollar fuel bill and lease payment.

United States: Thursday close: S&P 500 down 26.98 points, or 0.4 percent, to 7,592.40. Dow down 189.40 points, or 0.4 percent, to 52,297.09. Nasdaq Composite down 100.10 points, or 0.4 percent, to 25,366.09. Energy rose for a fourth straight session and was the best-performing sector. Broadcom (up 5.5 percent), AMD (up 5.9 percent), Alphabet and Amazon offset much of the Nvidia drag. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 5.12 percent from 4.98 percent as higher oil fed inflation worries. This morning October PPI rose 0.3 percent on the month, a bit below the 0.4 percent forecast, with core also up 0.3 percent, in line. The annual rate climbed to 6.5 percent, the highest since November 2022. Futures added to gains after the release. Markets fully price a quarter-point Fed hike at the December 8 to 9 meeting, with slight odds of a half point. Early Friday trade: S&P 500 down 0.1 percent (on track for a weekly gain of 2.8 percent), Dow up 125 points, or 0.2 percent, Nasdaq down 0.4 percent. A Microsoft Exchange Online outage had Microsoft down 1.4 percent premarket.

Asia (13 November close): Mixed. Nikkei 225 up 0.5 percent to 61,142.35, helped by chip equipment (Tokyo Electron up 1.7 percent) and demand for the upcoming Anthropic IPO, though SoftBank Group fell 5.1 percent and Kioxia 2.3 percent. Hang Seng down 1.6 percent to 23,629.58, a second straight heavy loss. Shanghai Composite down 0.6 percent to 3,634.35.

Oil: Thursday was the big move. Brent jumped 3.3 percent to $95.82 and WTI rose about 2.6 percent to $88.74 after the statement that the US no longer seeks a deal with Iran ("we've got their Strait!"). Friday morning was calmer: Brent up 0.2 percent at $96.04, WTI down 0.4 percent at $88.39. Attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure add to the risk premium. Most of November's jet fuel relief is now at risk.

#Today's industry move

OpenAI acquired Odessia, an AI travel agent, founded in late 2025 by former Sonder CEO Francis Davidson, who joins OpenAI to lead travel. Terms were not disclosed. Odessia is a full-service agent built on GPT 5.5 and Codex that plans trips and books directly with suppliers rather than redirecting to them, and it handled about $60 million in travel volume in its first year. Expedia fell about 4 percent, Tripadvisor more than 3 percent and Booking more than 2 percent on the news. A frontier model lab now owns a booking agent, so airlines face an AI layer that can sit between them and the traveller and handle discovery, choice and payment. Direct-channel strategies now have to work for agents as well as for people: fares, ancillaries and loyalty earn need to be machine-readable and bookable through an API, or the agent will book the airline that is.

Genuine update on the Anthropic IPO: Bloomberg reports the deal is about six times oversubscribed at the $70 to $78 range, with pricing expected November 18 and Nasdaq trading under ANT from November 19.

#AI infrastructure watch

Both Thursday after-close reports say chip and network capex is holding through the Nvidia transition.

  • Applied Materials beat with Q4 revenue of $10.41 billion, up 53 percent year over year, and adjusted EPS of $4.16 against $4.06 expected. Semiconductor Systems revenue grew about 64 percent and gross margin improved to 50.6 percent. Q1 guidance of $11.1 billion plus or minus $500 million in revenue and $4.35 plus or minus $0.20 in EPS came in above consensus at the midpoint, although the wide range leaves room to miss at the low end. Shares moved only slightly after hours, between about 0.3 percent lower and 1.5 percent higher.
  • Cisco posted record revenue of $18.5 billion, up 24 percent, with adjusted EPS of $1.40 against about $1.33 expected. Networking revenue rose 43 percent and product orders 21 percent. It raised its fiscal 2027 hyperscaler AI infrastructure forecast to $9 billion from $7.5 billion, more than double fiscal 2026, and lifted full-year revenue guidance to $74.8 to $75.8 billion. The stock was flat after hours near $102 because adjusted gross margin slipped to 65.6 percent on hardware mix and elevated memory costs. Memory has become the cost item to watch across the stack.
  • Nvidia follow-up: the stock closed Thursday down 4.34 percent at $166.19 after opening down about 14 percent, and retail investors bought about $111 million in the first 30 minutes, the largest opening buy on record for the stock. Shares were up more than 2 percent premarket Friday. The board named former Synopsys CEO Harvey C. Jones its first chair and will consider internal and external CEO candidates. Colette Kress stays CFO while serving as interim CEO. The pending $12.9 billion Hugging Face acquisition is still scheduled to close in the first half of 2027.

#Signal or noise?

Signal, but a different one than Thursday morning suggested: the market treated the Nvidia exit as a governance event rather than a demand event, because Applied and Cisco both confirmed AI capex is accelerating. The trend that matters for travel is the 5 percent-plus Treasury, 6.5 percent wholesale inflation and $96 Brent, which together squeeze both airline costs and consumer discretionary budgets.
