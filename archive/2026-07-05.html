Executive Pulse576 words

#Macro signal

Tariff-driven inflation is structural, not transitory, and the Fed is done pretending otherwise: New Fed research confirms tariffs implemented through November 2025 have raised core goods PCE prices by 3.1 percent through February 2026, accounting for the entirety of excess inflation in that category. New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish hold at his first meeting, with a statement notably stripped of forward guidance. Implication: plan for a higher-for-longer rate environment and sustained input-cost pressure. Pricing strategy and vendor contracts should assume tariff volatility persists past the fall trade-truce expiration, not fade quietly.

#Tech industry

  • AI capex vs. headcount divergence is now the default operating model, not an anomaly: Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon are on track to spend nearly $700 billion combined in 2026 on AI infrastructure while simultaneously cutting jobs, Cisco cut ~4,000 roles despite beating earnings, Cloudflare cut 20% of staff after 34% revenue growth. Strategic implication: boards are no longer waiting for AI ROI to prove itself before restructuring around it. The workforce plan and the capex plan are now the same decision, and separating them in your own planning cycle is a lag indicator.
  • OpenAI is offering the US government a 5% equity stake ahead of a targeted $1T+ IPO, structured like a sovereign-wealth arrangement (modeled on the Alaska Permanent Fund) to defuse political pressure. Whether or not this specific structure survives, it signals a new template: frontier AI labs pre-negotiating government alignment before going public, rather than after. Any leader relying on AI vendors should watch for similar equity-for-access arrangements reshaping vendor risk and neutrality.
  • China is widening trade leverage beyond rare earths to other choke-point goods, with the one-year rare-earths truce expiring this fall. About 4% of US GDP ($1.2T) sits in industries exposed to rare-earth supply. Diversification efforts are underway but not close to sufficient. Treat Q4 supply chain and input-cost planning as a live geopolitical risk, not background noise.

#AI at the leadership level

The layoff narrative is bifurcating in a way worth naming explicitly: 56% of 2026 layoff events now cite AI or automation, but the companies doing the cutting are frequently the same ones increasing AI spend and reporting strong earnings, prompting even OpenAI's own CEO to call some of it "AI washing." The strategic risk isn't that AI is destroying jobs; it's that "AI" has become an acceptable cover story for restructuring decisions leaders would otherwise have to justify on their own merits. Boards and leadership teams should be pressure-testing their own AI-attributed cuts: is this genuine automation-driven efficiency, or convenient narrative cover for cost discipline that would look bad stated plainly?

#Leadership & org design

Meta's May reorganization, moving roughly 7,000 employees into new AI-native units (Applied AI Engineering, an "Agent Transformation Accelerator") alongside an 8,000-person layoff, is now generating visible internal dissent, including a livestream disrupted by an employee criticizing AI leadership. The lesson for any org mid-restructure: speed of AI reorganization is outpacing the internal communication and trust-building needed to bring people along, and that gap becomes a public liability, not just an HR problem. Pair any AI-driven structural change with an explicit narrative for the people staying, not just a plan for the roles being cut.

#Question worth asking your team this week

When we say a decision was "AI-driven", a layoff, a restructuring, a budget shift, would that framing survive being said plainly, without the AI label attached?

#Try this now

  • Figma Code Layers (early access opened this week): Clone a GitHub repo directly onto the Figma canvas, convert any design layer into an interactive, code-backed layer with one click or prompt, then push changes back to the repo, all inside the same multiplayer file where PMs and designers already comment. Sign up for the waitlist beta at figma.com/config-betas. Worth 20 minutes because it collapses the design-to-dev handoff into one file: no more "is this the latest version" threads between design and engineering.
  • Context engineering over prompt engineering: Gartner's framing has gone mainstream, instead of hand-tuning prompts, teams are building repeatable systems that decide what information and tools an AI sees before it answers (docs, past decisions, style guides, live data). Practical starting point: audit one recurring AI workflow on the team (support replies, campaign briefs, PRD drafts) and write down exactly what context a human currently supplies from memory, that list is your first context package.

#Case study worth stealing

Lightspeed Commerce, AI agent handles nearly all first-line support: The e-commerce and fintech platform deployed Intercom's Fin AI agent across support channels; it now participates in 99% of conversations and autonomously resolves up to 72% of them end-to-end, with no incremental headcount. The transferable pattern isn't the vendor, it's the sequencing: they let the agent sit in on every conversation first (visibility before automation), then expanded resolution authority as accuracy proved out. Any digital team piloting AI support or triage should copy that staged rollout rather than flipping full automation on day one.

#Framework or mental model

Context engineering (vs. prompt engineering): The shift is from crafting a clever one-off instruction to designing the ongoing system that feeds an AI model the right information, tools, and format at the right time, treating context like a product with an owner, not a prompt like a script. Digital leaders running AI across product, design, and marketing should assign explicit ownership of "context sources" (brand voice docs, decision logs, customer data feeds) the same way they'd own a data pipeline.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Fox Corporation / Roku: Fox agreed to acquire Roku for $22B in cash and stock ($160/share), funded partly with $8B in new debt. Combines Fox's content (Tubi, NFL, MLB, Fox News) with Roku's CTV platform (100M+ households), creating the third-largest US TV player by viewing share, with ~$9B combined ad revenue and $400M targeted synergies. Roku stays an open platform, Netflix, Prime Video remain available. Expected to close H1 2027. Signal: content owners are buying distribution and ad infrastructure outright rather than renting it. CTV is consolidating into the same content-plus-pipes model that defined cable, but with programmatic buying built in from day one.
  • Publicis Groupe / LiveRamp: Publicis is acquiring LiveRamp for $2.2B ($38.50/share, ~$2.55B equity value), expected to close by year-end 2026. LiveRamp connects 25,000+ publisher domains and 500+ data/tech partners across 14 markets. Publicis frames this as building "data co-creation" infrastructure for agentic AI workflows. Signal: an agency holding company now owns a neutral identity/data-collaboration layer that its competitors also plug into, a structural conflict-of-interest question for any brand whose stack depends on LiveRamp for cross-partner clean-room work.
  • Zeta Global / Palantir: Seven-year strategic partnership rearchitecting Zeta's Data Cloud on Palantir Foundry, positioning Zeta's Athena engine as an "operating system for agentic marketing" for Palantir's commercial (non-government) client base. Zeta projects $100M+ in incremental annual revenue from the deal. Signal: martech vendors are outsourcing the AI-infrastructure layer to specialized players rather than building it in-house, expect more CDP/engagement platforms pairing with foundation-model or data-infra companies instead of shipping their own.

#Category watch

Ad tech M&A consolidation: Deal activity in martech/adtech is running ~13% above 2024 levels, concentrated in identity infrastructure, retail media management, and connected TV, the three categories where AI capability gaps are most expensive to build organically. The pattern across Fox-Roku, Publicis-LiveRamp, and smaller deals (Viant/TVision, Accenture Song/Whalar) is consistent: buyers are acquiring data rights and distribution, not just tooling. For anyone building a stack, this means longer-term vendor lock-in risk is shifting from "which platform" to "which holding company or hyperscaler ends up owning your identity graph."

#Stack intelligence

Consent infrastructure just became a hard ad-serving dependency, not a compliance checkbox. Google now requires a certified CMP to run ads in the EU/UK, and TCF v2.2 consent strings generated after February 28, 2026 are invalid, bid requests with invalid strings get rejected outright, which is a direct revenue cut, not a fine risk. Any org still treating consent management as a legal/privacy-owned line item rather than a piece of ad-serving infrastructure with uptime and validity SLAs is exposed. Build reviews of CMP integration into the same operational cadence as DSP or ad-server monitoring.

#Foundations concept of the day

Data clean rooms: A clean room is a secure environment where two or more parties (a brand and a retailer, or two brands with overlapping customers) can match and analyze their data together without either side seeing the other's raw records, only aggregated, privacy-safe outputs come out. Retailers use clean rooms to let CPG brands measure ad performance against actual purchase data without handing over their entire customer database. The mechanism matters more than the buzzword: identity resolution links records across datasets (usually via hashed emails or device IDs), then queries run inside the room and only pre-approved aggregate results leave it. This is the structural answer to a cookieless, privacy-regulated world, it's why LiveRamp, built substantially on clean-room interoperability, was worth $2.2B to an agency holding company. Cross-industry example: a travel brand and a card network can clean-room-match to see which cardholders who saw a campaign later booked, without the airline ever seeing raw card data or the bank ever seeing raw booking data.

#Watch list

  • LiveRamp under Publicis: whether competing agencies and brands continue trusting a Publicis-owned neutral data layer for cross-partner collaboration, or route around it.
  • Consent Mode v2 enforcement fallout: watch bid-volume and CPM data from EU/UK markets over the next few weeks as invalid TCF strings start getting rejected at scale.

#Macro pulse

Markets are closed for the weekend, last session was Friday, July 3 (a shortened pre-holiday day). Canada: the TSX Composite gained ~0.9% to 35,274, approaching record territory on strength in gold miners and financials. USA: markets closed early Friday ahead of the July 4th holiday; Thursday's session left the Dow at a record high, the S&P roughly flat, and the Nasdaq soft on continued semiconductor weakness. Asia had no notable moves to flag heading into the weekend. FX: USD/CAD sits around 1.4189, holding near the top of its recent 1.41-1.42 range as the greenback stays firm against the loonie. Oil: WTI closed near $68.76, little changed, as easing U.S.-Iran tension continues to unwind the geopolitical risk premium.

#Today's industry move

No fresh MarTech/AdTech or funding news over the holiday weekend. The one operational story worth tracking: a historic 2026 pilot shortage is compounding delays and cancellations across Delta, United, American, Southwest, Air Canada, JetBlue, and Lufthansa at major hubs (DFW, ATL, ORD, IAD, YYZ, FRA), pushing fares higher into peak summer travel. For any airline digital leader, this is a capacity-constraint story that shifts near-term priority toward demand-shaping and dynamic pricing tools rather than acquisition-driven growth.

#AI infrastructure watch

NVIDIA and SK hynix announced a multiyear partnership to co-develop next-generation memory for AI data centers, applying NVIDIA's CUDA-X libraries to chip design and using Omniverse-based digital twins in SK hynix fabs. Broader chip rally continues: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index is up over 47% year-to-date, though Thursday's session showed a pullback in AI-linked names, a reminder the rally isn't in a straight line.

#Signal or noise?

The pilot shortage is signal, not noise, it's a structural constraint that will keep pressuring fares and reliability through peak season regardless of what markets do Monday.

#Watch tomorrow

Markets reopen Monday: watch whether Thursday's semiconductor pullback extends into a third session, and whether the NVIDIA/SK hynix news gives chip stocks a fresh catalyst to reverse it.
