Executive Pulse1,185 words

#Macro signal

A September hike is now a coin flip, and the market repriced rates without repricing growth: Fed chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole keynote to refuse forward guidance while saying the bank still has work to do on inflation, and specifically that better than expected summer PCE and CPI prints do not tell him underlying trends have meaningfully improved. Futures moved to roughly 57 percent odds of a hike at the September 15 to 16 meeting, up from about 35 percent beforehand. The dollar strengthened about 0.5 percent and gold fell 3.2 percent to near $4,456. Equities barely moved.

That divergence is the signal worth reading. Investors treated the speech as a change in the timing of policy, not in the trajectory of the economy. For leaders that distinction matters more than the hike itself, because it means the cost of capital assumption in every 2027 plan is now unstable while the demand assumption is not. Elevated energy prices tied to continued supply chain disruption are doing much of the inflation work, which is the kind of inflation rate policy addresses slowly and expensively.

The implication for technology and product leaders is concrete and near-term. Multi-year capital commitments underwritten in a falling-rate world are being refinanced in a possibly-rising-rate one. Anything on your roadmap whose business case assumed cheaper money in 2027, large platform migrations, leveraged vendor deals, long-payback infrastructure, should be re-run at a higher discount rate before the September meeting rather than after it.

#Tech industry

  • Meta is building its flagship consumer agent on a competitor's model: Project Hatch, an agent platform reportedly weeks from launch inside Instagram at up to $199.99 per month for the premium tier, is reported to run on Anthropic's Claude during development rather than Meta's own models. Strategic implication: the company with the strongest public commitment to proprietary models just valued time to market above model ownership on its most visible consumer bet. Read that as a live datapoint in every build versus buy debate happening in your own org right now. The frontier model is being treated as a procurable input by a firm that spent years arguing the opposite, which should raise the bar on any internal proposal to train rather than buy.
  • Music publishers open a provenance front against Anthropic: Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell filed August 28, seeking up to $150,000 per infringed work and, separately, $25,000 per instance of removing copyright management information. Strategic implication: the second claim is the one boards should track. It attacks how provenance was handled rather than whether training is fair use, and provenance is an engineering property that can be audited. If it survives, enterprise buyers acquire a new diligence obligation on any vendor whose model or retrieval index ingests third-party content, and the question moves from legal opinion to a contractual attestation your procurement team has to know how to ask for.

#AI at the leadership level

The gap between what executives report and what they expect is now measured, and it is wide enough to be a governance problem.

The NBER survey of nearly 6,000 CEOs, CFOs, and senior executives across the US, UK, Germany, and Australia found that more than 90 percent report no effect of AI on employment over the past three years, and 89 percent report no effect on labor productivity. Adoption is not the explanation. Sixty-nine percent of businesses report current AI use, 78 percent in the US. Two thirds of senior executives personally use it in a typical week, at an average intensity of about 1.5 hours. Looking forward, the same executives forecast AI raising labor productivity 1.4 percent and reducing employment 0.7 percent over the next three years.

Three years of adoption with no measurable effect, and continued confidence in effects arriving shortly. Economists are reaching for the Solow paradox to explain it, and the historical analogy is genuinely reassuring: measured productivity gains from general purpose technologies lag deployment by years because the complementary reorganization takes years. But the analogy is also a warning. In the computing case, the firms that captured the gains were the ones that redesigned processes around the technology. The ones that layered it onto existing workflows captured almost nothing and kept paying for it.

The board-level question this raises is not whether to keep investing. It is whether your organization can distinguish the two cases in its own portfolio. Most AI programs today are instrumented for adoption, seats provisioned and queries run, and not for the reorganization that the historical record says is the actual causal mechanism. An org reporting 78 percent adoption and 1.5 hours a week of executive use is describing a tool that has been distributed, not a process that has been redesigned.

#Leadership & org design

A useful counterexample landed this week in the form of an operating detail rather than a strategy claim. Anthropic published how it runs internal self-service analytics through an agent in Slack, and the sequencing it recommends is: permissions before any agent code is written, distribution second, telemetry from day one, and the actual analytical capabilities last, built only once real questions show which are needed. In one data channel, the agent answered more than 75 percent of posted questions, usually within a minute or two.

Two things in that are transferable regardless of tooling. First, the order treats governance as the foundation rather than the compliance review at the end, which is the opposite of how nearly every AI program is currently staffed. Second, and more usefully for org design, the named failure mode is drift between the agent's instructions and a changed data model, and the mitigation is to treat those instructions as served content refreshed continuously rather than a deliverable shipped once. That converts an AI deployment from a project with a completion date into an operation with a named owner and a running cost.

Most organizations have not budgeted for the second thing. They funded the build. If nobody owns the refresh, the deployment has a decay clock, and the way it fails is not an outage. It is confidently wrong answers delivered at machine speed into channels where nothing surrounds them to signal that they are wrong.

#Question worth asking your team this week

We report adoption numbers on our AI program. Name one business process we have actually redesigned around it, and one we deliberately chose not to. If the honest answer to the first is that we have distributed tools into existing workflows, we should expect the same measured productivity effect the last 6,000 executives reported, which was none.

#Try this now

  • Anthropic's own Claude Tag analytics deployment, published as a how-to: Anthropic put out the internal playbook for how it runs self-service data analytics inside Slack, and it is the most useful agent-deployment document to land this week because it is a postmortem, not a pitch. The core move is treating the agent's service account as a shared read replica of the governed warehouse. Five controls do the work: the account reads only semantic layer output tables and curated marts, never raw event streams or personal sandboxes; a data catalog flags likely PII candidates that humans then classify, and warehouse access controls make those columns invisible to the agent; skills document the authentication paths so the agent can diagnose its own permission failures instead of guessing; channel membership is the access grant, so adding the agent to a channel means granting that channel's members read access; and every query carries labels identifying surface, conversation, and requesting user. In one data channel over a month, the agent answered more than 75 percent of posted questions, usually within a minute or two, often without being called. Worth 20 minutes because the sequencing is the transferable part, and most teams have it backwards.
  • Run the invisible-HTML injection test against your own email summarizer: Forcepoint X-Labs planted instructions in an email using HTML styling that renders invisible in Outlook but passes through intact to the model. It worked in all ten runs. The generated summary moved an invoice deadline from August 21 to September 3, dropped a named individual from the text, and restated an outstanding balance of 8,750 euros as 46,200 euros. Nothing was exploited in the model or the summarizer product. The failure is architectural: untrusted email body content was handed to an LLM without separation. The test takes an afternoon to replicate and the fixes are specific. Extract only user-visible content, detect hidden or suspicious CSS, split headers from body, and validate summaries against source. Any org running AI summarization over an inbox, a ticket queue, or a supplier portal has this surface open right now.
  • Auxia Agent Studio, early access: A control plane for marketing teams that pairs a marketing context graph, holding brand voice and prior campaign performance, with agents that plan, build, and optimize campaigns across the tools already in the stack rather than replacing them. The piece worth studying is not the campaign automation, it is the approval routing. The workspace collects feedback, reconciles conflicting requests, summarizes proposed changes, and routes approvals across legal, brand, creative, analytics, and operations. That is the actual bottleneck in enterprise marketing, and it is the first agentic marketing product to treat the approval graph as the product surface instead of an afterthought.

#Case study worth stealing

The Washington Post, agents over internal analytics: The Post assembled a network of agents on OpenAI's APIs that query multiple internal datasets spanning content, subscription, and advertising operations, follow permission logic across them, and return answers on short timeframes. The point is not that a newsroom is using AI. It is the shape of the problem they picked. They targeted recurring cross-dataset questions, the ones an analyst answers for the eleventh time by joining the same three sources, and compressed those into an on-demand assistant. Analysts moved from gathering data to interpreting it. Any org with an analytics team fielding a queue of repeat questions can adapt this directly, and the qualifying test is simple: if a question gets asked more than once a week and its answer requires joining known sources under known permissions, it belongs to an agent. Novel questions stay with humans. The permission logic is what makes it deployable, not the model.

#Framework or mental model

Permissions first, analytics skills last: The implementation order Anthropic recommends inverts how most teams sequence an agent build. Permissions before any agent code is written. Distribution second, verifying skill freshness end to end. Telemetry from day one, logging structured events immediately rather than adding observability after launch. Knowledge index once the thing is stable. Analytics skills last, letting real questions determine which forecasting, cohort, or funnel capabilities are actually needed. Most teams run this exactly backwards, starting with an impressive skill and retrofitting governance, which produces a demo that cannot pass review.

The companion idea is sharper. Treat skill files as served content, refreshed continuously, not as artifacts shipped once. The most damaging failure mode named is skill drift, where skill files fall out of sync with a changed data model. It is damaging specifically because users see isolated numbers without the surrounding context a dashboard provides, so a confidently wrong answer has nothing around it to contradict it. Digital leaders should apply this at contract and staffing time: an agent deployment is not a project with a launch date, it is a content operation with an owner and a refresh cadence, and if nobody is funded to keep the skills current, the deployment has a decay clock on it.

#Rabbit hole

Indirect prompt injection moved from demonstration to catalog this month. Forcepoint X-Labs published a set of ten distinct in-the-wild payloads, and the Cloud Security Alliance has a companion research note on operational indirect prompt injection. Read together they are an hour well spent for anyone whose roadmap includes agents touching external content, because the pattern across all of them is the same: the model is fine, the boundary between trusted instruction and untrusted data is where the product was never designed.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Meta, Project Hatch: Reporting this week points to a consumer agent platform launching within weeks, possibly early September, running inside Instagram and its roughly 2 billion daily users, with a premium tier under consideration at up to $199.99 per month. Hatch takes a goal, selects steps, and operates connected services end to end, with trained access to DoorDash, Etsy, Reddit, Yelp, and Outlook. The detail that matters most is that the agent reportedly runs on Anthropic's Claude during development rather than Meta's own models. Signal: two things at once. A company that spent years arguing its models were a strategic asset is shipping its highest-visibility consumer agent on a competitor's inference, which prices model ownership below time to market. And the commerce layer changes shape. If an agent inside Instagram completes a DoorDash order or a Yelp booking, the merchant relationship gets mediated by an agent that no brand configured, no brand pays for placement in, and no brand can currently measure. Every consumer brand with a paid social line item should be asking what its presence looks like on that surface, because the answer today is that it does not have one.
  • Nvidia, Vera Rubin repositioning: The pitch has shifted from raw GPU throughput to integrated systems pairing Rubin GPUs with Vera CPUs, explicitly tuned for orchestration and latency across multi-step agent workloads rather than single large inference passes. Nvidia claims 1.8x faster task completion against x86 on agentic, reinforcement learning, and data processing work. AWS took hand delivery of its first Vera CPU server and Vera Rubin GPU in Seattle in late August. Announced adopters include Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, ByteDance, CoreWeave, and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure. Signal: the industry has conceded that agentic workloads are a different computational shape than generative ones. An agent run is many small calls with orchestration, memory access, and tool invocation between them, so the constraint is coordination overhead, not matrix multiplication. When the chip vendor rebuilds around the CPU side of the ratio, the cost curve for agentic marketing infrastructure stops tracking token prices and starts tracking steps per task. Anyone modeling agent economics on cost per token is measuring the wrong denominator.
  • Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell sue Anthropic: Filed August 28 against Anthropic and named executives, alleging training on tens of thousands of copyrighted compositions, obtained partly through torrenting millions of books and partly by scraping lyrics from licensed sites including MusixMatch and LyricFind in breach of their terms. The publishers seek up to $150,000 per infringed work plus $25,000 per instance of stripped copyright management information. Signal: the claim about removing copyright management information is the part with stack consequences. It targets provenance handling rather than the fair use question everyone has been litigating, and provenance is a data engineering property, not a legal posture. If stripped attribution becomes independently actionable, every enterprise pipeline that ingests third-party content into a model or a retrieval index inherits a discovery surface. Ask your vendors what their training and retrieval corpora retain about origin, and get the answer in the contract.

#Category watch

Agentic marketing platforms are converging on the approval graph: Auxia's Agent Studio, in early access, pairs a marketing context graph holding brand voice and prior campaign performance with agents that plan, build, and optimize across existing email platforms and CDPs. The differentiator is not campaign generation, which is now table stakes across the category. It is the collaborative workspace that collects feedback, reconciles conflicting requests, summarizes proposed changes, and routes approvals across legal, brand, creative, analytics, and operations. PubMatic went at the same problem from the buy side with AgenticOS for agent-to-agent advertising earlier this year. The pattern is that vendors have stopped competing on output quality and started competing on who owns the sign-off path, because in any regulated or brand-sensitive enterprise the constraint on campaign velocity was never creative production time. Whoever owns the approval graph owns the workflow, and unlike creative generation it does not commoditize when the next model ships.

#Stack intelligence

The buy versus build line moved again this week, and it moved in a direction that should change what teams are currently scoping.

Meta building its flagship consumer agent on someone else's model, and Nvidia rebuilding its platform around orchestration rather than inference, point at the same conclusion from opposite ends. The model layer is being treated as procurable by the companies with the most reason to treat it otherwise. What is not procurable is the layer underneath: the permission model, the semantic definitions the agent resolves against, and the audit trail.

This is where a specific and underpriced risk sits. When an agent answers a question from a governed semantic layer, the definition of the metric is enforced at query time and every consumer gets the same answer. When the semantic layer does not cover the question, the agent writes ad hoc SQL and picks whichever field looks most likely. Marketing's definition of an active customer and finance's definition are both in the warehouse, and nothing in the agent adjudicates between them. Two teams, two answers, and the confidence of the prose is identical in both cases.

The operational takeaway is a metric worth instrumenting on day one: what fraction of agent queries route through the governed layer versus ad hoc SQL, broken out by surface and domain. That single ratio is an early warning system. When it drops it means either the skill files have drifted from a changed data model or the semantic layer does not cover the questions people are actually asking. Both are fixable while the ratio is falling and expensive once trust in the numbers is gone.

#Foundations concept of the day

The semantic layer: A governed translation between how data is physically stored and how the business talks about it. The warehouse holds tables with columns like usr_last_activity_ts and subscription_state_cd. The business asks about active customers, churn risk, and lifetime value. The semantic layer is where those business terms get exactly one definition, expressed as certified logic over the physical tables, along with the lineage showing how it was derived and the access policies governing who may see it.

How it works: instead of every dashboard, spreadsheet, and analyst writing its own SQL to define an active customer, they all call a single governed definition that resolves at query time. Increasingly these definitions are served over an endpoint an agent can call, so the agent retrieves the certified definition before it generates any SQL at all. The physical schema can change underneath without every downstream consumer breaking, because the contract is the definition, not the column name.

Why it matters at scale: for two decades this was a nice-to-have that lost budget fights to whatever shipped a dashboard faster. The failure mode was tolerable because a human sat between the query and the decision, noticed when a number looked wrong, and asked around. Agents removed that human. An agent will resolve an ambiguous metric silently, confidently, and thousands of times an hour, and the person reading the answer in a chat channel sees an isolated number with no dashboard context to contradict it. The semantic layer went from a data governance preference to the thing that determines whether agent output is trustworthy at all.

Cross-industry example: a retailer running a media network sells advertisers on incrementality. Incrementality requires an agreed definition of a converting shopper, a holdout population, and an attribution window. If the media team, the merchandising team, and the finance team each resolve those terms differently, the retailer will report a lift number its advertisers cannot reconcile with their own measurement, and the network loses the account on trust rather than on performance. In an airline the same structure governs a qualifying segment or an active member, where marketing, loyalty accounting, and revenue management each have a legitimate reason to define them differently and only one of those definitions can be the one an agent uses when it decides who gets an offer.

#Watch list

  • Whose semantic layer wins the agent endpoint: Cube, Atlan, the warehouse vendors, and the BI incumbents are all positioning to be the governed definition an agent calls at query time. This is a small-sounding integration decision that determines which vendor sits between every agent and every number in the business.
  • Agent-mediated commerce inside social: If Hatch ships into Instagram at consumer scale, a new intermediated purchase path opens with no brand placement model, no measurement standard, and no pricing. The first credible answer to how brands appear there will define the category.
  • Provenance requirements moving into vendor contracts: Watch whether the copyright management information claim in the music publishers' suit prompts enterprise buyers to start requiring corpus provenance attestations in AI vendor agreements. That would be the mechanism by which a training dispute becomes an enterprise procurement standard.

#Macro pulse

Weekend session, no cash equity trading anywhere. The read that matters today is what Friday left on the table going into a heavy data week.

The Jackson Hole repricing held into the close and showed up in the cross-asset tape rather than in stocks. The dollar firmed about 0.5 percent and gold dropped 3.2 percent to near $4,456, which is the clean expression of a market that raised its expected path for policy rates. Futures sit near 57 percent odds of a September hike, up from roughly 35 percent before the Warsh keynote, though prediction market platforms are less convinced, with Kalshi around 48 percent for a 25 basis point move. That spread between futures and prediction markets is itself informative. Nobody has conviction on this meeting.

Canada: The loonie ended Friday near 71.98 cents US with USD/CAD around 1.390, a second straight session of Canadian dollar weakness into a hawkish US Fed. That configuration is the one that compounds for Canadian carriers. A Fed that hikes while the Bank of Canada holds widens the differential, and every US-denominated cost line, aircraft leases, fuel, hedges, cloud and technology contracts, gets more expensive against fare revenue that is substantially domestic. There is no Bank of Canada decision this week to change it.

United States: Friday closed with the S&P 500 down 0.25 percent, the Nasdaq off 0.52 percent, and the Dow essentially flat, all three still higher on the week. The important item is the calendar rather than the close. This week runs ISM Manufacturing and JOLTS on Tuesday, ADP and the Beige Book on Wednesday, ISM Services and jobless claims on Thursday, and the August employment situation report on Friday, September 4. That payrolls print is the last major labour reading before the September 15 to 16 FOMC, which makes it the highest-leverage single data point of the quarter for the rate path.

Asia: Nikkei, Hang Seng, and Shanghai all closed Friday before the Jackson Hole text landed, so Monday's Asian open is still the first unfiltered regional read on a repriced September.

Oil: Brent finished near $88.88 and WTI in the low $83s, both essentially flat on the day. Crude sustained at these levels through the autumn booking season remains the live margin question for unhedged carriers, and elevated energy prices are also a named contributor to the inflation picture the Fed is responding to. Those two exposures are now correlated for airlines, which is an uncomfortable position: the input cost and the cost of capital are being driven by the same thing.

#Today's industry move

Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell filed suit against Anthropic on August 28, naming the company and executives including its CEO and a co-founder, over training on what the complaint describes as tens of thousands of copyrighted compositions. The publishers seek up to $150,000 per infringed work plus $25,000 for each instance of removing copyright management information, and the complaint alleges acquisition through torrenting at least 5 million books from Library Genesis in June 2021 and roughly 2 million more from a successor site in July 2022, plus scraping of licensed lyric services in breach of their terms.

The market-relevant part is the copyright management information count. Fair use litigation has been priced by investors as a slow, uncertain, settlement-shaped risk. A claim about stripped provenance is different in kind, because it is about a discoverable engineering practice with statutory per-instance damages rather than a doctrinal question. That structure produces a number that scales with corpus size instead of with a judge's reasoning, which is exactly the kind of tail risk that gets underwritten into a private valuation rather than ignored.

#AI infrastructure watch

Nvidia's positioning shifted this week from GPU throughput to integrated systems, pairing Rubin GPUs with Vera CPUs and marketing the combination on orchestration and latency for multi-step agent workloads rather than on raw inference. The company claims 1.8x faster task completion versus x86 on agentic, reinforcement learning, and data processing work. AWS took hand delivery of its first Vera CPU server and Vera Rubin GPU in Seattle in late August, and named adopters now include Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, ByteDance, CoreWeave, and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure.

Underneath the silicon, the power constraint is still the one with the longest lead time. Texas has roughly 474 GW in pending interconnection requests against a governor's moratorium on new data center grid connections announced August 4, pending project-by-project review of power, water, and ratepayer impact, with ERCOT postponing its large-load interconnection study as a result. NRG's response is the template worth watching: a preliminary 1.2 GW combined-cycle gas agreement with an unnamed hyperscaler at roughly $3.2 billion, structured on a bring-your-own-power basis where the generator is paid for megawatts made available rather than consumed, on a 15-year minimum term with a parent guarantee, targeting late 2029. Across the sector, hyperscalers have now committed more than $16 billion to nuclear in 2026 and close to 10 GW across more than 13 agreements.

#Signal or noise?

Signal, and the through-line is that the AI buildout's two hardest inputs both got more expensive in the same week. Capital repriced hawkish on Friday, and the compute itself is being redesigned around agentic workloads that consume orchestration and power rather than pure inference. Note the delivery dates in the power deals: 2029, 2030. Contracts being signed today against demand that arrives at the end of the decade, financed at a rate path that just became uncertain. The application layer moves in quarters and the supply layer moves in half-decades, and this week widened the gap between them.

Asia's Monday open, the first regional session to price the Jackson Hole repricing after closing ahead of it on Friday. Watch USD/CAD through 1.39 for any follow-through, and note that Monday is otherwise empty on the US data calendar, so the week's real test starts Tuesday with ISM Manufacturing and JOLTS and does not resolve until Friday's payrolls.
