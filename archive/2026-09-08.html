Executive Pulse1,209 words

#Macro signal

Two cost shocks landed on the same consumer today, and they are unrelated: Canada's retaliatory tariffs took effect this morning on more than 700 American products, at 15, 25, or 50 percent depending on the category, matching dollar for dollar the 50 percent US tariff that hit roughly C$27.6 billion of Canadian exports on 22 August after trade talks collapsed. The targeted sectors are steel, dairy, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. Economist Trevor Tombe estimates roughly C$4 billion in added economy-wide cost, average consumer prices up about 0.25 percent, and households earning under $30,000 losing more than half a percent of disposable income. Separately and simultaneously, oil rose for a third straight day to six-week highs, WTI at $92.56 and Brent at $97.20, after the US struck three Iranian oil tankers over the weekend in response to Iranian ballistic missiles fired at two US Navy warships. Goldman Sachs raised its December 2026 forecasts by $5 to $85 Brent and $80 WTI, and lifted 2027 as well, which is an analyst saying out loud that the conflict is now in the base case rather than the risk case.

Why this matters strategically: these are two independent shocks with the same destination. One raises the landed cost of goods, the other raises the cost of moving anything and of heating anything, and both compress the same discretionary income. The planning error to avoid is treating either as a shock to be absorbed until it passes. A tariff regime that is now explicitly reciprocal, with a prime minister publicly describing the relationship in wartime terms and refusing to resume talks, has no natural expiry, and an energy premium that analysts have moved into their central forecast is not being modeled as temporary by the people who price it. For technology, product, and marketing leaders the implication is a shift in what a roadmap is optimizing for. Demand elasticity just went up across consumer categories, which means pricing architecture, retention economics, and the ability to reforecast quickly matter more this quarter than any feature on the list, and the organizations that will handle it well are the ones that can change a price, a bundle, or an offer in days rather than in a release cycle.

#Tech industry

  • OpenAI says it hit its automated research intern milestone: The company announced on 7 September that it met the target set in an October 2025 livestream, a system that executes well-defined research tasks under human direction, including work that would take a skilled researcher several days. It disclosed two numbers alongside the claim: the research organization now logs 3.1 agent-workdays for every human workday, and more than half of successful four to eight hour agent tasks required at least one human intervention. The stated next target is a legitimate AI researcher by March 2028. Strategic implication: the honest version of this announcement is a supervised capacity multiplier, not autonomy, and the disclosure discipline is the part worth copying. A company reporting both its leverage ratio and its intervention rate is giving its board the two numbers needed to tell real capability from a demo. Most internal AI progress reports give the first and omit the second, which is why so many of them survive a board meeting and not a budget cycle.
  • ServiceNow buys Sweep for a reported several hundred million: The acquired company builds an agentic layer that governs enterprise systems across vendor boundaries, spanning Salesforce and HubSpot as well as ServiceNow. It is at least the seventh Israeli acquisition in a run whose cumulative value exceeds $8 billion, following ai.work under two months ago. Strategic implication: platform vendors have concluded that the durable position is not the system of record but the layer that governs agent activity across all of them. Any enterprise assuming its agent governance will be provided by whichever suite it already licenses should notice that the suites themselves do not believe that.

#AI at the leadership level

Two datasets published in recent weeks describe the same problem from opposite ends. McKinsey's State of AI 2026, from 1,719 respondents across 97 countries, found 40 percent of organizations above $1 billion in revenue now scaling AI agents, against 20 percent of organizations overall, a two-speed split that widened rather than closed over the year. The 2026 AI Adoption Gap Report from Human Driven AI, a three-year study across 22 organizations covering 2,724 professionals and 594 executive interviews, found 85 percent of professionals actively using AI in their work and 19 of the 22 organizations raising governance concerns, with the central finding stated plainly: organizations are not struggling to get employees to adopt AI, they are struggling to adapt to a workforce that already has.

Put together, the two-speed race is not a race in adoption. Individual adoption is close to universal and roughly flat across company sizes. The gap is institutional, and it sits in the ability to route agent work through controls, redesign the process around it, and account for the result. That is a governance capability, and governance capability is the thing that scales with balance sheet, which is why the split follows revenue rather than sector. The board-level consequence is that AI strategy discussions framed as adoption questions are answering a question the organization already resolved without asking. The live question is whether the operating model has changed to match what people are already doing, and in most organizations the honest answer is no, which means the risk being carried is not underuse, it is unaccounted use.

#Leadership & org design

The Human Driven AI study documents how the leadership question moved across three years, and the sequence is a useful diagnostic for locating where an organization actually is. In 2023 the question was whether employees should use AI. In 2024 and 2025 it was which tools to adopt. In 2026 it is how the work itself must change. An executive team still debating tool selection in the third year is not behind on procurement, it is one full question behind on the problem, and buying faster will not close that.

A working example of the third question ran in Korea last week. KB Financial Group held a group-wide AI agent competition drawing 316 employees across 116 teams from seven affiliates, including the bank, securities, insurance, and card businesses, with 12 teams reaching a final. The scoping decision is the instructive part: entries had to be agents deployable in real operations, with winning work expanded across the group, so the judging standard was production viability rather than presentation quality. That inverts where use cases originate. Instead of a central team inventing the automation list for processes it does not run, the people who own the processes nominate them, and the center's job becomes selection, standard-setting, and scaling. It also yields something no strategy offsite produces, a ranked and self-reported map of where the organization believes its own drudgery lives.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If our discretionary demand fell 5 percent this quarter and our input costs rose 3 percent, what is the fastest we could change a price, a bundle, or an offer, and is that number measured in days or in release cycles?

#Try this now

  • Re-architect your prompts around the 75 percent cache-read cut: Claude Fable 5.1 shipped 1 September with a 1M token context window and prompt cache reads dropping from roughly $1.00 to $0.25 per million tokens, while input and output pricing held at $10 and $50. The consequence is not that everything got cheaper. It is that the economics now reward putting large stable material into a cached prefix and keeping the variable part small. Take one production workflow this week and split its prompt in two: the durable half, meaning brand guidelines, product taxonomy, schema definitions, tone rules, policy text, and the volatile half, meaning this customer, this request, this campaign. Load the durable half once as a cached prefix and vary only the tail. Teams that were trimming context to control spend have been optimizing the wrong side of the bill.
  • Inventory every add-on your agents load, then check which ones fetch instructions at runtime: AIR Security came out of stealth on 1 September with research finding more than 17,800 public AI add-ons across 6.7 million installations that pull instructions from untrusted external sources. The mechanic is worth understanding precisely. A skill, plugin, or MCP server can look entirely legitimate at install time and still contain a line telling the agent to go read further instructions from a website, a GitHub repo, or a package. Nothing malicious sits in the artifact you reviewed. The instruction arrives later, from somewhere you never approved. The 20 minute version: list every skill, MCP server, sub-agent, and extension your agents currently load, and for each one answer whether its instructions are fixed at install or resolved at run. Anything in the second bucket is an unreviewed dependency with write access to your workflow.
  • Move your review queue before you add more agent capacity: GitHub put Agent Merge into public preview, which takes a pull request the rest of the way by resolving review feedback, failed checks, and merge conflicts, alongside experimental multi-root workspaces that run agent sessions per folder. The practitioner read is not about coding. It is that authoring stopped being the constraint and reviewing became it, and the tooling is now moving to the review side. Look at any workflow where you added AI to the production step, marketing creative, spec writing, analysis, and check where the work is actually queuing. If output volume went up and cycle time did not, the bottleneck moved and nobody re-measured.

#Case study worth stealing

KB Financial Group, sourcing the AI roadmap by competition instead of by committee: The Korean financial group ran a group-wide AI agent competition in the first week of September, drawing 316 employees across 116 teams from seven affiliates including the bank, the securities arm, the insurance arm, and the card business, with 12 teams reaching the final. The entries covered security log analysis, document review, customer feedback mining, insurance product development, and service operations. The design choice worth stealing is the entry criterion. The competition was explicitly scoped to agents deployable in real operations, with the winners' work expanded across the group, which means the judging standard was production viability rather than demo quality. Most organizations build the AI use case list centrally, in a room with fewer than a dozen people, none of whom run the processes being automated. This inverts it: the people who own the workflow nominate the workflow, and the central team's job becomes selection and scaling rather than invention. It also produces something a roadmap exercise never does, which is a ranked map of where employees already believe the drudgery is.

#Framework or mental model

Agent-workdays per human workday, and the intervention rate underneath it: OpenAI disclosed on 7 September that its research organization now logs 3.1 agent-workdays of effort for every human workday, and in the same disclosure noted that more than half of successful four to eight hour agent tasks required at least one human intervention. Read together those two numbers form a usable capacity model. The first is the multiplier, how much work the org can put in flight per person. The second is the tax, how often a person has to stop what they are doing to unblock it. A team can raise the multiplier by buying more agent capacity, which is cheap, and it will get nothing if the intervention rate stays where it is, because every intervention lands on a human whose day is already full. Digital leaders should measure both before approving the next agent expansion, because the ratio between them, not the multiplier alone, is what determines whether added capacity turns into delivered work or into a longer queue at the same few desks.

#Rabbit hole

OpenAI's writeup of the automated research intern milestone, published 7 September. The interesting part is not the claim, it is the grading. The company set a public target in an October 2025 livestream, then assessed its own progress against it a year later, and the honest disclosures are buried in the methodology: the supervision requirement, the intervention rate, and the fact that humans still set priorities and decide what scales. Worth an hour for anyone who has to write an internal AI progress report and is deciding how much of the failure surface to show.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • ServiceNow acquires Sweep: ServiceNow bought the Israeli startup Sweep in a deal reported at hundreds of millions of dollars, against $46 million of disclosed venture funding and a $22.5 million Series B in May 2025. Sweep is not a CRM. It is an agentic layer that sits across enterprise systems, connecting, interpreting, and governing them as they scale, and its integrations run into Salesforce and HubSpot as readily as into ServiceNow. Customers included Brex, LG Electronics, Mass General Brigham, NBC Sports, and Wix. ServiceNow's stated rationale was strengthening AI-native development and advancing agentic deployment for CRM. Signal: the layer being bought is the one that spans suites, not the one that deepens a single suite. A vendor that owns workflow inside its own product still loses the customer relationship at the boundary where the record moves to someone else's system. Buying the cross-suite governance layer is how a platform makes itself the place where multi-vendor agent activity is defined and adjudicated, which is a stronger position than owning any one system of record. Worth noting this is at least the seventh Israeli acquisition in ServiceNow's run, following ai.work less than two months earlier, with cumulative deal value above $8 billion. That is not opportunistic buying. That is a category being assembled.
  • CrowdStrike ships Falcon Guardian, and AIR Security leaves stealth the same week: Falcon Guardian discovers known and shadow AI agents across Windows and macOS through the existing Falcon sensor, producing a live inventory of every running and dormant agent, who deployed it, and its security posture, with runtime control rather than logging. AIR Security emerged on 1 September with an inline firewall for agent traffic and research finding more than 17,800 public AI add-ons across 6.7 million installations that draw instructions from untrusted external sources. Signal: agent inventory has become an endpoint security product rather than a governance spreadsheet, which tells you something uncomfortable about where agents actually entered the enterprise. They did not arrive through procurement. They arrived through browser extensions, developer tooling, and skill marketplaces on machines IT already manages, which is why the discovery product had to be built on the sensor that was already there.

#Category watch

The cross-suite agent governance layer: Twelve months ago this did not have a name and the assumption was that each application vendor would govern its own agents. That assumption is collapsing in two directions at once. From above, platform vendors are buying the span layer, which is what ServiceNow just did with Sweep and what Adobe did at smaller scale with Rilo. From below, endpoint security vendors are claiming the same territory on the grounds that they can already see every process on the machine, which is what CrowdStrike is doing with Falcon Guardian and what AIR Security is doing with an inline agent firewall. Those two approaches produce very different products. The platform version governs agents it can see through APIs it controls, which means it governs the agents you bought. The endpoint version governs whatever is executing, which means it also catches the agents nobody approved. Most enterprises will end up needing both and will discover that fact after buying only one, because the procurement conversation for each happens with a different buyer, the CIO for one and the CISO for the other, and neither one is scoped to notice the gap.

#Stack intelligence

The build versus buy shift now has a number attached to it and a shape worth reading carefully. McKinsey's State of AI 2026, drawn from 1,719 respondents across 97 countries, found 32 percent of organizations declined to buy at least one software product or feature because agentic coding tools made building it internally viable, concentrated in technology and healthcare, then professional services and energy. Among organizations above $1 billion in revenue, 40 percent report scaling AI agents, against 20 percent of organizations overall.

The practical read for anyone building a stack this quarter is that those two findings point in opposite directions and both are true. Large enterprises are scaling agents twice as fast as the market, which increases the number of internal systems that agents touch, and simultaneously buying less packaged software, which decreases the number of vendors obligated to keep those systems compatible. The seam between those two facts is integration maintenance, and it lands on internal teams by default. The layer worth buying rather than building right now is precisely the seam layer, the identity, the semantic definitions, the governance plane, because it is the part whose cost grows with the number of things connected to it rather than with the number of features it has. The layer worth building is the one that encodes decisions specific to how the business works, because no vendor sells it and no vendor can maintain it.

#Foundations concept of the day

Marketing mix modeling, and the incrementality question underneath it: Marketing mix modeling, usually shortened to MMM, is a statistical method for estimating how much each marketing channel contributed to sales, using aggregate historical data rather than individual user tracking. You feed it two to three years of weekly numbers, spend by channel, price, promotions, distribution, seasonality, competitor activity, and macro variables like unemployment or fuel cost, and it fits a model that attributes the movement in sales to those inputs. Because it uses aggregate data, it works without cookies, without device identifiers, and without consent from any individual, which is why a technique invented for consumer packaged goods in the 1960s came back into fashion after the identity layer of digital advertising degraded.

The thing MMM is really trying to answer is incrementality, meaning the sales that happened because of the marketing and would not have happened otherwise. This is a harder question than it sounds and it is the question most reporting quietly avoids. Last-click attribution credits the final touch before a purchase, which systematically over-credits channels that intercept people who had already decided, search on your own brand name being the classic example. A retargeting campaign shown to someone with your product already in their cart will look magnificent in the platform dashboard and may have changed nothing. MMM estimates the counterfactual instead, and a well-specified model routinely finds that the highest-credited channels in the platform reports are among the least incremental.

Why it matters at scale: when budgets are cut, the cut usually follows the reporting, and the reporting favors the channels closest to the transaction. So the first thing to go is upper-funnel spend whose effect the attribution model could never see, and the sales decline shows up two to four quarters later with no obvious cause. A cross-industry example: a large grocery retailer moves budget out of broadcast and into retail media because the retail media dashboard shows a return on ad spend of eight to one and broadcast shows almost nothing measurable. The retail media number is largely capturing shoppers already in the aisle. Two quarters later, new customer acquisition is down and nothing in the dashboard explains it. The defensible practice is to treat MMM as the budget allocation instrument, platform attribution as an operational tuning instrument, and to run at least one geographic holdout test a year, meaning turn a channel off in matched regions and measure the difference, because that is the only method that observes the counterfactual directly rather than estimating it.

#Watch list

  • Dreamforce, 15 to 17 September: The relevant question is not what Agentforce announces, it is whether Salesforce responds to the cross-suite governance move by extending its own agent layer beyond its perimeter or by defending inside it. A vendor that governs only its own agents is conceding the seam, and after this week the seam has a price tag on it.
  • The endpoint vendors' agent inventory data: Both CrowdStrike and AIR Security now have telemetry on what is actually running in enterprises rather than what was procured. Their next published datasets will be the first credible measure of the gap between the agent estate on the governance slide and the agent estate on the machines, and that gap is the number every AI governance program should be sized against.

#Macro pulse

Not a quiet day. Two policy shocks converged on the first US session after Labour Day.

Canada: Ottawa's retaliatory tariffs took effect this morning on more than 700 American products, at 15, 25, or 50 percent by category, matching dollar for dollar the 50 percent US tariff imposed on roughly C$27.6 billion of Canadian exports on 22 August. Targets include steel, dairy, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. The prime minister has said talks will not resume until Washington changes tone, which removes the near-term de-escalation path the market had been partially pricing. The TSX has been trading around 36,500 and slightly lower, with energy and financials, roughly 17 and 33 percent of the index, pulling in opposite directions: energy bid on the oil move, financials weighed by the trade shock. The loonie has been the cleaner read than the index all week and it has been soft. For a Canadian carrier this cuts both ways and the net is negative. A weaker Canadian dollar raises the local-currency cost of jet fuel, aircraft leases, and US-dollar debt service faster than it flatters translated international revenue. Economist Trevor Tombe puts the retaliation's economy-wide cost near C$4 billion, with average consumer prices up about 0.25 percent and households under $30,000 losing more than half a percent of disposable income, which is a direct hit to the leisure travel demand pool.

United States: Futures opened the session lower, with S&P 500 futures off 0.2 percent and Dow futures down 383 points, or 0.7 percent, ahead of the regular session. Two named drivers, the Iran conflict and the Canada trade escalation, which is an unusual combination in that neither is a rates or earnings story. Energy names are the offset holding the broad index up relative to the Dow.

Asia: Japan took the worst of it. The Nikkei 225 fell 1.70 percent to 65,269.33 and the TOPIX fell 1.83 percent to 4,050.33, which is the expected shape for a large net energy importer on a crude spike. The Hang Seng slipped 0.38 percent to 25,317.18 and the Shanghai Composite rose 0.20 percent to 3,940.55, mainland resilience against a regional risk-off session.

Oil: The number that matters today. WTI for October advanced 1.07 percent to $92.56 and Brent for November gained 0.20 percent to $97.20, a third consecutive daily gain and six-week highs, after the US struck three Iranian oil tankers over the weekend in response to Iranian ballistic missiles fired at two US Navy warships. Goldman Sachs raised its December 2026 forecasts by $5 to $85 Brent and $80 WTI, and lifted 2027 to $80 and $75. That revision is the more consequential item than the daily move. An analyst raising a year-ahead and a two-year-ahead forecast is moving the conflict from the risk case into the base case.

#Today's industry move

Airlines are where the macro lands hardest and the damage is already structural rather than prospective. US jet fuel roughly doubled from $2.50 a gallon on 27 February to $4.88 by 2 April after the conflict opened, and remains more than 80 percent above pre-conflict levels. IATA has the 2026 industry fuel bill at $350 billion, with margins at their weakest since the pandemic. United has guided to nearly $6 billion of added full-year fuel expense against its own start-of-year expectation and raised $3.7 billion in private bank liquidity explicitly as insurance against a further spike. Southwest reported fuel expense up $900 million year over year. Carriers pulled 9.3 million seats from the 1 June to 30 September schedule, per Cirium. US airline equities are in a bear market, down more than 22 percent from a multi-year high set only last month.

The strategic read for a digital organization inside a carrier: capacity has already been cut and fares already raised, which means the remaining margin levers are commercial and technological. Ancillary attach, dynamic offer construction, loyalty currency economics, and cost per served offer are no longer optimization projects, they are the P&L. That is a very different funding conversation than the one most digital roadmaps were built for.

ServiceNow acquired Sweep in a deal reported at hundreds of millions against $46 million of disclosed funding, buying a cross-suite agentic governance layer that spans Salesforce and HubSpot as well as ServiceNow. At least the seventh Israeli acquisition in a run exceeding $8 billion cumulatively, following ai.work under two months earlier. The signal is a multiple paid for span rather than for revenue.

#AI infrastructure watch

No new build announcement today, but the energy repricing is the infrastructure story. Hyperscalers have guided to roughly $700 billion of 2026 capex against about $410 billion in 2025, with the fourteen largest data center operators near $750 billion against under $450 billion last year, and US data center grid demand tracking toward roughly triple by 2030. A crude spike does not hit that build directly, since data centers run on electricity rather than oil, but it moves the marginal cost of gas-fired generation, and gas is what sets the clearing price in most of the markets where these campuses are being sited.

The structural observation worth holding: the hyperscalers that spent 2025 and early 2026 signing twenty-year nuclear power purchase agreements, Meta at up to 6.6 gigawatts across Vistra, TerraPower, Oklo, and Constellation, Amazon at 1,920 megawatts from Talen's Susquehanna facility through 2042, were widely read as making a decarbonization statement. Days like today reveal the other half of the rationale. A fixed-price twenty-year contract is a hedge against exactly this, and the firms that locked it in have converted an input that is now moving on Middle East headlines into a known line item. Anyone forecasting inference cost curves off published token prices should note that the cost floor underneath those prices is being set by power contracts signed years earlier, and the labs with the best contracts have the most room to cut.

#Signal or noise?

Signal, and specifically a regime signal rather than an event. The tariff schedule is legislated and reciprocal with no expiry and no active negotiation, and the crude premium has been moved into sell-side base cases rather than scenario tables. Neither of those unwinds on a headline. The one-day moves are noise. The repricing behind them is not.

#Watch tomorrow

Whether the loonie stabilizes once the retaliation is actually in force rather than anticipated. Tariff currency moves usually front-run the effective date and mean-revert modestly after it, so a Canadian dollar that keeps weakening tomorrow with the news already priced would say the market is discounting a longer regime than the announced measures, and that changes the fuel and lease math for every Canadian carrier well beyond this quarter.
