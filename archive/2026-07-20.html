Executive Pulse895 words

#Macro signal

Energy shock re-prices the entire rate path: A ninth consecutive night of US strikes on Iran and the suspension of the June interim accord pushed Brent to $90.97, up 3.3%, with direct tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz turning a risk premium into a physical supply problem. Swaps traders cut expected 2026 Fed easing to 56 basis points from 60, CME FedWatch now puts an 85.6% probability on no change at the July meeting, and the Bloomberg dollar index jumped 0.8% to its highest since early February. The ECB meets Thursday facing pressure for a second consecutive hike into weak growth, and the PBoC held its one-year rate at 3.0% to defend the yuan rather than stimulate.

The strategic read: the disinflation trade that underwrote every 2026 plan is now conditional on a geopolitical variable no operator controls. If you built next year's budget on a falling cost of capital, that assumption is live again. The dollar surge also quietly tightens conditions for anyone with emerging market revenue or supply exposure, and the IMF's 3.8% emerging market growth forecast carries clear downside from the combination of stronger dollar and higher oil. Leaders should be stress testing the stagflation case, not just the soft landing, and asking which contracts and hedges reset if energy stays above $90 through Q4.

#Tech industry

  • The AI infrastructure trade broke, and it was valuation not fundamentals: The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 11% in a week and is down roughly 24% from its late-June high, formally a bear market, with more than a trillion dollars of market value erased. Three catalysts stacked up: reports that Meta is standing up Meta Compute to resell surplus training and inference capacity, which rewrites the supply side of the AI infrastructure equation; Intel's 18A-P node reportedly not reaching profitable yields until late 2026 or 2027, undercutting a stock that had run 278% in H1; and SK Hynix slowing its high bandwidth memory expansion. Today's session added falling chips, rising oil, and a 10% post-earnings Netflix decline into one move. The implication is not that AI demand is fading, it is that the market has stopped paying in advance for it. Capital allocation committees that waved through AI spend on narrative alone will now be asked for a return schedule.
  • Inference is becoming a commodity input: Grok 4.5, GPT-5.6, and Muse Spark 1.1 all launched inside a 24 hour window, dragging output token pricing into the $4 to $6 range against $25 to $50 for legacy flagships, while mid-tier models approach flagship performance and open-weight options offer a cheaper usage-based route. Any product margin model built on last year's token costs is stale, and any moat premised on model access is gone. The defensible layer is proprietary data, workflow ownership, and distribution, not the model.

#AI at the leadership level

The gap is no longer adoption, it is governance. Board-level AI use has gone near universal, with 92% of directors reporting they used AI for board work in the past six months against 69% a year earlier, but formal oversight has not followed: documented responsibilities, committee assignments, and structured AI risk reporting remain rare. The structural data says the same thing. AI listed as a disclosed risk among S&P 500 companies went from 12% to 83% between 2023 and 2025, yet disclosed board AI expertise crawled from 1.5% to 2.7%, only 23% of executives rate their board as highly fluent in AI, and just 9% call their company very prepared for AI regulatory compliance. Boards are now heavy users of a technology they are not equipped to govern, which is precisely the shape of an incident waiting to be written up. The near-term move is unglamorous: name the owner, define the escalation path, and put AI risk on a reporting cadence before a regulator or an incident defines it for you.

#Leadership & org design

The flattening is moving from rhetoric to filed restructurings, and the interesting part is the design intent rather than the headcount. GitLab paired a 7% workforce reduction with the removal of up to three management layers in some functions and a split of R&D into roughly 60 smaller autonomous teams, explicitly framed as preparation for an agentic era. Coinbase cut to five layers below the CEO and COO and is piloting one-person teams that fold engineering, design, and product into a single role. Bayer, Amazon, and Meta have made comparable public commitments, and roughly 41% of employees say their company trimmed management layers last year.

The logic is specific, not fashionable. Middle management's core function was information routing: decisions down, status up, coordination across. Agents do all three, so the coordination layer compresses and spans of control widen. The trap is cutting the layer without replacing what it actually did, because judgment, context, and talent development were never routing work. Organizations that flatten well are redesigning the decision rights and the information flow first, then removing the layer. Ones that flatten badly are removing the layer and discovering what it was holding.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If oil stays above $90 and the Fed does not cut this year, which three commitments in our current plan stop being fundable, and would we rather choose which ones to kill now or have the number choose for us in October?

#Try this now

  • Claude Fable 5 access rules land today: Anthropic ended three weeks of rolling deadline extensions. As of July 20, Fable 5 is included in Max and Team Premium at 50% of standard weekly limits. Pro and Team Standard drop to usage credits with a one-time $100 grant, after which it bills at API rates of $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens. If a team standardized on Fable for high-stakes drafting or analysis while it was effectively free, the unit economics changed overnight. Worth 20 minutes to audit who on the team is on which plan and reroute routine work to Sonnet 5, which sits at $3 in and $15 out after the introductory window closes August 31.
  • SQLite Query Explainer: A browser tool that runs SQLite through Pyodide in WebAssembly, with no backend, and annotates both EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN and the low-level bytecode with plain-English descriptions of what the query planner is actually doing. Useful for analysts and PMs who write SQL against product data but have never been able to read a query plan. Caveat from the builder: the explanations have not been independently verified, so treat them as a learning aid rather than a source of truth.
  • LLM Cliché Highlighter: A single-purpose web app that flags ten recurring tells of machine-generated prose, including constructions like "worth naming" and "is real and." Run marketing copy, exec summaries, and customer-facing content through it before publishing. The practical value is not detection, it is calibration: it shows a team exactly which verbal tics their AI-assisted drafts keep reintroducing, which is the fastest way to build an internal style guard.

#Case study worth stealing

Klarna, the walk-back on AI-first customer service: Klarna's assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, resolved roughly two-thirds of support tickets, and cut average resolution time from 11 minutes to 2, with an estimated $40 million annual profit impact. The company then cut human support capacity too aggressively and had to start rebuilding it, because the system degraded on complex, ambiguous, and emotionally charged cases, and the institutional expertise to handle those had already walked out the door. The adaptable lesson is to segment the queue before you resize the team: automate the high-volume deterministic tier, then hold headcount on the ambiguous tier for at least two full quarters of production data before touching it. Deflection rate is a vanity metric if you are not also tracking resolution quality on the hardest 10% of contacts.

#Framework or mental model

The Coordination Trap: An essay published this week argues that AI project failure is systematically underreported not because individuals are dishonest but because no one can defect first. If an executive admits their AI gains are illusory, they embarrass peers and take on career risk. If everyone admitted it simultaneously, nobody would lose credibility, but no mechanism exists to coordinate that. The result is an organization where the public signal and the private belief have fully decoupled. The usable diagnostic is anonymous project sentiment polling: the author describes seeing half a team rate an initiative 3 out of 10 while the other half rated it 8 out of 10, and reads that bimodal split as direct evidence of suppressed information rather than genuine disagreement. Pair it with a ground-level adoption check, since many internal chatbot deployments show near-zero real uptake regardless of what the steering committee reports. Any leader running an AI portfolio can run both tests this quarter.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Apple: Apple emailed advertisers on July 14 about revised Advertising Services Terms of Service taking effect July 28. The contractual definition of where Apple Ads content may appear widened from Apple-owned apps and devices to a broader set of devices, operating systems, software, web applications and unspecified "other platforms or properties." This is the first documented case of Apple's own legal language describing ad surfaces it does not own. A separate Apple advertising policy effective July 14 sets Maps ad rules and bans home services categories (plumbing, HVAC, locksmith, roofing, pest control). Signal: the most privacy-positioned walled garden is quietly writing itself permission to sell demand off its own property. If Apple runs an off-platform network, the buy side gets a third large identity-rich demand source and the "privacy as product" narrative gets a lot more complicated to plan around.
  • Spotify: Six years after acquiring Megaphone, Spotify has fully absorbed the product into the Spotify Ad Server. Migration completed this month, VAST tags stopped working, and maximum ad duration moved from 60 to 90 seconds. Megaphone's Nielsen-based audience targeting and low-waterfall "Promotions" inventory were force-migrated too. Signal: acquisition is not integration, and the gap can run half a decade. The visible tell that a platform finally consolidated is when it kills the open standard (VAST) that let advertisers route around it. Every audio and video buyer who built workflows on Megaphone tags just had a dependency rewritten for them.
  • Zoom / Common Room: Zoom signed a definitive agreement on July 2 to acquire Common Room, an AI-native go-to-market intelligence platform that unifies CRM, product usage, marketing and engagement signals into person-level buyer profiles, with agents doing account research and outreach personalization. Close expected within weeks. Customers include Atlassian, Autodesk, Notion, Okta and Snowflake. Signal: buyer intelligence is being pulled inside the communications layer rather than sold as a standalone signal vendor. The category boundary between "where the conversation happens" and "who you should be talking to" is dissolving, which puts pressure on standalone intent-data vendors to become features.

#Category watch

GTM and revenue intelligence: This category is consolidating fast into adjacent platforms rather than growing as its own tier. Zoom absorbing Common Room, Marchex completing its Archenia acquisition on July 1 to fuse first-party call data with AI lead qualification, and Clari partnering with Salesloft on conversation-derived forecasting all point the same direction. The pattern: signal capture, signal interpretation, and signal action are collapsing into single vendors, because an agent that can research an account is only useful if it also sits where the outreach happens. For anyone building a martech org, the practical read is that the "best-of-breed intent vendor" slot in the stack is the one most likely to disappear into a platform renewal in the next 18 months.

#Stack intelligence

The strongest datapoint of the week is a negative one. Only about 34% of Salesforce customers have adopted Agentforce, roughly 23,000 of 150,000, and KeyBanc analysts attribute the gap to two things: enterprise data that is not clean, structured or connected, and deployments stuck in proof-of-concept rather than production. Deployment is not the bottleneck. Data readiness is.

This reframes the buy decision. The vendor layer has effectively finished shipping agentic capability. In the mid-July release wave alone, 6sense, OneSignal, Omneky and Wistia all shipped MCP servers, and dozens of vendors shipped autonomous agents across creative, research, lifecycle and service. Capability is now commodity and abundant. What is scarce is a data spine clean enough for an agent to act on without a human verifying every output.

The org-building implication: budget that would have gone to a third agentic point tool this year buys more if it goes to entity resolution, schema governance, and consent state consolidation. The companies moving fastest in 2027 will be the ones who did the unglamorous integration work in 2026, not the ones with the longest AI vendor list.

#Foundations concept of the day

Ad server and VAST: An ad server is the system that decides which specific creative to deliver into a given ad slot at request time, then logs the impression and the outcome. Publishers run one to manage their own inventory and direct-sold deals; advertisers run one to control creative and measure across the sites they buy. VAST (Video Ad Serving Template) is the open XML standard that lets a video or audio player on one company's platform call an ad from another company's ad server and understand the response: where the file is, how long it runs, which tracking pixels to fire. It matters at scale because VAST is what makes ad serving portable. If your creative and measurement run through VAST tags, you can move inventory and vendors without rebuilding your tracking. When a platform drops VAST support, as Spotify just did in folding Megaphone into its own ad server, every buyer on that platform loses the ability to serve and verify through their own stack and must trust the platform's numbers instead. Cross-industry example: this is the same dynamic that played out in mobile when app platforms moved measurement in-house, and in retail media where most networks report their own attribution with no independent serving path. The strategic question for a leader is not which ad server you use but how many of your channels can still be measured by a system you control.

#Watch list

  • Apple's off-property ad ambitions: The terms change lands July 28. Watch for any actual third-party placement, SDK, or DSP integration announcement in the following quarter. If it materializes, identity strategy for iOS-heavy audiences changes materially.
  • MCP servers as a martech distribution channel: The volume of vendors shipping MCP servers in a single release wave suggests the integration surface is shifting from API-and-dashboard to agent-queryable. Worth tracking whether buyers start evaluating vendors on agent accessibility rather than UI, because that would change how stacks get shortlisted.

#Macro week in review

Canada. The TSX held up better than almost anything else on the board. Friday it slipped 76.30 points, or 0.22%, to close at 35,263.85, staying above the 35,000 line. The mix explains the resilience: energy names rallied hard on the oil spike while mining and banks dragged. For a Canadian carrier the index is only half the story. The Bank of Canada held its overnight rate at 2.25% on Wednesday, the sixth consecutive hold, and struck a relatively hawkish tone, flagging that the economy is recovering from a weak start to the year and that inflation will run above prior forecasts through 2026 before easing. USD/CAD sat near 1.4050 to 1.4060, close to a one month low for the pair, meaning a modestly stronger loonie. A stronger CAD trims the translated value of USD-denominated international ticket revenue, but it also trims the CAD cost of fuel and USD-denominated leases, which matters more this week than usual given what crude did.

United States. A broad risk-off week. The S&P 500 fell 1.55%, the Nasdaq 2.9%, and the Dow 0.93%. Friday alone the S&P 500 lost 1.01% to 7,457.69, the Nasdaq 1.40% to 25,520.24, and the Dow 406.55 points, or 0.77%, to 52,146.42. This was the S&P's first losing week in three and only its third since March. Two drivers: a semiconductor rout, with the VanEck Semiconductor ETF down more than 4% on Friday, and renewed US and Iran escalation. Sector rotation was textbook defensive plus energy. Energy gained 4.7% on the week, REITs 2.2%, and consumer staples 1.27%, while tech and semis led the decline.

Asia. The worst of it. The Nikkei 225 fell 4.03% on Friday to 64,141.12 and posted its worst weekly loss since April 2025. The Shanghai Composite dropped 3.05% Friday to 3,764.155 and 5.81% on the week, with the Shenzhen Component down 8.9%. The Hang Seng fell 1.78% Friday to 24,562.24. Two forces stacked: the same AI spending and semiconductor valuation anxiety hitting the US, plus domestic liquidity pressure in China from upcoming mega-IPOs pulling cash out of the secondary market.

Oil and jet fuel. This is the line item that changes airline planning. WTI rose to $82.49 per barrel on July 17, up 4.48% in a single session and up more than 14% on the week, as the US reinstated a naval blockade near Iranian ports and Strait of Hormuz tanker traffic slowed to near halted. EIA's July Short-Term Energy Outlook noted refiners were holding jet fuel production alongside distillate to meet high global demand, which has kept the crack from blowing out even further, but a 14% weekly move in the feedstock is not something a hedging book absorbs quietly. Every carrier planning committee should be reworking Q4 fuel assumptions this week.

Rates and the consumer. The Fed is not riding to the rescue. Futures put roughly a 75% probability on a hold at 3.50% to 3.75%, with a modest tail pricing a hike rather than a cut, and implied odds of even one 25 basis point cut this year sitting near 20%. Inflation is the reason: CPI ran 4.2% year over year in May, and the Fed's preferred gauge came in at 4.1% headline with 3.4% core. Against that, the University of Michigan consumer sentiment print was the week's genuine upside surprise, rising to 54.4 in July versus a 51.0 expectation, a second straight monthly gain and the highest since February, helped by falling gasoline prices. All five components improved, with roughly 20% gains in durable goods buying conditions and year-ahead business expectations. One year inflation expectations eased to 4.2% from 4.6%. The catch: sentiment is still 12% below year ago levels, and the survey was taken before this week's oil spike works through to the pump.

The theme heading into next week. Consumer sentiment just improved on cheap gasoline, and gasoline is about to stop being cheap. That is a direct, mechanical threat to the discretionary travel demand curve in Q4. Pair it with a central bank on both sides of the border that has no room to cut, and the setup for travel and consumer discretionary is a demand signal that improved for a reason that is already reversing.

#MarTech & AdTech

  • Adobe, Salesforce, and HubSpot: The convergence story hardened rather than broke this week. All three are now positioning their platforms as infrastructure for AI agents rather than interfaces for human operators, with Adobe having rebranded Experience Cloud toward an agent-first CX posture. The distinction between martech, adtech, and sales tech is collapsing into one addressable surface, which means procurement decisions made on category logic are now buying overlapping capability.
  • The Trade Desk and Adobe: The Real-Time CDP integration lets advertisers activate first-party data across channels including CTV without leaving the Adobe stack. The signal is that the demand side is being pulled into the customer data layer rather than the reverse. For anyone in an Adobe shop, this deepens the gravity well: the more activation runs through the native integration, the harder the identity and audience layer is to move later.

#AI models & APIs

  • Moonshot AI, Kimi K3: The week's defining event. Moonshot released Kimi K3 on Friday, a 2.8 trillion parameter open weights model, the largest open weight release to date, claiming benchmark performance trailing only Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 on overall capability, at a fraction of the cost. Markets treated it as a second DeepSeek moment. IG's Tony Sycamore estimated $314 billion was wiped from valuation estimates for the two unlisted US labs. The strategic implication is not that Chinese labs caught up, it is that frontier-adjacent capability is now downloadable and self-hostable. Any enterprise AI roadmap built on the assumption that top-tier inference will stay expensive and API-gated needs rerunning.
  • Open weights as a pricing floor: The market reaction repriced the AI trade by layer, not uniformly. Model providers selling access got hit. Anyone whose business assumes cheap, abundant inference got a tailwind. That split is the useful frame for the next several quarters.

#Travel & airline tech

  • Amadeus, the look-to-book crisis: Both Amadeus and Sabre have now pointed to a possible future of up to 200,000 searches per ticket sold as agentic AI shoppers flood distribution systems with fare queries. Amadeus is answering with precomputed fare caching and an Advanced Airline Profile filtering layer that uses machine learning to block unactionable NDC search traffic before it reaches airline systems. Early results cut unproductive traffic by more than 70% in some cases, and Air France-KLM reported the solution blocked the majority of irrelevant traffic to its systems. This is the most underrated infrastructure problem in airline digital right now. Agentic shopping is a cost-per-search problem long before it is a conversion opportunity.
  • Amadeus and Microsoft, agentic airline operations: Their joint airline agentic AI whitepaper argues agents are production-ready across rebooking, commerce, marketing, turnaround management, and service. Trials show an agent identifying a booking, parsing a verbal change request, proposing options, articulating the fare differential, and initiating payment across multiple languages. The gating factor named in the work is governance, not capability.
  • Amadeus versus Sabre: The competitive fight escalated, with Sabre's CEO accusing Amadeus of holding a dominant monopoly position and signalling regulatory and legal avenues, while Amadeus argues its footprint now spans retailing, biometric identity, hospitality, and payments. Distribution leverage is becoming a regulatory question, not just a commercial one.
  • Air Canada and Club Avolta: Aeroplan members can now earn points on eligible purchases at more than 900 airport retail locations operated by Hudson and Dufry, at one point per C$2 spent, expanding later in 2026 to HMSHost food and beverage for nearly 1,900 total locations. Earning extends through Avolta's Reserve and Collect and the Club Avolta app. The signal: loyalty currency is being pushed into the physical airport dwell window, where intent is high and the airline previously had no earn surface.

#AI Infrastructure Stack

Raw materials & hardware

  • Critical minerals repricing: All 18 tracked rare earth elements rose in July, averaging a 16.7% gain, led by germanium up 27.8%, indium up 26.5%, and dysprosium up 25.4%. Gallium hit $288.82 per kg on the SMM industrial benchmark on July 1, up 4.2% from June, while the China FOB export price held near $400 per kg for a third straight month. That gap is the tell: export licensing, not geology, is the binding constraint. China's January catalogue update added samarium, gadolinium, and lutetium controls, and the current export-control reprieve expires November 10, 2026, which sets up a potential physical scarcity event in ex-China markets before Western supply reaches commercial scale.

Chips & semiconductors

  • TSMC: Record quarter, punished anyway. Net income surged 77% on record revenue, quarterly sales up 36%, June monthly revenue around NT$442 billion, up 67.9% year over year. Shares still fell 7.3% in Taipei after the company raised both spending and revenue projections. Raising capex is now read as a risk signal rather than a confidence signal, which is a meaningful shift in how the market is underwriting the AI buildout.
  • ASML: Raised full-year 2026 revenue guidance to EUR 43 to 45 billion from EUR 36 to 40 billion, the second increase this year, on AI chip demand. The equipment layer is still seeing orders that contradict the demand-doubt narrative in the equity tape.
  • Memory versus foundry divergence: Memory names led Asian tech lower on oversupply concerns and profit taking, while AI-serving foundries held on long-term demand. HBM supply, which Nvidia and AMD depend on through suppliers like SK Hynix, is being priced as a commodity cycle again rather than a structural shortage.
  • Intel: Became the first company to ship high-volume logic using ASML High-NA EUV scanners, alongside reported yield improvement on 18A. A credible second leading-edge source changes the negotiating position of every large accelerator buyer.

Cooling & energy

  • Nvidia's closed-loop liquid cooling: Newest AI servers move fully to liquid cooling using a recirculated water and propylene glycol coolant running hot, which Nvidia says eliminates large amounts of power usage and effectively all water usage. Water permitting has become a siting constraint in several jurisdictions, so removing it is as much a regulatory unlock as an efficiency one.
  • Rack-level physics is the new bottleneck: The Data Center Insights conference on July 15 and 16 centered on liquid cooling chemistry, 800VDC power architecture for megawatt-class racks, and 800G fiber validation. Roughly 76% of AI servers are expected to be liquid-cooled in 2026, and the liquid cooling market is around $3.7 billion this year. Facilities without liquid cooling already installed are not competitive for current-generation deployments.
  • Virginia's data center power tax: A consumption levy of $0.011 per kilowatt-hour on all data center electricity took effect July 1, expected to raise roughly $600 million annually. The first serious sign that jurisdictions will monetize AI load rather than simply court it. Expect the model to travel.
  • Nuclear as baseload strategy: The pattern set by Microsoft's roughly $1 billion, 20-year Constellation PPA for 835 MW from Three Mile Island Unit 1, and extended by Google's Kairos Power SMR agreement, is now the default hyperscaler playbook. AI data centers are projected to push US data center power demand from roughly 176 TWh today to 325 to 580 TWh by 2028, and nuclear is the only always-on zero-carbon source at that scale.

Data centers & cloud capex

  • Hyperscaler capex guidance keeps climbing: Forecasts for 2026 hyperscaler AI capital spending were raised toward the $750 billion range from roughly $670 billion, with Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, and Alphabet collectively guiding to north of $710 billion combined and projections crossing $1 trillion in 2027. The capex commitment is going up in the same week the equity market punished the companies making the commitment.

Stack insight

The stack split in two this week and the split is the story. Physical layers, foundry, lithography, cooling, and power, are reporting record demand and raising guidance. The financial layer is repricing risk downward on the same news. Kimi K3 is the hinge: if frontier-adjacent capability is open weights and cheap to run, the value of proprietary model access falls while the value of compute, power, and cooling to run inference everywhere rises. The 12 to 18 month constraint is not chips. It is interconnection queues, megawatt-rack thermal design, and licensed access to gallium, germanium, and heavy rare earths ahead of the November 10 expiry. Whoever locked power and cooling in 2025 will ship in 2027. Whoever is still negotiating a grid interconnect will not, regardless of how many accelerators they can buy.

#Funding rounds worth noting

  • Fireworks AI, $1.505 billion Series D: Tooling for enterprises to turn general-purpose models into specialized intelligence trained on their own data. The size of this round is the market pricing the exact thesis Kimi K3 validated: the durable value is in adapting models to proprietary data, not in owning the base model.
  • Helsing, $1.8 billion: Led by JPMorgan Chase, Lightspeed, and Iconiq. Defense AI at a scale that now competes with frontier labs for capital and talent.
  • Together AI, $800 million Series C: Led by Aramco Ventures at an $8.3 billion post-money valuation. Infrastructure for running open source models, funded by an energy major. That investor pairing tells you where both open weights and power converge.
  • AIsphere, $439 million Series C: Led by Alibaba, closed July 14.
  • Chai Discovery, $400 million Series C: Led by Index, Kleiner Perkins, and Sequoia.
  • 8090 Solutions, $135 million: Led by Salesforce. A platform for building enterprise software with coordinated AI agents under human oversight, which is Salesforce buying optionality on the agent-built-software thesis rather than building it internally.

#M&A signal

Deal flow this week was concentrated outside the AI application layer. American Industrial Partners agreed to take Avanos Medical private in an all-cash deal valued near $1.272 billion, and GH Power, a clean-energy and critical-materials company, is going public via combination with Matinas BioPharma. No major MarTech or AdTech acquisition landed. The more interesting consolidation signal is negative: with the Google and HubSpot combination having already collapsed under antitrust pressure, the large platforms are pursuing integration and minority investment rather than acquisition. Adobe and The Trade Desk partnering instead of merging, and Salesforce leading a round rather than buying the company, are the same behaviour. Category consolidation is still happening, it is just being executed through partnership and equity stakes because the regulatory path for outright purchase is closed.

#The week's big bet

The week's biggest bet was not made by a buyer, it was made by Moonshot giving away a 2.8 trillion parameter model. Releasing frontier-adjacent capability as open weights is a bet that the value in AI migrates from model access to everything required to run models at scale, which is precisely the layer where capex kept climbing even as equities sold off. The market read it immediately, wiping an estimated $314 billion from US lab valuations while energy, REITs, and the physical infrastructure stack held or gained. For digital leaders in travel and MarTech, the implication is concrete: any vendor contract, roadmap, or business case that assumes premium model access is the scarce input is now built on a melting assumption. The scarce inputs are proprietary data, the ability to serve inference cheaply, and the governance to let agents transact. Spend the next planning cycle securing those, and stop paying a premium for a capability that just became downloadable.

#The convergence watch (loyalty data → media networks)

Card spend has now grown double digits for seven consecutive quarters, concentrated in premium Reserve holders. What it signals: the co-brand channel is still the highest-margin monetization of loyalty data at scale, and it sets the revenue bar any airline media network has to beat before it earns real board attention. (Benzinga transcript, Investing.com transcript)

Loyalty revenue up 11%, a second-quarter record for new co-brand card accounts at plus 22%, card spend plus 14%, MileagePlus enrollments plus 9%. Notably, Kinective Media was not broken out as a disclosed revenue line. What it signals: the airline that built the category's first media network is still reporting the loyalty story through the card, not the ad business. Media network revenue remains strategically loud and financially quiet, which is the honest state of the category. (Yahoo Finance call highlights, AeroTime)
  • Myer launches an in-house media network off MYER one (Jul 16): Australian department store Myer stood up the Myer Media Network, built on MYER one loyalty data and omnichannel touchpoints, with an AI-native sponsored product layer from Mirakl Ads, and hired Michael Sharlassian to run it. What it signals: the loyalty-program-to-media-network playbook is now being executed by mid-cap retailers with a few million members, not just 100M-member carriers. The scale threshold for a defensible network is falling, which compresses the window in which "we are too small for this" is a credible answer. (Mi3, ContentGrip)
  • Trend line, not this week: The demand-side plumbing that makes travel loyalty data sellable keeps consolidating. Marriott put Bonvoy segments into a self-serve model via The Trade Desk in mid-June, monetizing a roughly 237M-member graph with 200-plus targetable attributes across roughly 9,500 properties. United and Marriott have been jointly pitching advertisers on in-trip targeting across seatback and in-room screens since March. Kinective Media is also selling inventory on behalf of JetBlue, meaning one carrier is now an ad sales house for another. Watch whether a second carrier signs onto Kinective rather than building its own. (AdExchanger, Skift, Adweek)

#Airline loyalty & digital

  • Lufthansa Group: Miles & More will be renamed Lufthansa Group Miles & More by end of 2026, consolidating branding across group carriers including ITA Airways. What it signals: group-level loyalty consolidation as the integration lever. When a program becomes the group brand rather than the airline brand, the data asset is being centralized ahead of any monetization play. (Upgraded Points, week of Jul 14-18)
  • JetBlue: Partnered with ClarityPay to offer installment payment at checkout, with the lowest available plan surfaced inline. By year end, TrueBlue points will accrue incrementally as installments are paid rather than at purchase. What it signals: points earn is being decoupled from the transaction moment and re-attached to the payment schedule. That turns the loyalty ledger into a repayment engagement loop and creates a new behavioral data stream tied to credit posture, not just travel intent. (Upgraded Points)
  • Canada, watch item: AIR MILES completes its transition to BMO's Blue Rewards this summer, collapsing Cash and Dream Miles into a single balance at 1,500 points equals $10, moving travel fulfillment onto Expedia Group, and adding Porter Airlines, Accor, Instacart and MTY as partners while Shell Canada exits to Scene+. What it signals: the Canadian coalition layer is being rebuilt around bank-owned currency with an OTA fulfillment backend. Coalition programs are becoming bank distribution assets, and Porter is quietly buying reach through someone else's coalition rather than scaling VIPorter alone. (Prince of Travel, Milesopedia)

#Hotel & travel loyalty

  • Hilton Honors: Program terms and conditions updated effective Jul 15, covering Yotel joining Honors, event planner earning, and points purchase rules. What it signals: brand-count expansion remains the cheapest way to grow a member graph, and every new brand added is more inventory and more attribute depth for a future media offer. (LoyaltyLobby)
  • Marriott Bonvoy: New "Exclusive For You" targeted member offers went live Jul 15, valid through Oct 31. What it signals: the same first-party segmentation Marriott sells to advertisers is being run against its own P&L first. Targeted internal offers are the proof-of-concept that de-risks selling the segments externally. (LoyaltyLobby)

#AdTech & retail media

  • Netflix, second-quarter results (Jul 16): Ad revenue tracking to roughly $3B in 2026, roughly double 2025. Management confirmed programmatic access to Pause Ads and live sports inventory opening this summer, with The Trade Desk, Amazon DSP and Google DV360 as partners. Amazon shopping audiences are already usable against Netflix inventory via Amazon DSP in the US and across EMEA. Shares fell about 8% on softer forward guidance. What it signals: commerce and loyalty audiences are now routable into premium CTV through standard DSP workflows. For a carrier, that means first-party segments have a credible high-attention destination beyond owned screens, which materially improves the business case for packaging loyalty data as addressable inventory. (PPC Land, eMarketer)

#Loyalty tech vendors

  • Plusgrade / Points (announced Jul 7): Launched Buy BreezePoints with Breeze Airways on its Loyalty Currency Retailing platform, extending an existing ancillary relationship that already covered Preferred Seating, Extra Legroom and Seat Blocker into loyalty currency sales. What it signals: the vendor land grab is happening at the currency layer, not the engine layer. Plusgrade is bundling ancillary and points retailing into one commercial relationship, which makes a later rip-and-replace of the loyalty stack considerably harder. Build-vs-buy read: buy the currency retailing, keep the member graph and identity resolution in house. (PR Newswire, Runway Girl Network)

#Strategic implication

The two biggest US carriers reported this week and the loyalty story in both was the credit card, not the media network. United, which owns the category's first-mover asset, still did not break Kinective out as a line item while its co-brand accounts hit a Q2 record. Meanwhile a mid-cap Australian retailer stood up a loyalty-fed media network in a week. The strategic question for a CDO or VP Digital at a major carrier is no longer whether to build a media network, it is whether the member graph is clean enough to be sold twice, once to a bank as co-brand remuneration and once to advertisers as addressable inventory, without the two propositions eroding each other. The forcing function is identity: with Amazon and Trade Desk audiences now routing into premium CTV as standard workflow, a carrier that cannot resolve its members to an addressable ID will end up renting reach from the same platforms it hoped to compete with.
