#Macro signal

Consumer confidence broke while input costs kept climbing: The University of Michigan sentiment index fell to 51 in early August from 55.2 in July, missing expectations of 54.5 and ending two months of recovery. Both components weakened, current conditions to 51.8 and expectations to 50.6, and the decline was broad-based across demographic and political groups, sharpest among older and lower-income consumers. Expectations for business conditions fell 11% over the next year and 17% over the longer horizon. The driver is energy: shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has effectively collapsed since the conflict began in late February, running between eight and fifteen vessel transits per day against roughly 130 before, against a waterway that carried about a fifth of global oil supply. WTI is holding near $87 after a second consecutive weekly gain of roughly 5%. The strategic read is that this is a supply-side shock arriving on top of a tired consumer, which is the combination that pricing power cannot solve. Leaders planning 2027 on a demand-elasticity assumption drawn from the last two years are working from the wrong base case. Build the plan against a consumer who is still transacting but has stopped feeling good about it, because those two states diverge before volume does.

#Tech industry

  • Bond volatility, not AI sentiment, set the tape: All three major US indexes posted weekly losses of roughly 2%, breaking three-week winning streaks for the S&P 500 and Nasdaq, as a Treasury sell-off pressured risk assets and a mid-week intervention bought only a day of relief. Friday recovered some ground on the strongest US business activity reading in more than four years, with the S&P 500 closing at 7,674.37 and the Nasdaq at 26,180.45. Strategic implication: the cost of capital is now the binding constraint on the AI buildout, not enthusiasm for it. Any multi-year investment case that clears only at low rates should be re-underwritten this quarter, not next.
  • NVIDIA reports August 26 into an $800 billion capex year: Consensus sits at $93 billion to $95 billion in quarterly revenue. The four largest hyperscalers spent a combined $166 billion on capital expenditure in the June quarter alone, up 87% year over year, with Amazon guiding to roughly $220 billion for 2026 and Alphabet raising to roughly $200 billion, both citing supply constraints. Strategic implication: a single earnings print now carries enough weight to move the index that most corporate treasuries and pension liabilities are benchmarked against. That concentration is itself the risk, and boards should know their indirect exposure before Wednesday rather than after.

#AI at the leadership level

The EU AI Act became enforceable on August 2, with the Commission's AI Office and national authorities now active. Transparency obligations bite first: AI systems must disclose that a user is interacting with a machine, generated or altered content must carry machine-readable marking, and deepfakes require labels. Penalties reach €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. High-risk system rules were pushed to December 2027 under the Omnibus amendments, and a voluntary Code of Practice on transparency offers a recognized compliance path.

The enforcement arrives into a governance vacuum. Adoption surveys put agentic AI in production at a large majority of enterprises while only about 21% report a mature governance model for agents, and only 8% of organizations globally claim a comprehensive AI governance framework. Sixty-seven percent of executives believe their company has already suffered a data leak through unapproved AI tools, and 35% admit they could not immediately shut down a rogue agent. Read together, these are not three separate findings. They describe an organization that has deployed a capability it cannot inventory, cannot label and cannot stop, into a jurisdiction that has started issuing fines as a percentage of global revenue. The board-level question is no longer whether to adopt, it is whether anyone can produce a current list of what is already running.

#Leadership & org design

Two organizations with identical AI tooling are now producing wildly different throughput, and the variable is structure rather than software. The pattern in teams pulling real output: cross-functional composition spanning product, marketing and technology, teams organized around a product rather than a function, decision authority held at the team, and approval gates removed rather than automated. The failure pattern is a functional silo where AI can execute a task in minutes but the work still takes months because it passes through sequential hand-offs and three or four review rounds. AI compresses the execution step, which means the queue and the approval chain become the entire cycle time. Meanwhile 95% of executives report that roles and team structures are changing because of AI, and 54% of C-suite leaders say adoption is tearing their company apart. The practical conclusion is that the highest-return AI investment available to most organizations this quarter is deleting approval steps, not buying another platform.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If one of our agents started making bad decisions this morning, who has the authority to stop it, how long would that take, and what would we tell a regulator about what it produced in the interim?

#Try this now

  • Agentic Context Engineering (ACE): A research pattern from Stanford, SambaNova and UC Berkeley that splits context management into three roles instead of one prompt. A Generator produces the reasoning trajectory, a Reflector distills what actually worked and what failed, and a Curator folds those lessons back into a structured playbook the agent reads next time. Reported gains of 10.6% on agent tasks and 8.6% on finance tasks with no model retraining. The 20 minutes: take your single worst-performing internal agent, add a reflection step that writes failures to a running playbook file, and feed that file back as context. Most agent failures are context failures, not model failures, and this is the cheapest way to prove that in your own stack.
  • Instrument token cost per outcome, not per call: Anthropic made Claude Sonnet 5 pricing permanent at $2 per million input and $10 per million output tokens on August 10, ending the expected September step-up to $3 and $15. The trap is assuming published rates predict spend. Reasoning tokens mean the number of tokens needed to hit a given outcome moves independently of the rate card. Pull one week of logs for your highest-volume AI workflow and compute dollars per completed task, not dollars per thousand calls. Most teams have never seen that number and it is the only one that survives a model swap.
  • HubSpot Agent Hub: Agent management moved from a side feature into a governed surface, with centralized control over which agents run and what a user sees. Worth 20 minutes even if you are not a HubSpot shop, because it is a preview of where every suite vendor is heading. Open it, look at what the permission model actually controls, then ask whether your own agent deployments have an equivalent kill switch.

#Case study worth stealing

Google, Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair and Target, the Universal Commerce Protocol: Rather than waiting for shopping agents to scrape their storefronts, this group co-developed a protocol that exposes discovery, cart, checkout and post-purchase as agent-callable endpoints. It is live alongside OpenAI and Stripe's Agentic Commerce Protocol, which already processes real transactions inside ChatGPT across Etsy and more than a million Shopify merchants. The gap in current behavior is the point: roughly 62% of commerce AI usage is product comparison, but only about 23% reaches checkout and 19% survives to post-purchase. The adaptable move is to stop optimizing for being described by an agent and start publishing a machine-readable feed of your actual purchasable inventory, with price, availability and fulfillment terms, so an agent can transact rather than merely summarize. Any org with a catalog, a fare, a rate or a plan can do this in a quarter.

#Framework or mental model

Inclusion is not selection: Being cited in an AI answer and being chosen by an AI agent are two different competitions with two different win conditions. Inclusion is won with content presence, structured data and citation-friendly authority. Selection is won with machine-readable commercial terms, real-time availability and a transaction endpoint an agent can complete without a human. Teams currently pour budget into the first and have no strategy for the second. Apply it by auditing your top ten revenue-driving pages twice, once asking whether an agent can describe this offer, and once asking whether an agent can buy it.

#Rabbit hole

Chicago Fed Letter no. 521 rebuilds the relationship between consumer sentiment and actual spending using a new composite index. Timely, because the University of Michigan index just fell to 51 while discretionary spending has not broken down in the same shape. If your demand forecasting still treats sentiment as a leading indicator of purchase behavior, this is an hour that will change how you weight it.

Signal: agent governance is becoming a bundled suite feature rather than a separate purchase.   • Anthropic pricing: Sonnet 5's introductory rate of $2 per million input and $10 per million output tokens became permanent on August 10, cancelling the planned September increase to $3 and $15. Signal: model pricing is now a martech cost-of-goods input, not a procurement footnote. Any vendor whose margin depends on inference is repricing quietly, and any buyer signing a multi-year AI-powered contract without a token pass-through clause is taking on someone else's cost risk.

#Category watch

Retail media networks: The category has moved from land grab to saturation, with trade coverage this week openly asking why there are so many networks and calling the moment RMN-mania. Every retailer, marketplace, airline and hotel chain with first-party data now has one, but the number of buyers with staff to operate dozens of separate self-serve consoles has not grown at the same rate. The next phase is not more networks, it is aggregation: demand consolidating into a small number of buying surfaces that normalize inventory and measurement across networks. Whoever owns that abstraction layer captures the margin, which is why the identity and clean room vendors are repositioning toward it.

#Stack intelligence

The build versus buy math changed when inference became a metered variable cost. Under the old model, buying meant a fixed license and building meant fixed engineering headcount, so the comparison was capex against capex. Now every intelligence layer running through a metered model carries a per-transaction cost that scales with usage, and most stacks were architected on the assumption that both the token rate and the tokens needed per outcome would stay flat. Neither did. The practical move before your next renewal cycle is to build a cost-per-outcome model for each AI-mediated workflow, then ask each vendor to state, in writing, who absorbs a model price change. Vendors who cannot answer are carrying risk they intend to pass to you.

#Foundations concept of the day

Retail media networks: A retail media network is an advertising business built on top of a company's own customer data and owned surfaces. The retailer knows who its customers are, what they browse and what they buy, and it sells brands the ability to reach those customers with ads on its site, its app, its email and increasingly on third-party inventory off its own property. It works because the retailer can close the loop: it sees the ad impression and the resulting purchase in the same system, which makes attribution far cleaner than open-web advertising, where the ad and the sale live in different databases. It matters at scale because the margin profile is unlike retail itself. Selling a product might return single-digit margin, while selling an ad against that product returns far more, so a network can become a disproportionate share of operating profit on a small share of revenue. Amazon Ads is the archetype and now a business measured in tens of billions. Walmart Connect proved a traditional retailer could copy it. The pattern then jumped verticals: United built Kinective Media on roughly 40 years of MileagePlus data and more than 100 million loyalty members, and hotel groups have followed with their own media arms. The through-line is that any business with authenticated, repeat, high-intent customers is sitting on undeployed ad inventory.

#Watch list

  • The agent commerce standards fight: Google's Universal Commerce Protocol, developed with Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair and Target, against OpenAI and Stripe's Agentic Commerce Protocol. Two competing definitions of how an agent transacts. The one that wins determines who sits between a brand and its customer at the moment of purchase, and neither answer is good for brands that have not published a machine-readable catalog.
  • LiveRamp's neutrality position: Following the $2.2 billion acquisition earlier this summer, the market reaction was muted, but a neutral identity layer that acquires its way up the stack eventually stops being neutral. Watch whether large advertisers begin dual-sourcing identity resolution as a hedge.

Signal: capacity remains sold before it is built, which means enterprise buyers should expect availability, not price, to be the negotiating variable in cloud AI contracts through 2027.

Stack insight

Every layer of this week's infrastructure news points at the same constraint, and it is not chips. Blackwell is sold out, capex is up 87% year over year, and the money is flowing upstream into power generation and grid position because that is where the queue actually forms. Compute you can order. Electricity at that scale you have to wait for, and the interconnection timelines are measured in years while model release cycles are measured in weeks. The twelve to eighteen month implication is that inference cost curves will flatten sooner than the current pricing trend suggests, not because models stop improving but because the marginal watt gets more expensive. Anthropic locking Sonnet 5 pricing permanently reads differently in that light: it is a commitment made while power is still cheap enough to honor it. Plan AI-dependent product economics on the assumption that today's token prices are the floor, not a waypoint on the way down.

#M&A signal

Seismic and Highspot closing on August 18 is the week's clearest consolidation event, and it is the second-order kind that matters more than a headline acquisition. Two direct competitors in sales enablement merged rather than one buying a capability, which is what happens when a category concludes it cannot grow into a platform on its own and its buyers have started asking why it is a separate line item. Paired with LiveRamp's post-acquisition neutrality question and HubSpot bundling agent governance, the through-line is that the middle of the martech stack is collapsing into the suites. The vendors surviving as independents will be the ones holding either identity or a governed content and permission layer, because those are the two things an agent-driven stack cannot safely improvise.

#The week's big bet

The week's defining move was not an acquisition or a model release. It was NVIDIA putting $3 billion into Lancium to tie compute directly to power generation, in the same week that hyperscaler capex printed at $166 billion for a single quarter and the Department of Energy projected data centers consuming up to 12% of US electricity by 2028. That is the AI industry conceding that its growth constraint is now physical infrastructure with multi-year lead times, not chip fabrication or capital. Everything downstream reprices off that: inference costs stop falling on the current curve, model providers who locked pricing did so while the input was still cheap, and enterprises negotiating cloud AI contracts will find availability rather than price is the scarce variable through 2027. For digital leaders in travel and MarTech, the implication is uncomfortably concrete. Any 2027 plan that assumes today's per-outcome AI cost declines by another order of magnitude is planning against a supply chain that has just told you it cannot deliver that. Build the business case at current token prices, instrument cost per completed task now, and treat cheap inference as an upside case rather than the base case. The teams that priced their AI roadmap on falling costs will be the ones defending it in a year when fuel is above $100 and the consumer is printing 51.

#The convergence watch (loyalty data → media networks)

No new airline or hotel media network launched in the last seven days, but the trend line moved in a more important direction: the existing networks started buying and selling from each other rather than only selling to endemic brands.

Marriott Media, now past its first year since the June 2025 launch, has expanded its media partnership with United Airlines and broadened its personalization offering for advertisers. That partnership is the tell. Marriott is monetizing roughly 237 million Bonvoy members, more than 200 targetable attributes, nearly 9,500 properties and close to a million in-room TV screens across the US and Canada. United is monetizing about 40 years of MileagePlus behavior across an app, a seatback fleet and a loyalty base above 100 million through Kinective Media. When two travel media networks partner, they are not sharing audiences out of generosity. They are building a combined travel-intent graph that neither could assemble alone, and they are doing it without a third-party identity vendor in the middle.

That is the strategic shape of the next eighteen months. Phase one was every travel brand launching its own network. Phase two, now visible, is those networks federating into a travel media category that can sell against Amazon and Walmart on intent quality rather than on scale. A carrier that has not launched a network yet is no longer late to phase one, it is at risk of being locked out of phase two, because the federation partners are being chosen now.

The economic urgency is arriving at the same moment. IATA has industry fuel spend rising toward $350 billion in 2026 from $252 billion in 2025, with industry profit halving to roughly $23 billion. Jet-A averaged $8.31 a gallon in August, up 70 cents from July. In a year when the core business margin is cut in half, the loyalty data business is the only line on an airline P&L with software economics. Expect boards that deferred this in 2024 and 2025 to fund it in the next planning cycle, and expect the resulting rush to compress the window in which a differentiated position is still available.

#MarTech

Relevance to loyalty stacks: the defensible asset in that deal is content governance, not the agents. Any loyalty program generating personalized offers at member scale with AI faces the identical problem, which is proving after the fact which offer went to which member under which approval. Governance is the part of the stack to buy, not build.
  • HubSpot, Agent Hub: Centralized agent management with granular visibility controls. Relevance: agent governance is becoming a bundled suite capability, which means the window for standalone agent-ops purchases in a loyalty marketing stack is closing before most programs have made one.
  • Data trust as the constraint: Survey work published this week found only 49% of B2B marketers fully trust the accuracy and completeness of the data shaping their budgets. Relevance: loyalty programs are usually the cleanest first-party data asset in a travel enterprise, and that relative advantage is worth more in a market where everyone else is discovering their data is not decision-grade. It is also the argument that wins internal funding for the media network case.

#AdTech & retail media

  • Retail media network proliferation: Trade coverage this week questioned the sheer number of networks now in market. Relevance to the convergence thesis: the ceiling on standalone networks is buyer operating capacity, not inventory. No agency will staff dozens of separate consoles. This is exactly why the Marriott and United partnership matters, and why the winning travel networks will be the ones that aggregate rather than the ones that merely exist.
  • Agent commerce standards: Google's Universal Commerce Protocol, developed with Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair and Target, is now competing with OpenAI and Stripe's Agentic Commerce Protocol, which already processes live transactions across Etsy and more than a million Shopify merchants. Relevance: whoever defines how an agent transacts sits between a loyalty program and its member at the point of purchase. A program whose fares, rates and award inventory are not machine-readable and transactable will be described by agents and bought around. Current commerce AI behavior shows roughly 62% of usage at comparison, only about 23% reaching checkout, which is the window still open.
  • LiveRamp: Neutrality questions persist following the $2.2 billion acquisition, with client reaction so far muted. Relevance: any loyalty media network leaning on a third-party identity spine should be pricing the scenario where that spine develops its own commercial interests. The federation model that Marriott and United are testing routes around this problem entirely.

#Loyalty tech vendors

  • Eagle Eye: Won the Wakefern contract for AI-driven real-time loyalty and personalization on its AIR platform, going live mid-2026, its fourth North American win of the fiscal year. Build-versus-buy signal: real-time offer decisioning at grocery transaction volume is now a proven buy, and the reference architecture is being validated in a vertical with far higher transaction frequency than travel. If it holds at that volume, the build case in travel gets harder to defend.
  • Plusgrade and Points: Continued expansion of loyalty currency retailing, including buy, gift and reinstate capabilities launched for China Airlines Dynasty Flyer. Build-versus-buy signal: points commerce is settled infrastructure, not differentiation. The differentiation moved to what sits on top, which is the offer decisioning and the media layer.

#Strategic implication

The single move that matters this week is Marriott and United partnering rather than competing. It marks the transition from every travel brand launching its own media network to those networks federating into a category that can credibly sell travel intent against Amazon and Walmart. Federation partners are being selected now, and the pool of attractive ones is small. Meanwhile the economics are forcing the issue from the other side: with industry profit halving on fuel and Jet-A at $8.31 a gallon, the loyalty data business is the only part of an airline P&L still earning software margins, which means capital that was hard to get for this in 2024 will be easier to get in the next planning cycle and the competitive window will close faster than the build timeline. The question for a CDO or VP Digital at a carrier this week is not whether to build a media network. It is which loyalty and travel partners you would want in a federated intent graph, whether anyone has started that conversation, and whether your first-party data is structured well enough to be worth joining. The second question follows from the first: if your fares and award inventory are not machine-readable and transactable today, the agent commerce layer will route around your program regardless of how good the media network turns out to be.
