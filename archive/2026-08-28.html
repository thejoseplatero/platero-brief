Executive Pulse1,056 words

#Macro signal

The Fed chair's first Jackson Hole address lands today, and the market is pricing a hike, not a cut: Kevin Warsh delivers his debut keynote as chair this morning at the Jackson Hole symposium, nineteen days before the September 16 meeting. Traders are pricing roughly a two-in-three chance rates hold and a one-in-three chance they rise. He has told reporters he wants the speech to frame structural questions rather than debate quarter-point moves, which is a signal in itself about how much guidance to expect. The strategic point for planning is that the entire 2026 corporate assumption set was built on a path of cuts, and that path has quietly inverted while attention was on AI capex. Anyone still carrying a 2027 plan that assumes cheaper capital should reprice it this week rather than in November. The second leg matters more in Canada. US tariffs of 50 percent on a broad range of Canadian goods took effect August 22, and Canada's matching response covering over 700 American goods at rates from 15 to 50 percent begins September 8. The Bank of Canada's projection has GDP roughly 1.5 percent lower by end of 2026 than its earlier baseline, with growth at 1.1 percent this year. A tightening bias meeting a scheduled escalation in trade costs is a genuinely difficult combination for anything discretionary, and travel and consumer-facing categories should be stress-testing demand assumptions against both at once rather than either alone.

#Tech industry

  • Salesforce posted a record quarter and used it to declare the AI-eats-software thesis dead: revenue of $11.35 billion, up 11 percent, with FY27 guidance raised to $46.1 to $46.4 billion. Agentforce ARR reached $1.5 billion, up more than 240 percent year over year, AI and data ARR approached $4 billion, and agentic work units hit 3.2 billion in the quarter, up 97 percent sequentially. The CEO's framing was blunt, calling the SaaSpocalypse narrative nonsense and pointing at seat growth, near-record-low attrition and lengthening contracts. The stock rose 8.7 percent Thursday. Strategic implication: the counter-evidence offered is real but it is one quarter of a lagging indicator. Seats and attrition move slowly by construction, so they are the last place a business model shift would appear. The more interesting evidence sits in the same day's announcement, where the company shipped a supported way for its users to work without opening its interface. Both things are true, and the honest read is that the incumbents are hedging the interface while defending the seat.
  • Nvidia's quarter reset the AI capex debate and narrowed it at the same time: revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106 percent year over year, with data center at $89 billion, up 117 percent, now 92 percent of total sales. Guidance of $108 billion for the current quarter came in ahead of the roughly $104 billion consensus and explicitly assumes no data center revenue from China. Shares rose about 7 percent Thursday after an initially negative after-hours reaction. Strategic implication: a company at this scale deriving 92 percent of revenue from one segment, guiding above consensus while writing off an entire geography, is describing a demand curve that is steep and concentrated. For anyone building a capacity or cost plan around AI, the useful read is that supply is being allocated by relationship and power access rather than by price, and that a procurement strategy built on expecting price relief is likely to be wrong for at least another year.

#AI at the leadership level

More than a hundred companies, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, CrowdStrike, Okta and Fortinet, signed an open letter yesterday warning that AI-enabled cyber attacks will become far more widespread and sophisticated, and calling for coordinated defensive action across local, national and international levels. The letter names critical infrastructure specifically, hospitals, water treatment facilities and internet systems, and references recent incidents in which AI agents autonomously breached companies. Two things deserve board attention. First, the substance is credible and the incidents cited are documented, so the risk framing is not marketing. Second, several signatories both build the frontier models creating this exposure and sell the defensive products against it, which is a structural conflict that should shape how their recommendations are weighted rather than whether they are heard. Alongside this, the EU AI Act's transparency obligations became enforceable on August 2, requiring interactive AI systems to disclose that they are AI and generated content to be labelled. The AI Office can now demand information and model access, though it has brought no action yet. The high-risk tier has been deferred to December 2027 for standalone systems, which means the compliance work available to do right now is disclosure design, and the quiet window to do it well is open.

#Leadership & org design

Validity's State of CRM Data 2026, from a July survey of 500 marketing professionals, found that 62 percent have presented or acted on an AI recommendation they later suspected was flawed because of the data underneath it. The distribution is the org design finding. Individual contributors, 41 percent. C-suite, 78 percent. SVPs and VPs, 92 percent. Authority to act on AI output and ability to verify it are inversely correlated, and the org chart guarantees it, because seniority is defined by distance from the source system. This is not a training problem and it will not be solved by AI literacy programs, which target the wrong variable. It is a routing problem. The practical fix is structural: any AI-derived figure entering a decision forum arrives with its source query attached, and the person accountable for the underlying data, not the person presenting the slide, gets asked whether the number holds this week. Meanwhile only 21 percent of the same respondents believe their CRM data is ready for AI, and 45 percent already run agents acting without human oversight. Those two numbers describe most organizations in this market.

#Question worth asking your team this week

Of the AI decisions we let run without a human in the loop, which ones read from a field that no one on this call can name an owner for.

Practitioner Pulse1,121 words

#Try this now

  • Measure data readiness on the three fields your agents actually read, not on the whole CRM: Validity's State of CRM Data 2026, out this week from a July survey of 500 marketing professionals across the US, UK, Brazil, Australia and New Zealand, found that 91 percent say data readiness matters for AI adoption while only 21 percent believe their own CRM data is ready. Meanwhile 45 percent already run agentic AI that acts without human oversight, and only 26 percent say more than three quarters of their CRM records are accurate and complete. The trap is that "clean the CRM" is an unbounded project nobody finishes. The bounded version takes an afternoon. List the agents or automations currently live in your function, open each one's configuration, and write down the specific fields it reads to make a decision. Usually it is four to eight fields, not four hundred. Measure completeness and last-updated age on those alone, and fix only those. Everything else in the CRM is a separate problem that is not currently making decisions on your behalf.
  • Check who on your team is most likely to act on a bad AI recommendation, because it is probably the most senior person: The same survey found 62 percent of respondents have presented or acted on an AI recommendation they later suspected was flawed because of the data underneath it. The distribution is the finding. Among individual contributors it was 41 percent. Among C-suite executives, 78 percent. Among SVPs and VPs, 92 percent. Seniority correlates with acting on output you cannot personally verify, which makes sense, because seniority is distance from the source data and proximity to the decision. Two cheap countermeasures. Require any AI-sourced number entering a leadership deck to carry the query or the source table beside it, so the reader can see what was counted. And ask the person who owns the underlying data, not the person presenting, whether the number is trustworthy this week.
  • Audit your Meta placements against the changelog, not against the campaign manager: Meta's Marketing API v26.0 removed the Messenger Stories placement, and the deprecation date is genuinely inconsistent across Meta's own documentation. The Graph API changelog says October 27, the Marketing API changelog points at late August 2026, and advertiser notifications reportedly cite August 27. That is yesterday. If you or your agency run Advantage+ or any automated placement expansion, this is the failure mode where nothing errors, spend quietly redistributes, and the performance change surfaces in a monthly review three weeks later. Pull your placement-level delivery report for the last five days and compare it to the five before. Twenty minutes, and it is the kind of check that only ever gets done after someone has already lost a quarter to it.

#Case study worth stealing

Hill's Pet Nutrition, fixing reach arithmetic before fixing media mix: Hill's is the named early adopter of LiveRamp's expansion of Cross-Media Intelligence to accept Meta campaign data, which puts social performance in the same de-duplicated report as CTV, programmatic and audio inside a clean room. The tactic worth stealing has nothing to do with the vendor. It is the recognition that adding platform dashboards together produces a reach number that is arithmetically wrong, because the same person counted once as Meta-exposed and again as CTV-exposed inflates total reach and deflates measured frequency at the same time. Most large advertisers have been budgeting against that inflated number for years, which means incremental reach on a new channel has been systematically overstated and the frequency someone is actually absorbing has been systematically understated. The adaptable version for any team without a clean room budget: take last quarter's channel reach figures, add them, and compare the sum against your addressable population. If the sum exceeds the population, you already know your planning inputs are wrong and by roughly how much. That single comparison is usually enough to change where the next dollar goes. Worth noting honestly that the announcement disclosed no pricing, no customer count, no performance metrics, and did not specify which Meta surfaces are covered or what the reporting latency is, so the capability is real and the proof is not yet public.

#Framework or mental model

The interchangeable middle: MIT's widely cited 2026 finding that 95 percent of enterprise generative AI pilots fail to deliver measurable P&L impact is usually quoted as an indictment of the technology. The more useful number sits underneath it: in 42 percent of the implementations studied, the model itself was fully interchangeable. Swapping the vendor changed nothing about the outcome. What separated the 5 percent was not model quality, budget or technical talent. It was the orchestration layer, the proprietary data feeding it, and the organization around it. The same study found that buying from specialized vendors and building partnerships succeeded roughly 67 percent of the time, while internal builds succeeded about one third as often. Two caveats keep this honest. The 95 percent measured whether a pilot produced rapid P&L impact inside six months, and it sampled heavily in sales and marketing, which was the lowest-ROI area in the study. So it is a statement about a specific bar, not about the technology. Where this applies: it is a sorting rule for AI investment. Anything that lives in the interchangeable middle, the model choice, the prompt library, the wrapper UI, is a rental decision and should be made quickly and reversibly. Anything that touches the orchestration layer, the data you own, or how decisions get approved, is where the durable advantage sits and deserves the internal build effort that is currently being spent arguing about vendors.

#Rabbit hole

The EU AI Act's transparency obligations became enforceable on August 2 and this is the first month anyone has had to live with them. Interactive AI systems must disclose to users that they are AI, and generated or altered content must be labelled. The AI Office and national authorities can now request information from companies and request access to models, though no enforcement action has been brought yet. The Digital Omnibus, in force since July 27, pushed the high-risk tier out to December 2027 for standalone systems and August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products, which means the near-term obligations are disclosure obligations. Worth an hour for anyone shipping a conversational surface, a generated-content pipeline or a personalization feature into Europe, because disclosure is a design problem before it is a legal one, and the design decision of where and how the disclosure appears is currently being made by default in most products.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Salesforce and Anthropic: Claudeforce was announced alongside Salesforce's Q2 FY27 earnings. The centrepiece is Salesforce in Claude, a plugin that brings CRM data and actions into Claude with 37 prebuilt sales skills covering meeting prep, deal health review and pipeline review, governed by the permissions and business rules already configured in Salesforce. Onboarding reads a seller's connected context across Salesforce, Slack and other Claude connectors and generates a tailored dashboard. It is live for pilot customers with open beta planned for September. Signal: the largest system of record in enterprise software just shipped a supported path for its users to stop opening it. That is a deliberate concession of the interface layer in exchange for keeping the data layer and the permission model, and it is the clearest statement yet of where incumbents think the defensible position sits. The pricing question nobody has answered follows immediately: when the seat is no longer where the work happens, what is the seat for. Salesforce's own answer this quarter was that seats grew anyway and attrition sat near record lows, which is evidence for one quarter, not a resolution.
  • Integrate acquires CaliberMind: A universal lead management and data governance platform bought a GTM intelligence platform carrying multi-touch attribution, buyer journey analytics, marketing mix modeling, account scoring and revenue intelligence. Terms undisclosed. CaliberMind stays a separate product line with its existing team, with native integrations arriving over the next few months. Signal: measurement is being absorbed into activation. The stated problem is that campaigns launch and leads route in one system while attribution and MMM happen in another, weeks later. Buying the measurement layer rather than integrating with it is the third such move in a month, after Seismic closed its Highspot merger on August 18 to form a 2,500-customer, 3.5 million-user go-to-market platform. The B2B stack is consolidating around whoever can close the loop from spend to revenue inside one system rather than across three.
  • Meta Marketing API v26.0: The Messenger Stories placement was removed, and Meta's own documentation disagrees on when it stops serving everywhere. The Graph API changelog says October 27, the Marketing API changelog indicates late August, and advertiser notifications reportedly cite August 27. Signal: minor on its own, structurally relevant because automated placement expansion means a deprecated placement does not throw an error, it silently reallocates budget. Every media plan now carries an undocumented dependency on a vendor changelog that the people running the plan do not read.

#Category watch

Cross-media measurement and clean rooms: The category is moving from "can we collaborate on data privately" to "can we produce one de-duplicated number across walled gardens." LiveRamp's expansion of Cross-Media Intelligence to ingest Meta campaign data, with Hill's Pet Nutrition as the named early adopter, puts social into the same de-duplicated report as CTV, programmatic and audio. The gap this fills is arithmetic rather than technical: summing platform dashboards double-counts people who saw the same campaign in two places, which inflates reach and understates real frequency. On the retail media side, fewer than half of US retail media networks currently offer clean room capability at all, which is the growth headroom and also the reason most retail media measurement claims cannot be independently verified today. Where this heads: clean rooms stop being a privacy product and become a measurement product, and the buying criterion shifts from "who can we safely share with" to "whose de-duplicated number will our CFO accept."

#Stack intelligence

The buy versus build question now has a number attached to it, and it is unflattering to internal builds. MIT's 2026 enterprise study found that purchasing from specialized vendors and forming partnerships succeeded roughly 67 percent of the time, while internal builds succeeded about one third as often. In 42 percent of implementations studied, the model itself was fully interchangeable, meaning the vendor choice at the model layer was not what determined the outcome. Read alongside this week's Validity survey, where 91 percent of marketers say data readiness matters for AI and only 21 percent believe their own CRM data is ready while 45 percent already run agents acting without human oversight, the pattern for anyone building a martech org is specific. The model layer is a rental and should be procured fast and swapped freely. The orchestration layer, the governed data underneath it, and the approval structure around it are the build, and they are where the 5 percent that produced measurable P&L impact actually spent their effort. Most stacks currently have this inverted, with a long vendor evaluation at the model layer and no owner at all for orchestration.

#Foundations concept of the day

De-duplicated reach and frequency: Reach is the count of distinct people who saw a campaign at least once. Frequency is the average number of times each of those people saw it. Both are trivially easy to state and surprisingly hard to compute, because a campaign runs across platforms that each measure only their own inventory. Meta knows who saw it on Meta. A CTV platform knows who saw it on connected TV. Neither knows about the other, so each reports its own reach honestly, and when a planner adds the two together the same person is counted twice. The error compounds in a specific direction: total reach goes up, and because frequency is impressions divided by reach, measured frequency goes down. So the planner believes the campaign reached more people less often than it actually did. De-duplication is the process of resolving those separate exposure logs against a common identity spine so each person is counted once regardless of how many platforms saw them. Mechanically this happens in a clean room, a governed compute environment where two parties can join data without either party receiving the other's raw records. The advertiser contributes its first-party customer file, each platform contributes exposure logs keyed to a shared pseudonymous identifier, the join happens inside the environment, and only aggregate outputs leave. Why this matters at scale: two of the most consequential planning decisions in advertising depend on this number being right. Incremental reach determines whether a new channel earns budget, and it is measured as the people a channel adds who were not already reached elsewhere. If the base is inflated, incremental reach is understated and new channels get systematically under-invested. Frequency capping determines how often a person is exposed before spend becomes waste or irritation, and if measured frequency is understated, the cap is set too high and real people absorb far more exposure than intended. Cross-industry example: a bank running acquisition across search, social, CTV and podcast finds that its four dashboards sum to more distinct adults than live in its target provinces. That impossible number is not a vendor lying, it is four correct answers to four different questions being added as though they answered one. The fix is not a better dashboard, it is a shared identity spine and a place to join on it, which is why every large advertiser eventually ends up building or renting one.

#Watch list

  • Attribution vendors as acquisition targets: the Integrate and CaliberMind deal follows the logic that measurement standing alone is a feature, not a company. Any independent multi-touch attribution or MMM vendor with real enterprise logos is now priced as a strategic asset for whoever owns activation.
  • Whether Claudeforce ships with usage-based pricing at open beta in September: this is the tell for how seriously incumbents believe the interface is moving. If the seat price holds and the plugin is bundled, the interface concession is defensive positioning. If a consumption meter appears on the plugin, the seat business is being actively repriced.

#Macro pulse

Canada: The S&P/TSX composite closed Thursday at 36,834.25, up 20.60 points, effectively flat while US indexes rallied. Financials weighed as the big banks finished third-quarter earnings, with capital markets divisions driving profits and elevated loan provisions the line item worth watching. Bank executives described tariff exposure as manageable while acknowledging consumer and sector-specific uncertainty. The loonie firmed slightly to 72.14 cents US from 72.07, putting USD/CAD near 1.386. That is a move of less than a tenth of a percent, so no material change to the currency translation on international revenue, but the level itself remains the story. A Canadian dollar in the low 72s keeps US-denominated costs, aircraft leases, fuel and technology contracts, expensive against domestic fare revenue.

United States: The S&P 500 rose 55.29 points, or 0.7 percent, to 7,730.99. The Nasdaq composite gained 411.16 points, or 1.6 percent, to 26,541.35. The breadth is the number that matters more than the direction: technology was the only S&P 500 sector to advance. Nvidia rose about 7 percent on its earnings print and Salesforce climbed 8.7 percent on its own. Attention now turns entirely to the Fed chair's first Jackson Hole keynote this morning, with traders pricing roughly a two-in-three chance of a hold and a one-in-three chance of a September hike.

Asia: The Nikkei 225 closed at 66,665.64, up 0.81 percent. The Hang Seng erased an opening dip to finish about 0.3 percent higher, with offshore flows favouring Hong Kong. The Shanghai Composite slipped 0.11 percent to 3,952. Korean chip names remained a drag on the regional session.

Oil and commodities: October WTI crude settled at $83.53 a barrel, up $1.30, a gain of roughly 1.6 percent. That is just below the flag threshold on its own, but crude in the mid-80s sustained through the fall booking season is a real margin question for carriers that have not hedged forward. December gold settled at $4,664.00 an ounce, up $10.70.

#Today's industry move

Salesforce delivered its strongest quarter in four years and paired it with a partnership announcement that cuts against its own interface. Revenue of $11.35 billion, up 11 percent, beat the roughly $11.32 billion consensus, and FY27 guidance was raised to $46.1 to $46.4 billion including a $300 million constant-currency raise. Agentforce ARR hit $1.5 billion, up more than 240 percent year over year, with AI and data ARR approaching $4 billion and accounts running agents in production up 70 percent sequentially. Agentic work units reached 3.2 billion for the quarter, up 97 percent. Net new average order value growth was the strongest in four years and attrition sat near record lows. The stock rose 8.7 percent Thursday. Announced the same day, Claudeforce puts Salesforce data and 37 prebuilt sales skills inside Claude, live for pilot customers with open beta planned for September. The market read the earnings as a refutation of the thesis that AI erodes seat-based software. The product announcement is the same company hedging that thesis in public.

#AI infrastructure watch

Nvidia's Q2 print is the week's infrastructure event and the concentration in it is the signal. Revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106 percent year over year, with data center at $89 billion, up 117 percent, now representing 92 percent of total company sales. Guidance of $108 billion for the current quarter came in above the roughly $104 billion consensus and explicitly assumes zero data center revenue from China. Underneath the chips, the power layer keeps repricing. Constellation's CEO has said projected 2026 hyperscaler spending runs nearly 75 percent above last year and continues to be revised upward. NextEra put a conversion rate on it, telling investors that every gigawatt of large load under its approved Florida tariff represents roughly $2 billion of capex. TECfusions brought its Kensington data center live yesterday with GPU capacity and an on-site power plan, which is the pattern worth tracking: new capacity is increasingly announced with its generation story attached, because interconnection queue position has become the binding constraint rather than land or silicon.

#Signal or noise?

Signal on the AI capex trajectory, noise on market breadth. A session where one sector advances and the other ten do not is a repricing of a single thesis, not a broad risk-on move, and the Canadian tape confirmed it by finishing flat.

#Watch today

The Fed chair's Jackson Hole keynote this morning. He has signalled he wants to frame structural questions rather than guide on the next move, which means the market may extract less from it than the positioning implies. The asymmetry sits in any language about inflation persistence, because September hike odds near one in three leave room to move in both directions on a single sentence.
