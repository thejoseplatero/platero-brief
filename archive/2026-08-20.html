Executive Pulse1,100 words

#Macro signal

The consumer split, priced in one earnings report: Walmart reported Q2 FY27 before the open Thursday. Revenue of $187.9 billion, up 5.9 percent. Adjusted EPS of $0.81 against $0.74 expected. US comparable sales up 2.6 percent, global ecommerce up 23 percent, operating income up 28.8 percent to $2.1 billion with help from tariff refunds. Full year guidance was set at 4.0 to 5.0 percent net sales growth and 7.0 to 8.5 percent adjusted operating income growth. Read against July retail sales, which unexpectedly fell 0.6 percent, the steepest decline since May 2025, the two data points do not contradict each other. They describe a bifurcation. Higher income households are still spending across categories while budget constrained households are trading down, and the retailer positioned to capture both sides of that trade posts a beat in the same month the aggregate number falls. The strategic implication is that the headline consumer figure has stopped being a usable planning input. Any forecast, pricing model, or demand elasticity assumption that runs on a single blended consumer will be wrong for at least one half of the base, and probably wrong in opposite directions. The organizations that will plan well through the next two quarters are the ones already segmenting demand by ability to pay rather than by product line.

#Tech industry

  • Consolidation is now peer to peer, not exit to platform: Seismic completed its merger with Highspot on Tuesday, combining the two leading sales enablement vendors into roughly 2,500 customers and 3.5 million users under Permira's continued control. Earlier this month Nielsen agreed to acquire DoubleVerify for $2.15 billion in cash, folding independent ad verification into the incumbent audience measurement business, with close targeted for Q1 2027 and a combined entity serving clients representing more than $300 billion in advertising spend. Strategic implication: two category leaders merging with each other, rather than one being bought by a larger platform, is the signal that the strategic acquirer did not show up. For any executive whose vendor is in a maturing software category, the practical exposure is that the roadmap you bought is now subject to a rationalization exercise, and the useful question at renewal is not what is coming next but which of the two overlapping products your account is being migrated onto.
  • The measurement layer is concentrating at exactly the wrong moment: The Nielsen and DoubleVerify combination puts audience measurement and independent verification under one P&L just as AI systems take over placement decisions at volumes no human reviews. Strategic implication: independence in measurement was always structural rather than ethical, valuable precisely because the party counting was not the party being paid. When the machine picks the inventory and the grader and the graded share a parent, the organization buying the media is the only remaining independent check, which means the capability has to exist in house or it does not exist.

#AI at the leadership level

The UK Information Commissioner's Office issued 107 recommendations to five police forces on Wednesday following an audit of their facial recognition deployments, and the finding worth a board's attention is not the count. Compliance was materially stronger for live deployments than for retrospective image searches, with the gaps concentrated in oversight, training, and data controls on the secondary use. Landing the same week: a settlement in Bloomington, Minnesota with a man wrongly arrested after a facial recognition misidentification despite home surveillance footage placing him at his residence, and a federal case where a submitted photograph returned at least a dozen matches from public web sources, some allegedly of the wrong people. The generalizable lesson for any organization deploying automated decisioning, identity resolution, or biometric verification is that governance attaches to the flagship use case because it is visible and someone owns it, while the error rate lives in the ad hoc secondary use that inherited the system without inheriting the controls. This is the same structural point the EU AI Act's transparency obligations, enforceable since 2 August with penalties up to 15 million euros or 3 percent of worldwide turnover, are approaching from the regulatory side. The board question is not whether the AI system was approved. It is who else is now using it, under what authority, and whether anyone would notice if they were wrong.

#Leadership & org design

Two moves this month describe the same reorganization from opposite ends, and most organizations are executing only one of them. Walmart cut roughly 1,000 corporate roles in a reorganization led by global technology head Suresh Kumar, targeting overlapping roles created in part by integrating AI focused product units into existing teams. That is the subtraction half: AI made some structure redundant, so the structure comes out. The addition half was described in a piece published Wednesday by Melissa Reeve, who diagnoses why the productivity rarely compounds. Frontline staff, often non technical people closest to the messy work, are the heaviest AI users and develop genuinely valuable practice, a nine page creative brief prompt used daily by one marketer, which nobody else in the company has. Enablement runs one way, from a central team outward through training and playbooks, so every lesson learned at the edge stays at the edge and the organization pays for it repeatedly. Her proposed structure is a flywheel rather than a program, spark to spread to scale to sustain, held up by three unglamorous mechanisms: a named role whose explicit job includes noticing when someone mentions a workaround in passing, a formal landing zone where a noticed practice gets captured, evaluated, refined, and standardized, and horizontal pathways so a solution found in one function reaches the other function facing the identical problem. The practical read for an executive is that cutting the overlap is the easy half and it is measurable in the quarter it happens, while building the noticing and spreading machinery is the half that determines whether the remaining headcount actually gets more capable or simply gets busier.

#Question worth asking your team this week

Name the most useful thing anyone on this team has figured out about working with AI in the last month, and tell me how many other people in the company now have it. If you can name the thing but not the number, we have a spreading problem and it is a process gap we can close. If you cannot name the thing at all, we have a noticing problem, which means the practice is already here and we are structurally unable to see it, and no amount of additional training budget will fix that.

Practitioner Pulse1,199 words

#Try this now

  • Split your AI visibility work by engine, because the engines do not agree: A Steady Demand study published Thursday analyzed 14,472 citations across 1,487 local service queries in 50 US metros and 10 categories, running identical prompts in Gemini and ChatGPT. The numbers make a blended AEO program indefensible. Gemini pulls roughly 60 percent of its local citations from business websites, with Reddit second at 13.7 percent, ahead of established directories like Angi and Thumbtack. ChatGPT leans the other way, on Reddit and directories. The two engines cited the same domains only 8 percent of the time and recommended the same top business 4.2 percent of the time. Instability inside a single engine is worse than the gap between them: repeated identical Gemini queries returned overlapping sources about 40 percent of the time, against roughly 90 percent consistency in Google's classic local pack, and Gemini named the same top business on just 7 percent of repeat runs. The practical move this week is to stop funding one AI visibility workstream and split it in two. The Gemini track is your own site: structured, current, complete product and location data on pages you control. The ChatGPT track is third-party surface area you do not own, which is a community and review operation, not a content operation. Same budget, two different teams, two different success measures.
  • Run the four-stage agent audit on one real product page: A framework published Wednesday by the IAB's Angelina Eng separates four things most teams collapse into one metric. Visibility is appearing in the answer. Consideration is being represented accurately enough to make a shortlist. Selection is meeting the decision criteria to be the recommendation. Commercial outcome is the booking or purchase actually completing. Pick one product or route page and score it against her three questions. Is the information current, complete, and consistent everywhere it appears, including the places you do not control. Is it verifiable, meaning reviews, ratings, and photos corroborate the claim rather than contradict it. Is it actionable, meaning a customer can complete the transaction without a workaround or a fee that appears at step four. This takes an afternoon and it usually fails at question two, because the claim and the reality diverged and nobody owned the gap. Agents compare your claim to independent sources by default, so a mismatch is not a messaging problem you can rewrite around.
  • Time your capture prompt by observed intent instead of a fixed timer: Attentive shipped AI Grow on Wednesday, which decides when to show an email or SMS sign-up prompt based on live browsing behavior rather than a set delay or scroll depth. Reported medians are a 25 percent lift in sign-ups and 35 percent in welcome series revenue. You do not need the product to test the principle, and testing it is the point. Almost every capture prompt in production fires on a timer or a scroll threshold that someone chose in a meeting years ago and nobody has revisited. Run one experiment where the trigger is a behavioral signal you already collect, second category viewed, return visit inside seven days, price filter applied, against your current fixed rule. If a rule that arbitrary is beatable, it tells you how many other timing rules in your stack were also set once and never contested.

#Case study worth stealing

Walmart, growing the media business sixteen times faster than the store: Q2 FY27 results landed Thursday morning. Revenue of $187.9 billion, up 5.9 percent, adjusted EPS of $0.81 against $0.74 expected, US comparable sales up 2.6 percent, global ecommerce up 23 percent. The number worth studying is Walmart Connect in the US, up 43 percent excluding VIZIO, with global advertising up 38 percent. The retail business grew 2.6 percent. The advertising business attached to it grew 43 percent, on data the company already had for operating reasons. Two things are worth stealing and one is worth a warning. First, the asset was never the audience, it was the closed loop: Walmart can show an advertiser the exposure and the resulting transaction in the same ledger, which nobody else in the chain can do, and that provable link is the whole product. Second, the precondition is organizational rather than technical, since the transaction log, the identity resolution, and the consent posture usually already exist and the missing piece is a commercial team willing to sell measurement an advertiser will renew against. Any business with authenticated customers and repeat transactions, and that includes carriers, banks, telcos, and travel platforms, is sitting on the same structure. The warning: operating income rose 28.8 percent to $2.1 billion with help from tariff refunds, so read the profit line and the media line separately before quoting either in a business case.

#Framework or mental model

Contribution instead of attribution: A. Lee Judge published a reframing on Thursday that is worth adopting for the failure mode it names. Attribution asks which touch deserves the credit and answers with pixels, cookies, and UTM strings. Its characteristic failure is being confidently wrong, producing a clean chart from a broken chain. Contribution asks a different question: was marketing present and useful at each decision point the buyer actually passed through, and did the sales team put it to work in live deals. Its inputs are sales conversations, what customers say they used, and observed content usage, and its characteristic failure is being honestly directional, which is a materially better place to be wrong. The operating loop he proposes is small enough to start Monday: pick one rep and one deal stage, interview the rep for the questions customers actually asked, build the asset in the customer's words rather than the brand's, put it back in the field, then measure whether it got used. The reason this matters more now than last year is that assistant-referred demand arrives with no referrer and no click, so the touch-level chain is losing the data it was built on, while conversations with buyers are not going anywhere.

#Rabbit hole

Three items landed together in Wednesday's identity technology roundup and read as one story. The UK Information Commissioner's Office issued 107 recommendations to five police forces after auditing their facial recognition use, and the finding buried in it is the interesting one: compliance was stronger for live deployments than for retrospective image searches, with gaps in oversight, training, and data controls. Alongside it, a Minnesota case where a submitted photograph returned at least a dozen matches from public web and social sources, some allegedly depicting the wrong people, and a wrongful arrest settlement in Bloomington involving a man misidentified despite home surveillance footage placing him at his residence. The pattern generalizes past policing to anyone deploying identity resolution or automated decisioning. The flagship use case gets the governance, the training, and the audit trail, because it is visible and someone owns it. The secondary use, the retrospective lookup, the ad hoc match, the analyst running a query outside the designed workflow, gets none of it and is where the error rate actually lives. Worth an hour for anyone whose identity graph is used by more teams than commissioned it.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Seismic and Highspot: The merger closed Tuesday, uniting the two largest sales enablement vendors under the Seismic name, led by Seismic CEO Rob Tarkoff, with Highspot founder Robert Wahbe joining the board and Permira, Seismic's backer since 2020, remaining controlling shareholder. Combined footprint is roughly 2,500 customers and 3.5 million users, with the first roadmap detail promised at Seismic Shift in October. Signal: this is two category leaders merging with each other rather than either being absorbed by a CRM or a content platform, which is what consolidation looks like when a category stops growing and the exit to a larger platform is not on offer. Note the language: the combined company is positioning as "go-to-market performance," not sales enablement. A category rename at the moment of consolidation is usually an attempt to move from a shrinking budget line to a larger one, and the open question is whether the underlying data and process integration justifies the new label or whether buyers get two content repositories with one logo.
  • Zeotap: Launched a fully composable CDP as a native app on the Snowflake Marketplace, running identity resolution, audience segmentation, journey orchestration, and AI modeling entirely inside the customer's own Snowflake account, with more than 250 connectors at launch spanning ad platforms, ESPs, CRMs, retail media networks, and service tools, and a quoted time to value of eight weeks from connection to first deployment. Signal: the CDP is dissolving into the warehouse as a software category. Shipping as an app inside someone else's data plane means the vendor no longer holds the data, which removes the single largest procurement objection and simultaneously removes the vendor's main source of lock-in. Expect the differentiation fight to move up into identity resolution quality and decisioning, because storage and connectors are becoming table stakes provided by the platform underneath.
  • Wunderkind Ads and Amazon Publisher Services: Wunderkind integrated its network of more than 300 publishers directly into Amazon Publisher Services, connecting that supply to Amazon's demand-side platform and ad marketplaces and cutting integration steps for buyers. Signal: supply path optimization keeps resolving toward fewer, larger paths, and Amazon is quietly assembling the position on the sell side that it already holds on the buy side. Every hop removed from the chain is efficiency for the advertiser and concentration for the ecosystem, and those are the same event described from two seats.

#Category watch

AI visibility and answer engine optimization tooling: The category has gone from novel to crowded in under a year. Cloudflare added an AEO Visibility Dashboard to its AEO suite this month, measuring citation rate and mention rate against competitors and pairing with its existing Agent Readiness check, joining Semrush and Ahrefs plus specialists including Profound, Peec AI, and Scrunch. The structural problem is that the metric these tools sell is not stable. A study out Thursday found identical Gemini queries returned overlapping sources only about 40 percent of the time, and Gemini and ChatGPT recommended the same top business in 4.2 percent of cases. A dashboard reporting a single visibility score against an underlying signal that reproduces four times in ten is selling precision the measurement cannot support. The vendors that survive this category will be the ones that report distributions and repetition rates rather than a number, and the buyers who get value will be the ones who ask what the confidence interval is before they put it in a board deck.

#Stack intelligence

The buy versus build question in customer data is resolving into a third answer that is neither, and this week gave it a concrete shape. The argument, made well in a piece published Wednesday, is that legacy architecture was designed to answer questions about the past, so it is batch oriented and schedule driven, and the value of an intent signal decays faster than the pipeline can move it. The instinctive response is a rebuild, which takes two years and is obsolete on delivery. The emerging pattern instead is that the organization owns the storage and governance layer, typically Snowflake, BigQuery, or Databricks, and rents everything above it as swappable applications running against that data without copying it out. Zeotap's Snowflake native app is one instantiation, Databricks embedding an agentic CDP directly in the lakehouse with CustomerLake in June is another, and Fivetran's acquisition of Census folded warehouse-native activation into the pipeline layer. The practical consequence for anyone running a stack is that the durable investment is the data layer and the identity resolution logic, while the activation and orchestration tools should be selected on the assumption that you will replace them inside three years. That changes contract length, integration depth, and how much custom logic you are willing to let a vendor hold.

#Foundations concept of the day

Composable CDP: A packaged customer data platform is a single vendor product that ingests your customer events, stores its own copy, resolves identities, builds segments, and pushes audiences out to channels. A composable CDP does the same four jobs but unbundles them into separate layers that sit on top of a data warehouse you already own. The mechanics matter. Storage stays in Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift, or Databricks, where your finance, product, and operations data already lives. Identity resolution runs as a process inside that warehouse, stitching a person's email, device identifiers, loyalty number, and transactions into one profile. Segmentation and modeling are queries and models computed in place, so the segment is a view rather than an exported list. Activation happens through reverse ETL, which is simply a pipeline that reads the computed segment out of the warehouse and writes it into an operational tool such as an email platform, an ad account, or a support desk. The label for the underlying principle is zero copy: rather than moving customer data to each tool, each tool comes to the data, and the raw records never leave the governed environment. Why this matters at scale: in the packaged model, every new channel meant another copy of your customer file living in another vendor's cloud under another data processing agreement, which is a compliance surface that grows linearly with your stack and a deletion request that has to be honored in fifteen places. In the composable model, the customer record exists once, consent is enforced where the data sits, and a right to erasure is executed at the source. Cross-industry example: a bank that spent three years failing to get a packaged CDP live, because legal would not approve exporting account-linked behavioral data to an external platform, ships the same use case in a quarter when the segmentation runs inside the warehouse the data was already governed in, and the only thing crossing the boundary is an anonymized audience key. Two honest caveats. The distinction between warehouse-native and composable is largely vendor marketing, since the architecture is the same at every layer. And composability solves storage and governance without solving decisioning: you still need a policy layer that decides which of five eligible offers a customer actually receives this week, and buying the composable pieces without building that layer reproduces the same disappointment the packaged buyers reported.

#Watch list

  • Nielsen and DoubleVerify, the $2.15 billion all cash deal at $13.60 per share announced earlier this month and targeted to close in Q1 2027, producing a combined entity with over $4 billion in revenue serving clients representing more than $300 billion in ad spend. The thing to watch is not the close but the advertiser reaction, because audience measurement and independent verification landing in one house puts the grader and the graded under a single P&L at exactly the moment AI is making the placement decisions no human reviews.
  • Snowflake versus Databricks as the CDP substrate. If both data platforms keep absorbing identity resolution and activation natively while independent vendors ship as apps on top, the customer data platform ends its run as a feature of a data cloud rather than a category, and the interesting question for anyone renewing a stack contract is which of the two your organization is actually standardizing on, because that decision is quietly becoming a martech decision.

#Macro pulse

Canada: TSX closed 36,401.79 on Wednesday, up 0.09%, effectively flat while US indices sold off. USD/CAD fell to 1.3821, down 0.55% on the session, which crosses the flag line. A stronger loonie cuts both ways for a Canadian carrier. US and international point-of-sale revenue translates into fewer Canadian dollars, while jet fuel, aircraft leases, and most technology contracts are USD-denominated and just got cheaper. On a cost base that heavy in USD, a sustained move here is a margin input, not a rounding error.

USA: S&P 500 down 0.7% to 7,691.76. Nasdaq down 1.3% to 26,289.71 on a chip selloff. Dow down 0.2% to 53,343.4. Seven of eleven sectors closed red. Two things drove it. The July FOMC minutes released Wednesday showed a 9 to 3 hold, the most fractured vote in years, with three regional bank presidents preferring a quarter-point increase and hawkish sentiment extending well past the dissenters. Separately, Treasury announced it will at least double long-end buybacks, raising maximum operation size from $2 billion to at least $4 billion targeting the 10 to 20 and 20 to 30 year sectors. The 30-year fell roughly 9 basis points to 5.196% after touching 5.33% the prior session, a 19-year high.

Asia: Rebound Thursday as global yields backed off. Nikkei 225 up 1.3% through 66,000. KOSPI up roughly 6% on a sharp chipmaker recovery. Hong Kong, China, and Australia all advanced. The Korean move is the tell: the same names that dragged the Nasdaq down Wednesday led Asia up Thursday, which is a liquidity and rates story more than a demand story.

Oil: WTI at $86.15, up 1.42%. Below the flag threshold but moving the wrong direction for airline cost structures three days running.

#Today's industry move

OpenAI confirmed ChatGPT Ads expands to 31 European markets with serving beginning August 24, six months after the US test. The structural detail matters more than the geography. OpenAI is building the European ad business on user consent rather than legitimate interest, the basis most incumbents rely on. Consenting free and Go tier users get personalized ads using purchase history, ad interaction, and interest data. Non-consenters still see ads, but targeting narrows to the live conversation and coarse location, with chat history and memory excluded. Paid tiers stay ad-free. Access runs through the OpenAI Ads Solutions team, agency partners, and technology partners, with self-serve Ads Manager later.

#AI infrastructure watch

Nvidia disclosed up to $105 billion in financing for an OpenAI data center in Ohio, supporting an initial 4.25 gigawatts with an option on another 3.75, phasing online from 2028. Nvidia supplies the compute and the credit. Alongside it, Nvidia is working with six large asset managers on platforms to deploy $500 billion of third-party capital into datacenter projects. Hyperscaler capex guidance for 2026 sits above $700 billion against roughly $410 billion spent in 2025.

The connection to the bond desk is now explicit. Coverage of Wednesday's 19-year high on the long bond attributed it to AI spending and deficits competing for the same long-duration capital. Compute buildout has crossed from a sector capex story into a rates story.

#Signal or noise?

Signal. When the chip complex, the 30-year, and Treasury's buyback calendar all move on the same input, AI infrastructure has stopped being an equity theme and become a macro variable.

#Watch tomorrow

Whether long-end yields hold the buyback-driven decline or resume climbing once the operation is absorbed. If the bid does not return, the vendor-financed compute structures announced this week get repriced before they get built.
