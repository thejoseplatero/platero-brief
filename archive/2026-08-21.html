Executive Pulse1,116 words

#Macro signal

The intervention lasted one session: On Wednesday the US Treasury doubled the maximum size of its long-end buyback operations, from $2 billion to at least $4 billion, covering the 10-to-20 and 20-to-30 year sectors and taking effect September 9. Yields fell. On Thursday they went straight back up, with the 30-year rising more than seven basis points to as much as 5.27 percent before settling near 5.25 percent, within a whisker of the 19-year high it set Monday. The lesson is about tools, not about bonds. Treasury reached for a liquidity instrument to solve a supply problem. The drivers strategists name are a deficit set to exceed last year's, inflation that keeps printing above target, and a wave of corporate issuance competing for the same buyers. None of those respond to a buyback. For anyone running a multi-year capital plan, the implication is uncomfortable and specific: the discount rate you underwrote in 2024 is not coming back on the schedule your model assumes, and the first plans to break are the ones with the longest payback. That includes most enterprise AI programs, which is why the chip complex sold off in the same week that AMD's data center revenue grew 107 percent year over year. Demand is not the variable. The cost of the money is.

#Tech industry

  • Two retailers, one windfall, two very different reads: Target reported $994 million in pre-tax tariff refunds in Q2, following the Supreme Court decision striking down the IEEPA tariffs and the refund program that began in May. Operating income rose 94 percent year over year to roughly $2.6 billion, and the CFO said the company will put money into price, on top of more than 10,000 items already reduced in the past year. Walmart booked its own tariff refunds and still fell 9 percent, because comparable sales grew 2.6 percent excluding fuel against 3.7 percent expected, the slowest since the pandemic, with management citing customers making trade-offs under high fuel costs. Strategic implication: this earnings season has a one-time item large enough to hide the operating picture at almost any consumer-facing company. Read the refund line and the demand line separately, in your own numbers and in every competitor's, before anyone quotes a margin trend in a board deck. The second implication is a loop worth tracking: crude is up more than 5 percent on the week on Iran sanctions escalation, fuel cost is now showing up by name in retail trade-down commentary, and discretionary demand is downstream of both.
  • YouTube is paying to keep what it thought it owned: The platform is offering top creators millions of dollars for upload exclusivity and warning those who also post to Netflix that they will lose marketing support, event placement and a share of certain large brand deals. The trigger was honest: when the same video runs in two places, YouTube cannot sell advertisers on its exclusive value. Strategic implication: aggregation advantage is only structural while there is no second buyer. The moment a credible alternative appears, the platform discovers its supply was never captive and the advantage converts into a procurement expense that recurs every year. Any business whose position rests on being the only viable route to market for an independent supplier should model what that line item looks like on the day it stops being free.

#AI at the leadership level

The regulatory surface stopped being a single question and became a map. Illinois now requires independent third-party safety audits of frontier models, going beyond the disclosure-only regimes in California and New York. Colorado has moved to regulate AI chatbots specifically where minors are involved. California's SB 942 metadata and labeling requirements took effect August 2, the same day as Article 50 of the EU AI Act, and New York's synthetic performer law has been live since June. Running against all of it, Representatives Obernolte and Trahan have circulated a roughly 270-page draft that would preempt state laws specifically regulating AI model development for three years, while adding transparency, third-party audit, impact reporting and whistleblower provisions at the federal level.

For a board, the planning question is not which regime is correct. It is that the compliance surface is diverging by state and by use case at the same time a federal preemption fight is starting, which means the cost of compliance is now a function of your operating footprint rather than your technology choices. The practical move is to know, per jurisdiction and per use case, which of your AI deployments would be in scope tomorrow, and who owns each one by name. The IAB's new advertising disclosure framework makes the pattern explicit by asking organizations to designate an AI Disclosure Lead within 60 days. Regulation converts a judgment call into a role, and the organizations that survive audits are the ones that filled the role before they needed it.

#Leadership & org design

The governance numbers behind enterprise AI have gotten hard to look away from. Roughly 72 percent of firms report agentic AI in production while about 60 percent have no formal governance for it. Thirty-six percent have no plan at all for supervising AI agents. Sixty-seven percent of executives believe their company has already suffered a data leak or breach through unapproved AI tools. Against that, 56 percent of CEOs surveyed by PwC say they are getting nothing from their AI efforts, and 42 percent of leaders say the technology is capable but their organization is not structured to capture the value.

Those findings are usually reported as two separate problems, a governance gap and an ROI gap. They are one problem. An organization that cannot say which agents are running, who owns them and what they touched is also an organization that cannot attribute a result to any of them. The absence of a control surface removes both the safety and the measurement at once. That is why the ROI question keeps coming back unanswered no matter how much is spent: nobody can produce the ledger. The organizations reporting real gains are consistently the ones that put agents inside an owned workflow with a named accountable human and an outcome the human is measured on, which looks like bureaucracy from the outside and turns out to be the thing that makes the number legible.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If we had to name, today, the single human accountable for each AI system currently running in production, and the specific business outcome each one is measured on, how many could we name, and what does the length of that list tell us about the number we have been reporting as AI value?

#Try this now

  • Write your AI disclosure decision tree before someone writes it for you: The IAB published version 2 of its AI Transparency and Disclosure Framework this week, and it is the first document in this space that is usable rather than aspirational. The core rule is narrow on purpose: disclose when AI involvement could mislead a reasonable consumer about what is authentic, factual or human-created. Routine post-production, color correction, cleanup, and obviously stylized or fantastical imagery are explicitly out of scope. US disclosure takes one of two forms, the standardized Unicode sparkle or plain text saying AI-generated, and stricter binding law wins wherever it applies. The framework asks for a named AI Disclosure Lead within 60 days. Do three things this week. First, take twenty live assets and sort them into disclose, do not disclose, and cannot decide, because the third pile is your actual policy gap and it will be bigger than anyone expects. Second, check which markets you serve are already under binding rules rather than guidance: New York's synthetic performer law since June, California SB 942 and Article 50 of the EU AI Act since August 2, South Korea's revised AI Basic Act in the same window. Third, name the lead. Not a committee. A person, with the authority to say no to a campaign at 4pm.
  • Run one agent-to-agent buy against your own direct benchmark: Butler/Till and iHeartMedia completed what both describe as the first premium streaming audio campaign transacted through paired agents, for an agricultural solutions client. The agency's buying agent and the publisher's selling agent met over an MCP server, with a human planner supplying the brief. CPMs came in 42 percent under the client's own direct-buy benchmark, and mid-roll took 48 percent of impressions against 33 percent in the traditional plan. Two things make this worth copying rather than admiring. The benchmark was the client's own prior direct buying, not an index, which is the only comparison that survives scrutiny. And the savings came from removing negotiation overhead, not from removing quality, since the placement mix actually improved. Pick one channel where your team still buys direct with a lot of back-and-forth, run a small parallel test, and hold the brief constant. If the number is anywhere near this, the finding is not about audio. It is about how much of your working media is currently paying for people to email each other.
  • Score your own budget confidence before the planning cycle opens: Fresh survey data this week puts the number at 49 percent, the share of B2B marketers who fully trust the accuracy and completeness of the data used to shape their budgets. Underneath it: three-quarters make investment decisions on partial data, 47 percent have only moderate confidence in measuring true cross-channel ROI, and 24 percent use media mix modeling to reallocate against live performance. US marketers hold the biggest budgets of any region and report the lowest confidence in hitting growth targets. The uncomfortable version of that finding is that the industry solved data collection and never solved data trust, so leaders quietly fall back on experience and last year's split. Run the two-question test on your own team before the next cycle. Which line items in this plan would we defend with evidence in front of a skeptical CFO, and which are inherited. Then put the second list on one page. Whatever survives contact with that page is your real strategy, and everything else is a renewal.

#Case study worth stealing

Walmart, selling the same data twice to two different buyers: Walmart Data Ventures confirmed Thursday that Scintilla, its first-party commerce intelligence platform, comes to Sam's Club in 2027, giving Sam's merchants and suppliers member demand signals, omnichannel performance and outcome data in one place. The CFO said it had been one of the top supplier requests. What is worth stealing is the structure, not the product. Walmart runs two businesses on one data asset. Walmart Connect sells attention to advertisers and grew 43 percent last quarter. Scintilla sells operating intelligence to the suppliers already in the building, and it scales by adding a banner rather than by adding advertisers, which is a far cheaper growth curve. Most organizations that get excited about retail media copy the first business and never notice the second one sitting next to it, even though the second is usually easier to start: the buyers are existing commercial partners, the contract already exists, and nobody has to be convinced the data is real because they helped generate it. Any business with authenticated customers, repeat transactions and a supplier or partner ecosystem has the same two-sided asset. The question to take into next week is which of your partners is currently making decisions about your business with worse data than you already hold, and what it would take to sell them the view.

#Framework or mental model

The implied-truth effect, and why blanket AI labeling backfires: The finding driving the IAB's selective approach is that labels do not just describe content, they redistribute credibility. An NYU Stern study found that disclosing generative AI involvement cut an advertisement's click-through rate by 31.5 percent. A separate Raptive study of 3,000 US adults found that merely suspected AI authorship roughly halved reader trust and produced a 14 percent decline in purchase consideration for adjacent advertising, meaning the penalty is already there whether or not you label. The implied-truth effect is the second-order problem: once some content carries a label, audiences read unlabeled content as more credible, which means a blanket labeling policy does not just tax the labeled assets, it inflates trust in everything you left alone and eventually trains people to distrust the whole category. The operating conclusion is that disclosure is a design decision with a measurable cost and a nonlinear effect, so it belongs to the same people who own conversion, not filed away as a legal checkbox. Label where deception is genuinely possible. Do not label to signal virtue, because the audience prices it as a defect and the assets you skipped get the credit.

#Rabbit hole

The thing worth an hour is not any single AI law but the shape the map is taking. Illinois now requires independent third-party safety audits of frontier models, a materially higher bar than the disclosure-only regimes in California and New York. Colorado has gone the other direction, regulating a single narrow use case, chatbots where minors are involved. California SB 942 imposes metadata and labeling obligations, the EU AI Act's Article 50 covers transparency, New York targets synthetic performers specifically, and South Korea's revised AI Basic Act adds a fourth flavor. Meanwhile a roughly 270-page federal draft from Representatives Obernolte and Trahan would preempt state laws regulating model development for three years while adding transparency, third-party audit and whistleblower requirements of its own. Read side by side, they are regulating four different things and calling all of them AI: the model, the developer, the output artifact, and the deployment context. Which one a given law targets determines whether it lands on your vendor, your platform team, your creative studio or your product manager. That is the practical exercise. Take your five largest AI deployments and sort them by which of those four objects they actually are, because a compliance program organized around the word AI will be organized around nothing, and the teams that get caught out are the ones who assumed the vendor's certification covered the deployment.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Walmart Data Ventures: Confirmed Thursday that Scintilla, its first-party commerce intelligence platform, comes to Sam's Club in 2027, giving Sam's merchants and suppliers member-level demand, omnichannel performance and business outcome data in one place. CFO John David Rainey said it was one of the top supplier requests. Signal: the retail data business and the retail media business are separating into two products with two buyers. Walmart Connect sells impressions to advertisers. Scintilla sells operating intelligence to suppliers, on the same underlying transaction and identity layer, and it scales by adding banners rather than by adding advertisers. Anyone sitting on authenticated transaction data has been reading the retail media playbook and missing the second, higher-margin one next to it.
  • IAB: Published version 2 of its AI Transparency and Disclosure Framework this week, defining which uses of generative AI in advertising creative warrant a consumer-facing label and which do not. Two disclosure options for US advertising, a standardized sparkle icon or plain text. Routine post-production such as color correction and cleanup is out of scope, as is obviously stylized or fantastical imagery. The trigger is whether AI involvement could mislead a reasonable consumer about what is authentic, factual or human-created. The framework asks organizations to designate an AI Disclosure Lead within 60 days. Signal: this is a self-regulatory body racing four binding laws that already landed. New York's synthetic performer law took effect in June, California SB 942 and Article 50 of the EU AI Act both took effect August 2, and South Korea's revised AI Basic Act is in the same window. Creative disclosure has crossed from brand judgment into a compliance workflow that needs a named owner and an audit trail.
  • Butler/Till and iHeartMedia: Completed what both describe as the first premium streaming audio buy executed through an agent-to-agent workflow, running for an agricultural solutions client. Two agents, one built by the agency and one by the publisher, paired over an MCP server. A human planner handed the buying agent a campaign brief and the agent negotiated with the publisher's agent. CPMs came in 42 percent below the client's own direct-buy benchmark, and 48 percent of impressions landed in mid-roll against 33 percent in the traditional plan. Broadcast radio is next. Signal: MCP is quietly becoming the transaction interface for media, not just the integration layer for tools. The first channels to fall are the ones where direct buying carried the most human negotiation overhead, which is precisely where the 42 percent was hiding.

#Category watch

Agent-to-agent transacting: Six months ago agentic media buying meant an LLM writing a brief or optimizing a bid inside someone's DSP. The Butler/Till and iHeart test is a different thing: two counterparties each fielding an agent, meeting over a shared protocol, with the human moving up to brief-setting and approval. The agency side is being built by independents rather than the holdcos, which is the usual pattern for a capability that threatens an existing margin structure. The open question is not capability but governance. When your agent negotiates with a publisher's agent, the audit trail, the brand safety constraints and the acceptable-trade envelope have to be encoded in advance, because there is no buyer on the phone to say no. Expect the first serious dispute in this category to be about what an agent agreed to, not about what it delivered.

#Stack intelligence

Two data points from this week describe the same failure. Only 49 percent of B2B marketers fully trust the accuracy and completeness of the data used to shape their budgets. Three-quarters make investment decisions on partial data, 47 percent report only moderate confidence in measuring true cross-channel ROI, and just 24 percent use media mix modeling to reallocate against live performance. US marketers hold the largest budgets of any region and report the lowest confidence in hitting growth targets. Separately, HubSpot's Agent Hub and Agent Builder went to public beta targeting what it names directly as agent sprawl, agents in production that do not know what the other agents are doing, with no clear ownership or performance visibility.

The through-line is that the industry solved collection and never solved trust. Now it is repeating the shape of the problem one layer up. The first wave built a stack of tools nobody could reconcile into one number. The second wave is building a fleet of agents nobody can reconcile into one account of what happened. The remedy is the same in both cases and it is organizational: a single ledger of what ran, what it cost and what it changed, owned by someone whose job depends on it. Buying an agent console does not create that ledger any more than buying a CDP created the first one.

#Foundations concept of the day

Supply path optimization (SPO): When you buy a programmatic ad, your money can reach the same publisher impression through many different routes. A demand-side platform can bid into it via three or four different supply-side platforms, each of which may have gotten it through a reseller, and each hop takes a cut and adds latency. SPO is the practice of deliberately choosing which paths you buy through and shutting the rest off. The mechanics: you log which SSPs and resellers actually deliver your winning bids, measure the fee take and the win rate on each path, verify the inventory is legitimately sourced through ads.txt and its server-side counterpart, then consolidate spend into a handful of direct, preferably contracted, paths. Buyers who do this well typically recover somewhere between a tenth and a quarter of working media that was previously eaten by intermediation, and they get cleaner logs as a bonus, because fewer hops means fewer places for a discrepancy to hide. It matters at scale because the tax is proportional: an enterprise spending nine figures programmatically is funding an entire layer of the ecosystem it never chose to buy from. The cross-industry example is any large advertiser that ran a path audit and discovered it was bidding against itself, with two of its own SSPs competing for the same impression and raising the clearing price it then paid. Worth understanding right now because agent-to-agent buying is SPO taken to its endpoint. The 42 percent CPM reduction in this week's audio test is what happens when the path collapses to one hop and the negotiation cost goes to near zero.

#Watch list

  • MCP as a commercial protocol rather than a technical one. It was designed to connect models to tools. It is now being used to connect a buyer's balance sheet to a seller's inventory. Nobody has written the terms of service for that yet, and the first vendor to ship credible agent-to-agent commerce governance, spend caps, negotiation boundaries, signed transaction logs, will define the category.
  • Scintilla versus Amazon Marketing Cloud as the supplier-facing data layer. Two retailers, two philosophies. One is packaging insight as a subscription product for merchants and suppliers, the other is exposing a clean room and letting sophisticated advertisers query it. Whichever model wins sets the template for every carrier, bank and telco that eventually monetizes its own operating data.

#Today's industry move

YouTube is offering top creators millions of dollars for upload exclusivity, and warning those who post simultaneously to Netflix that they will lose marketing support, event placement and a share of proceeds from certain large brand deals. No deals are signed yet, though several are close. The stated reason is honest and instructive: when a video runs on both platforms at once, YouTube can no longer sell advertisers on that video's exclusive value. This is a platform discovering that its supply is independent. Aggregation leverage that looked structural turns out to be a procurement line item once a credible second buyer shows up.

#AI infrastructure watch

The chip complex is in its sharpest pullback of 2026. The PHLX Semiconductor Index posted its steepest three-day decline since March. AMD fell more than 4 percent through the $500 level. Nvidia shed as much as $153 billion of market value in a single session, more than its entire projected fiscal 2026 profit of roughly $120 billion. None of this is a demand signal. AMD's data center segment grew 107 percent year over year to $6.7 billion in Q2. What repriced is the cost of the capital that funds the buildout, which is exactly what the 30-year is telling you. Nvidia reports Wednesday, August 26, and that print resets sector tone in one direction or the other.

#Signal or noise?

Signal. Treasury deployed a technical fix, doubling long-end buyback operations from $2 billion to at least $4 billion effective September 9, and the market absorbed it in a single session. When a supply-and-inflation problem is met with a liquidity tool, the relief has a half-life. The compute buildout is now underwritten at a long-end yield that will not cooperate.

#Watch tomorrow

Whether the Friday futures bounce survives the cash session with the long bond parked at 5.25 percent, because a rally that needs stable yields is not a rally, it is a pause. Next week carries both catalysts: Nvidia on Wednesday the 26th, then Jackson Hole from the 27th to the 29th with Kevin Warsh delivering his first keynote as Fed chair on Friday morning.
