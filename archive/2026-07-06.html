Executive Pulse413 words

#Macro signal

SpaceX joined the Nasdaq-100 today, the first trading session since its record $1.75T IPO. Passive index funds now carry automatic exposure to space infrastructure as an asset class, a category that barely existed in major indices a year ago. For any leader thinking about long-horizon infrastructure bets, this is a signal that "unconventional" infrastructure categories (space, energy, compute) are being absorbed into mainstream capital allocation faster than governance and risk frameworks are catching up.

#Tech industry

PTC unveiled a wave of AI product innovations at PTC NEXT Chicago this week: two new cross-portfolio products, a new AI platform, 12 new AI agents, and 10 new integrations across its CAD/PLM portfolio, including a new "PTC Orbit" solution specifically designed to close the gap between a product as designed and the asset as actually maintained in the field. Strategic implication: the "as-designed vs. as-maintained" gap PTC is targeting in physical product manufacturing is the same structural problem digital orgs have with systems drifting from their documented architecture. Worth the analogy regardless of industry.

#AI at the leadership level

BCG's 2026 AI Radar finds nearly three-quarters of CEOs now consider themselves their company's chief decision-maker on AI, a genuine shift from AI being routed to the CTO as a technical concern. The stakes are personal: 50% of CEOs globally say they believe their own job stability now depends on getting AI strategy right, and more than half of non-CEO executives believe the CEO or board should resign if the company loses market share due to an inadequate AI strategy. This is a materially higher-stakes framing than the governance-structure conversations from earlier this week. It's no longer about whether a CAIO role exists, it's about personal accountability at the top.

#Leadership & org design

The same BCG research finds nearly all CEOs believe AI agents will produce measurable returns in 2026, yet 60% admit they've intentionally slowed their own implementation over concerns about errors and malfunctions. That's a genuinely rational tension, not indecision: the leaders most convinced of the upside are often the same ones most aware of what an uncontrolled failure would cost.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If your organization's AI agents made a costly, visible error tomorrow, is the accountability chain actually understood, or does everyone assume it's "someone else's" call until it happens?

--- Sources: Bloomberg: Stock market today July 6, PTC NEXT Chicago product announcements, BCG AI Radar 2026: CEOs take the lead

#Try this now

PTC's new AI-assisted design feedback in Onshape (announced this week at PTC NEXT Chicago): the CAD/PDM platform now gives AI-driven design feedback directly in the modeling environment, plus expanded robotics simulation and cloud-connected ECAD-MCAD collaboration. Worth a look even outside hardware/CAD contexts. The pattern of "feedback embedded directly in the creation tool, not a separate review step" is the same shift happening across every design discipline right now, and Onshape's specific implementation is a useful reference for what "good" looks like.

#Case study worth stealing

No new case study surfaced today that clears the bar beyond what's already captured. See this week's Alaska Airlines identity-resolution note if you haven't already sat with it.

#Framework or mental model

As-designed vs. as-maintained gap: PTC's new "Orbit" asset-intelligence product is built specifically to close the gap between how a product was originally designed and how it actually exists after real-world maintenance and drift. The mental model transfers directly to software and org design: any system, a codebase, a team's operating model, a martech stack, drifts from its documented architecture the moment real usage begins. The practical move is treating "as-built" audits as a recurring discipline, not a one-time architecture document you write once and never revisit.

#Rabbit hole

Worth 20 minutes: PTC's NEXT Chicago announcement covers 12 new AI agents and 10 new integrations shipped in a single event, a useful benchmark for how fast an established enterprise software vendor can now move when AI-native competitors are pressuring the whole category. If you're evaluating vendor velocity as a buying signal, this is a concrete data point for "how much AI shipping speed should I expect from an incumbent."

#Vendor & platform moves

  • CartographAI gaining real enterprise pilots: the vendor-comparison tool that lets marketers navigate 1,000+ MarTech/AdTech vendors is now confirmed in active testing at Reckitt, LinkedIn, and Mars. Signal: this is moving from concept to validated enterprise workflow. The "which vendor" decision is becoming its own tooling category, not just a research exercise, because the vendor landscape has gotten too large to navigate manually (nearly 1,500 tools added to the category in 2026 alone, per CDP Institute tracking).
  • HubSpot's continued AI-native CRM push: recent HubSpot moves reinforce that CRM platforms are shifting from passive customer databases into active systems that identify buying intent and take autonomous action. Signal: the CRM layer is quietly becoming the same kind of "decisioning engine" that CDPs were supposed to be. Worth watching whether this blurs the CRM/CDP boundary entirely over the next 12 months.

#Category watch

The "Custom AdTech Renaissance": 2026 is confirmed as the year the pendulum swung from renting opaque, high-fee third-party ad platforms toward organizations owning key parts of their own AdTech infrastructure. The AdTech market itself grew to $1.117T in 2026 (up from $986.87B in 2025), but the growth is increasingly captured by owned infrastructure and first-party data layers rather than pure platform spend.

#Stack intelligence

With nearly 1,500 tools added and 1,300 tools disappearing from the MarTech landscape in a single year, vendor churn risk is now a genuine stack-architecture concern, not just a procurement inconvenience. The practical takeaway: any point solution adopted this year should be evaluated on data-portability and API-continuity terms, because the base rate of vendor disappearance or acquisition in this category is now high enough that "will this vendor exist in 18 months" is a real diligence question, not paranoia.

#Foundations concept of the day

First-party data layer as table stakes: A first-party data layer, whether a formal CDP or an equivalent internally-owned system, is now considered baseline infrastructure, not a differentiator, according to CDP Institute tracking that shows the strongest organic category growth since 2022. Why it matters at scale: for years, having a unified first-party data layer was a competitive advantage. That window is closing. It's becoming the price of entry to compete at all, meaning the actual differentiation is shifting to what you do with that data (activation speed, decisioning quality, privacy-safe collaboration) rather than whether you have it in the first place. Cross-industry example: two airlines can both have a "unified loyalty data layer." The one that wins is the one that activates it fastest into personalized, real-time offers, not the one that simply owns the data.

#Watch list

  • CRM/CDP boundary blur: watch whether CRM vendors (HubSpot and peers) keep absorbing CDP-style decisioning capability, which could make the "which category do I even need" question harder for buyers over the next year.
  • Vendor churn as a stack-risk metric: with ~1,300 tools disappearing from the category this year, tracking vendor survival rate is becoming as important as tracking vendor capability when building a stack.

#The convergence watch (loyalty data → media networks)

Marriott's Riott Media Network has grown its cross-brand advertising partnership with United Airlines one year after its official debut, expanding personalization offerings for advertisers across both loyalty ecosystems. Signal: this is the convergence thesis playing out directly. A hotel media network and an airline media network are now commercially partnering with each other, not just independently monetizing their own first-party data. The travel category is starting to look less like separate airline/hotel ad networks and more like an interconnected travel-media mesh, which changes the competitive question from "should we build our own media network" to "which media networks should we be inside of."

#Airline loyalty & digital

  • British Airways × Porter Airlines codeshare goes live July 8: British Airways Club members will instantly earn Avios and Tier Points on eligible Porter-operated flights, giving BA customers seamless access to 17 Canadian destinations via Toronto Pearson and Montréal-Trudeau. Signal: this is a direct competitive pressure point on Aeroplan's Canadian domestic network. A European carrier's loyalty program now reaches deep into Canada through a partner that isn't Air Canada.
  • Aeroplan devalued its award chart in June 2026: increases on transatlantic and transpacific redemptions, some reductions on intra-Europe business class. Reporting ties the move to intensifying competition from Porter, WestJet, and United's newly launched Canadian credit card. Signal: devaluation under competitive pressure (rather than pure margin management) suggests Aeroplan is defending yield on award inventory precisely where new entrants are contesting share.
  • WestJet and Aeroplan launched a dual-currency partnership (MyWestJet): members now earn both WestJet and Aeroplan points simultaneously on eligible WestJet flights. Signal: coalition-style dual-earning structures are becoming a competitive response pattern in the Canadian market, not just an Air Canada/Aeroplan-specific mechanic.

#Hotel & travel loyalty

  • Marriott Riott Media Network: one year post-launch, continuing to expand both its advertiser base and its cross-brand travel partnerships (see convergence section above). The clearest current example of a hotel loyalty program operating as a mature, standalone media business rather than a discount mechanic.

#AdTech & retail media

  • Data clean rooms have crossed into default infrastructure, not innovation: roughly 90% of B2C marketers now use clean rooms for marketing use cases, and industry reporting expects them to be the norm in retail media deals by year-end. Signal: clean-room capability is no longer a differentiator for a retail or travel media network. It's a baseline requirement to be taken seriously as a media seller at all.
  • CTV ad spend inside retail media networks is projected to more than double, from ~$5B to ~$10.28B by 2028, as retail and travel media networks extend into connected TV to combine high-intent audience data with big-screen reach. Signal: any airline or hotel media network not yet building a CTV extension path is leaving a fast-growing, high-value inventory category on the table.

#Strategic implication

The defining move this week isn't a single launch. It's that travel media networks are starting to interconnect with each other (Marriott × United) rather than operate as isolated, single-brand ad businesses. For any carrier's loyalty program considering a media network build, the real strategic question is no longer "can we monetize our own first-party data." Most credible programs already can, and clean rooms have become table stakes for doing so credibly. The harder question is which travel-media partnerships to plug into, since the category is starting to reward networks that are interconnected over networks that are merely large.
