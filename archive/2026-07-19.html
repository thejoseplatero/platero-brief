Executive Pulse569 words

#Macro signal

The AI buildout is now embedded in the inflation math, and that changes the rate calculus: Post-earnings weakness this week was driven by a "three-headed" pullback, a chip sell-off (the semiconductor index sliding toward 20% off its highs), oil breaking above $80 on Middle East tension, and a 10%-plus Netflix drop. But the durable signal underneath is that the cost of the AI capex supercycle is now showing up in the CPI itself, even as broader inflation data stayed reassuring enough that no rate hike is expected this month. Strategically, this reframes AI spend: it is no longer a discretionary line item that gets cut when rates rise, it is a structural cost input that regulators and central banks are starting to watch. For tech, product, and marketing leaders, the implication is that budgets for AI infrastructure should be defended as fixed operating cost, not tested against every quarter's macro mood. Concentration risk is the other side: a market this dependent on a handful of chip and platform names means one earnings miss moves the whole index.

#Tech industry

  • Hg Capital's $6.4B all-cash take-private of OneStream: A classic PE play for corporate performance management software sold into finance departments. Strategic implication: the finance-software layer is being consolidated under patient private capital precisely as CFOs become the internal owners of AI governance and spend discipline. Expect the tools that finance teams use to plan, forecast, and control AI budgets to get bundled and repriced. Buyers should assume roadmap and pricing volatility on CPM platforms over the next 18 months.

#AI at the leadership level

The EU AI Act's high-risk obligations were quietly deferred from August 2, 2026 to December 2027 under the Digital Omnibus, but the Article 50 transparency rules were not deferred and still land on August 2. That means any customer-facing system must disclose when a user is interacting with AI, on schedule, in weeks. The board-level read: the hard compliance cliff moved, but the reputational and disclosure floor did not. Meanwhile the governance gap is stark. Deloitte finds AI is now on 69% of board agendas (up from 55%), yet only about 39% of Fortune 100 boards have any explicit AI oversight mechanism, and a majority of enterprises run AI in production with no designated leadership owner. The move that matters: name an accountable executive for AI now, before the transparency deadline forces the question in public.

#Leadership & org design

The strongest signal in operating-model design this quarter is the collapse of the management pyramid. Because one human can supervise far more agent output than human output, spans of control are widening and the pure-manager layer is thinning. BCG's 2026 work reports organizations that redesign the operating model around AI, rather than bolting AI onto the existing chart, are seeing up to 60% cost reduction and 80% cycle-time reduction. PwC frames the same shift as the "rise of the generalist," broader outcome-owning roles replacing narrow functional ones. The practical takeaway for leaders: the win is not headcount cuts, it is redrawing the "work chart" around outcomes and treating agents as first-class org units with named human owners.

#Question worth asking your team this week

If a regulator or a customer asked us today who is personally accountable for our AI systems, could we name that person, and does their authority actually match the risk we have in production?

#Try this now

  • Framer external agents: Framer just opened its canvas to Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and Gemini CLI. Copy the prompt from framer.com/agents/external, run one terminal command, and the agent gets direct read/write access to your canvas, components, and CMS with no manual MCP setup. Setup is 5-10 minutes and it works on the editor, not the live site, so nothing publishes until you say so. Best first job: point it at a CMS collection and have it do bulk content edits, localization, or redirects that would take a person an afternoon.
  • Context audit on your longest automation: The practical finding making the rounds this week is that model reasoning starts degrading around 3,000 tokens, well below the technical max, with a sweet spot near 150-300 words per task. Before shipping any multi-step prompt or agent, log the full context payload at each turn and cut anything that does not need to be there right now. Trim your three longest production prompts and watch quality and cost both improve.
  • Meeting-to-action extraction: Drop a raw transcript or recording into an assistant and prompt "Summarize the decisions made, the open questions, and the action items by owner." It is one of the highest-leverage 20-minute habits available and almost nobody outside of tech has adopted it. Standardize the exact wording across your team so the output is consistent enough to paste straight into a tracker.

#Case study worth stealing

Lightspeed Commerce, autonomous support at 99% coverage: The e-commerce and fintech platform routed its Intercom Fin agent into 99% of support conversations and let it autonomously resolve up to 65% of them. The move was not "add a chatbot," it was making the agent the default first touch on every ticket and measuring resolution rate rather than deflection. A digital team can steal the framing: pick one high-volume support or ops queue, put an agent on 100% of intake as triage, and hold it to an autonomous-resolution number instead of a vanity deflection metric.

#Framework or mental model

Loop engineering: Instead of writing the perfect prompt each time, you design the system that prompts the agent for you, made of scheduled automations, isolated worktrees, codified skills, connectors to real tools, and a subagent that independently verifies the output, with progress stored in an external file so it survives between runs. The named risk is "cognitive surrender," taking whatever the loop returns without an opinion. Digital leaders can apply it to any recurring deliverable (weekly reports, competitor scans, content drafts) by building the loop once and reviewing outputs with judgment rather than restarting from a blank prompt daily.

#Rabbit hole

GEO is now a measured discipline, not a buzzword: At least eight generative-engine-optimization platforms now exist to track how often a brand appears in AI answers, and the tactics have hardened into something a team can execute. Write for citability with clean headers, concise paragraphs, and FAQ patterns so an answer engine can lift a clean response. Distributing the same content across many trusted publications reportedly lifts AI citations up to 325% versus publishing only on your own site. Platform choice matters: 97% of Perplexity answers cite sources versus 16% for ChatGPT, so the surface you optimize for changes the play. The metric to adopt is Share of Model Voice, how often your brand shows up in AI answers relative to competitors, because ranking on Google no longer means being visible where a growing share of buyers now ask.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Zoom / Common Room: Zoom signed a definitive deal (announced early July, closing within weeks) to acquire Common Room, an AI-native go-to-market intelligence platform whose RoomieAI agents unify first-party CRM, product, and engagement signals into person-level buyer profiles and act on them. Common Room's roster includes Atlassian, Anthropic, Okta, and Snowflake. Signal: a communications platform is buying its way into the revenue-data layer. The battleground is no longer "who stores the data" but "who owns the agent that acts on the signal," and adjacent-category players are willing to pay to get there.
  • BlueConic / Blueshift: BlueConic closed its acquisition of Blueshift, bolting cross-channel decisioning (email, push, in-app, SMS, web) onto its first-party profile engine. The combined base is 600-plus customers across CPG, retail, DTC, and travel and hospitality (ASICS, L'Oreal, StitchFix, Five Below). Signal: this is explicitly framed as building an agentic CDP, not a data-consolidation play. The CDP category is moving from "unify and store" to "decide and execute" in a single vendor.
  • Databricks / CustomerLake: Databricks' agentic CDP, launched at Data + AI Summit and now in private preview with HP, Circle K, and AB InBev, folds identity resolution, audience building, activation, and "infinity campaigns" (continuous agentic loops) directly into the lakehouse under Unity Catalog governance. Signal: the data-cloud vendors are collapsing the martech stack into the warehouse itself. If the profile, the model, and the activation all live where the data already sits, the standalone CDP's reason to exist narrows.

#Category watch

Agentic CDPs: Three moves in roughly a month (Zoom/Common Room, BlueConic/Blueshift, Databricks/CustomerLake) point at the same shift. The 2018-era CDP promise was unification, get every customer signal into one profile. The 2026 promise is action, put an agent on top of that profile that builds the audience, picks the next-best move, and activates across channels on a loop. For anyone building a martech org, the buying question is changing from "which CDP unifies our data best" to "where does the decisioning agent live, and do we trust it inside the warehouse, inside a point solution, or as a layer we assemble ourselves."

#Stack intelligence

The consolidation pattern to watch is the warehouse-native stack. When Databricks embeds a CDP inside the lakehouse and governs it with the same catalog that governs everything else, the enterprise buy-vs-build math flips. A team that already runs its data platform on a lakehouse or a cloud warehouse can now treat the customer data layer as a feature of infrastructure it already owns, rather than a six-figure standalone contract with its own identity model, its own governance, and its own copy of the data. The architectural tell is data gravity: activation is moving to where the first-party data already lives instead of the data being shipped out to a marketing tool. Point-solution CDPs counter by racing to add agents and execution faster than the warehouses can add marketing polish. For an org-builder, the near-term call is less "which tool" and more "how many copies of the customer profile do we want to maintain, and who governs them."

#Foundations concept of the day

Data clean rooms: A clean room is a secure, neutral environment where two parties (say a retailer and a brand, or an advertiser and a publisher) can match and analyze their combined data without either side ever seeing the other's raw customer records. Each party uploads its first-party data, the room matches individuals on a shared key like a hashed email, and both sides get aggregate results (overlap size, campaign reach, conversions) rather than row-level identities. It matters at scale because third-party cookies are gone and no single replacement ID won, so the durable way to connect audiences across companies is privacy-safe collaboration on first-party data. Real example: a consumer-goods brand runs a campaign on a retailer's media network, then uses the retailer's clean room to see which ad-exposed shoppers actually bought, without the retailer handing over its customer list or the brand exposing its own. Vendors like Optable now run live Chrome-auction campaigns and attribution through this model, a sign the clean room has moved from privacy insurance to standard media plumbing.

#Watch list

  • Warehouse-native CDPs vs point-solution CDPs: watch whether enterprise buyers standardize on the customer data layer their data cloud already offers (Databricks, and expect a Snowflake response) or keep the marketer-friendly standalone CDP as a separate agentic execution layer. The next 12 months decide whether the CDP is a product category or a warehouse feature.
  • The post-Sandbox identity portfolio: with Google's Privacy Sandbox wound down and no single cookie replacement, the 2026 identity stack is a portfolio (UID2, ID5, RampID, clean-room matches, first-party and contextual signals). Watch for tooling that abstracts across these IDs so buyers do not have to bet on one, that abstraction layer is where the next durable adtech position gets built.

#Macro pulse

Markets are closed for the weekend, so this reads off Friday's closes and a rough week. Two forces dominated: a China-AI shock and a Middle East oil spike. Canada: the S&P/TSX composite slipped about 0.2% to roughly 35,340 Friday, whipsawed as energy gains offset a global chip selloff and softer gold. USD/CAD sat near 1.40, essentially flat on the day and below the 0.5% flag, so no material hit to Canadian carrier international revenue for now. USA: the S&P 500 fell 1% to 7,475, the Dow lost 0.8% to 52,146, and the Nasdaq dropped 1.4% to 25,520, with all three down on the week (Nasdaq off 2.9%) as semiconductors got smoked. The Bank of Canada held at 2.25% on July 15 for a sixth straight hold, flagging CPI at 3.2% in May driven by war-linked gasoline, with core near 2%. Asia led the rout: Nikkei crashed 4% to 64,141, Shanghai fell 3.1%, Hang Seng lost 1.8%. Oil: WTI surged about 4.5% to $82.49, well past the 2% flag, up roughly 12% on the week on the Iran conflict.

#Today's industry move

The week's defining move was risk-off in tech, not a single deal. Chinese lab Moonshot released Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter open-weight model, triggering a DeepSeek-style repricing of the "more compute equals more value" thesis. TSMC fell 7% Friday despite a 77% jump in quarterly operating profit, SoftBank dropped 9%, and Nvidia briefly ceded the most-valuable-company crown to Apple. On the deal side, Zoom acquired go-to-market intelligence startup Common Room (terms undisclosed), folding CRM plus product-usage signals and AI outreach agents into its stack, a signal that signal-based, AI-native GTM tooling keeps commanding premium multiples.

#AI infrastructure watch

The selloff hit the physical stack hardest. AMD fell 5%, Intel 4%, and Nvidia slid 3% before partly recovering as capital rotated out of AI chips. The trigger was fear that cheaper open-weight models let hyperscalers scale back capex, compressing the demand curve that premium silicon valuations are priced against. TSMC compounded it by guiding to higher-than-expected capex on rising tool prices, feeding margin-compression worries across the supply chain. This is the first serious market test of whether the app-layer efficiency story undercuts the infrastructure buildout.

#Signal or noise?

Part of a larger trend: the second China-model shock in eighteen months means the "compute is destiny" premium now reprices on every efficiency breakthrough, and the oil leg is a genuine conflict-driven repricing, not a blip.

#Watch tomorrow

Watch Monday's open in Asia and North America for whether the chip rout stabilizes or cascades, and track WTI, if the Iran conflict keeps crude above $80 into next week, jet-fuel-exposed carriers and Canadian CPI both feel it, though a higher oil complex cushions the TSX energy weighting.
