Executive Pulse718 words

#Macro signal

Middle East war keeps energy markets in a high-volatility regime, not just a high-price one. Brent has been swinging between $85-120/barrel since the conflict began, with the Strait of Hormuz disruption producing the largest oil supply shock on record (10M+ bpd off the market at peak) and European gas jumping from €31 to €47/MWh. The strategic point for leaders isn't "energy is expensive", it's that input-cost planning now has to assume volatility, not a stable elevated baseline. Budget and pricing models built on point forecasts will keep getting blindsided; scenario bands belong in every cost and pricing conversation this quarter, especially anywhere freight, travel, or energy-intensive infrastructure (data centers) touches the P&L.

#Tech industry

  • Oracle cut 20-30K jobs via a 6am email the same quarter it posted strong earnings, redirecting the freed budget into data center buildout, then hired a new CFO on a $2.5M bonus target. Meta is running the same play: a 10% headcount cut (~8K people, guidance suggests up to 20% by year-end) with savings redirected to AI research and infra. Strategic implication: strong revenue is no longer a defense against restructuring, boards are now underwriting the bet that fewer humans plus more AI infrastructure beats the current org, even when the current org is winning. If your own budget defenses lean on "the numbers are good," that argument is losing force with this cohort of boards.
  • Enterprise AI agent deployment moved from pilot to production faster than security posture caught up: agents are reported to increase network attack surface by 450%+ versus a human account, and this is happening while agents get real authority over code, legal, and financial workflows. Worth a direct check with security/IT: what's our agent-to-human attack-surface ratio right now, and does anyone own it end to end?

#AI at the leadership level

CEOs are consolidating AI decision rights instead of delegating them: nearly three-quarters now call themselves the company's chief AI decision-maker, roughly double last year's share, and half say their own job is on the line if AI doesn't pay off. The CEOs seeing results aren't just buying tools, they're redesigning the C-suite itself (decision rights, cross-functional authority, workflow ownership), and that cohort shows 17% higher revenue growth over three years. Corporate AI spend is set to roughly double in 2026, from ~0.8% to ~1.7% of revenue, and 82% of CEOs expect governance investment needs to keep rising alongside it. The takeaway for a functional leader: "AI strategy" that stays inside one department is already behind the CEOs winning with this, it has to touch org design and decision rights, not just tooling.

#Leadership & org design

The pattern across BCG and IBM's 2026 CEO research is consistent: the leaders getting compounding returns from AI are the ones treating it as a reorg exercise, not a procurement exercise, redesigning how decisions get made and who has authority, then embedding AI across full workflows rather than bolting it onto existing ones. The CEOs putting in real time (8+ hours/week building their own AI fluency) are the ones generating value; delegation-only approaches are underperforming. Practical read: if AI adoption in your org is still a tooling rollout owned by IT or a single team, it's structurally behind where the top-quartile performers already are.

#Question worth asking your team this week

Where in our org are we still defending headcount or budget with "the numbers are good", and if a board evaluated that team the way Oracle's and Meta's just did, would strong performance actually protect it, or would we be one AI-infra reallocation decision away from the same email?

#Try this now

  • Claude Design (Anthropic Labs): Turns natural-language prompts into full prototypes, slide decks, one-pagers, and marketing collateral, no Figma required. On onboarding it reads your codebase and design files and builds a design system (colors, type, components) that it auto-applies to every project after. If you're a PM or marketer who's been blocked waiting on design bandwidth for a quick mock or a stakeholder deck, spend 20 minutes pointing it at a real brief this week and see what a first draft looks like.
  • ActiveCampaign Active Intelligence 2.8: The AI Brand Kit now imports your brand style straight from your company URL (fonts, colors, logo, layout) and holds custom instructions, voice, strategic priorities, across every campaign, automation, and recommendation it generates. Worth testing if your team is still re-explaining "on-brand" to an AI tool every session; this is the pattern (import-once, persist-everywhere) other martech vendors are converging on.
  • Meeting-transcript summarization as a standing habit: With context windows now routinely holding a full day's worth of transcripts, the highest-leverage habit for anyone in a meeting-heavy role is pasting the raw recording/transcript in and asking for decisions made, open questions, and action items by owner. Not new tech, the shift is context windows finally being big enough to make it reliable at real meeting length. Worth turning into a default step after any planning or stakeholder call.

#Case study worth stealing

Alaska Airlines, unified customer data as a loyalty engine, not just a CDP: Alaska Airlines used Amperity's identity resolution to merge siloed booking, loyalty, and app data into single customer profiles, then built dynamic pre-trip messaging (up to four touches per trip, each tuned to that traveler's likely offer, upgrade, meal choice, loyalty enrollment). Result: +198% conversion on loyalty program sign-ups and a 61.1% lift in email open rates from segmentation alone. The transferable move isn't the CDP purchase, it's treating "unify the identity graph" as the precondition for personalization, then building journey-triggered messaging on top rather than one-off campaigns. Any digital org sitting on fragmented profiles (loyalty, app, POS, support) can steal the sequencing: resolve identity first, orchestrate moments second.

#Framework or mental model

Context engineering (vs. prompt engineering): The shift practitioners are naming this year is that the hard problem isn't wording a single prompt well, it's deciding what information an agent or workflow should carry forward and what to actively discard, since irrelevant context degrades output as much as missing context does. LangChain's 2026 state-of-agents data backs this up indirectly: 57% of orgs have agents in production, but quality, not model capability, is the top cited barrier, and observability adoption (89%) is far outpacing evals adoption (52%), meaning most teams can see what their agents did but can't yet systematically judge if it was right. For any leader running AI workflows beyond single prompts, the practical move is to start building even lightweight evals now, you're likely flying on observability alone.

#Rabbit hole

LangChain's "State of Agent Engineering" report is worth the full read if you're evaluating whether to move agent work from pilot to production this quarter, it's the source for the quality-barrier and observability/evals gap stats above, with more granular breakdowns by industry and team size.

Markets Signal284 words

#Macro pulse

Markets pulled back from Q2's record run: S&P 500 -0.22% to 7,483.23, NASDAQ -0.66% to 26,040.03, Dow roughly flat at 52,305 as of Wednesday's close, semiconductor names led the retreat on profit-taking after chip stocks surged 80%+ in H1. TSX reopened today after the Canada Day close, no material catch-up move to report. USD/CAD flat at 1.4216, no impact to Air Canada's international revenue today. WTI crude broke below $68/bbl, its lowest level since late February, as the US-Iran ceasefire holds, a tailwind for airline fuel costs if it sticks.

#Today's industry move

Meta announced it will launch a cloud business to sell its excess AI computing capacity, sending its stock up nearly 9% Wednesday. It's a notable strategic shift, a hyperscaler-adjacent move from a company that has spent years buying compute, not renting it out, and a signal that even the most aggressive AI spenders are looking to monetize capacity they've overbuilt.

#AI infrastructure watch

The semiconductor selloff (Nvidia, AMD, Intel, Marvell all down) is the infra story today, after doubling in H1, chip stocks are digesting concerns that AI capex has outrun near-term demand. Meta's move to resell excess compute is a data point in the same direction: even top-tier buyers may be sitting on more capacity than they can immediately use.

#Signal or noise?

Reads as a pause, not a reversal, a single down day after an 80% run is normal digestion, and Meta's excess-capacity sale is opportunistic monetization, not evidence of a broader infra retreat.

#Watch tomorrow

Whether the chip pullback extends into a second session, that would start to look like a real reassessment of AI capex pacing rather than routine profit-taking.
