Executive Pulse1,267 words

#Macro signal

The consumer cracked in the same week inflation cooled, and only one of those two facts is in most 2027 plans: July retail sales fell 0.6 percent month over month, the steepest drop since May 2025, with sales excluding autos and gasoline down 0.2 percent. The preliminary University of Michigan sentiment reading for August came in at 51.0 against a 54.5 consensus and 55.2 the prior month, an eight percent decline that ended a two-month improvement. Both missed economist estimates. One-year inflation expectations rose to 4.3 percent from 4.2. The strategically important detail is in the composition rather than the headline: the deterioration was described as pervasive across demographic groups with the largest reductions among older consumers, lower-income consumers, and those without a college degree. That is a different signal than a broad, shallow slowdown. It says the softness is concentrated in the cohorts with the least balance sheet buffer, which is where discretionary spending gets cut first and fastest, and it arrives while headline inflation is easing, which removes the usual comfort that relief on prices will restore demand on its own. Sentiment at 51 with cooling CPI is not a story about prices. It is a story about income confidence. For anyone planning next year, the reasonable base case shifts from a recovering consumer to a bifurcating one, where premium and loyal segments hold while the middle and value segments contract. The planning consequence is specific. Blended volume forecasts will be wrong in both directions at once, and any 2027 plan built on a single demand curve should be rebuilt on at least two.

#Tech industry

  • Nvidia has organized more than $500 billion in AI data center financing with six major financial institutions including BlackRock and Goldman Sachs, structured around GPU-backed lending: alongside it, TSMC approved a $29.44 billion capital budget, guided 2026 capex to $60 to $64 billion, at least $4 billion above its prior forecast, raised revenue growth guidance to slightly above 40 percent, and committed an additional $100 billion to Arizona. Hyperscaler capex for the year is tracking somewhere in the $690 billion to $800 billion range depending on the estimate. Strategic implication: the financing structure deserves more board attention than the dollar figure. When the chips themselves become the collateral, the asset's residual value becomes a systemic variable rather than a buyer's private problem, and the entity that controls the depreciation schedule of that collateral is also the entity selling it. That is a concentration of interest with no precedent in enterprise infrastructure. The practical read for a buyer is that the current abundance of AI capacity is being financed on an assumption of durable demand, and financed capacity behaves differently from cash-funded capacity when demand disappoints. It does not get switched off. It gets discounted. Plan for compute prices that are more volatile in both directions than a straight-line cost curve implies, and avoid signing multi-year commitments at today's price on the theory that scarcity persists.
  • Consolidation of measurement and identity infrastructure accelerated to four transactions in a year: Publicis is acquiring LiveRamp, Nielsen is acquiring DoubleVerify for $2.15 billion, IAS went to Novacap at $1.9 billion, and Vista Equity with Quinti Capital has an offer out for Criteo. Combined with Omnicom's merger with IPG inheriting Acxiom's roughly 2.6 billion identities, the independent middle of the measurement market has largely been purchased by the parties being measured or by financial sponsors. Strategic implication: this is a governance question that will arrive at boards disguised as a procurement one. Organizations that spent the past five years building first-party data strategy did so on an assumption of a neutral layer available to verify what platforms report about themselves. That assumption is expiring. The board-level version of the question is simple and worth asking now rather than at renewal: which of our marketing performance numbers are produced by a party with a commercial interest in the result, and what is our independent check. Most large organizations will find the honest answer is that the check was outsourced to a vendor that has since been acquired.

#AI at the leadership level

The gap that defines this phase of enterprise AI is no longer capability, it is organizational adaptation, and the data on it has gotten uncomfortable. Roughly 79 percent of organizations report challenges adopting AI, a double-digit increase over 2025, and 54 percent of C-suite executives say AI adoption is tearing their company apart despite significant investment. Only about a third of surveyed organizations report using AI to deeply transform products, processes, or business models, with the remainder applying it to existing workflows. A three-year study covering 2023 to 2026 lands on the same conclusion from the other direction: adoption stopped being the constraint some time ago and organizational adaptation became it. Most companies responded to AI with training programs rather than by changing how work is structured, which is the cheaper intervention and the one that leaves the operating model untouched. The leadership implication is that AI investment is now producing a measurable split between organizations that changed decision rights, review paths, and team shapes, and organizations that gave everyone a license. The second group is not failing loudly, which is why the problem persists. It is producing exactly the productivity gain you would expect from better tools applied to unchanged processes, calling that the return on AI, and concluding the technology is overhyped. The tell that distinguishes the two groups is not spend or tooling. It is whether any process was retired.

#Leadership & org design

Span of control rose from 8.1 in 2013 to 12.1 in 2025, with credible projections toward 25 by 2028, and roughly 41 percent of companies have cut management layers while 45 percent of remaining middle managers report burnout. The prevailing narrative is that AI permanently flattens hierarchy. Two pieces of evidence complicate it enough to change how a leader should sequence this. First, a 2026 arXiv study on generative AI and organizational structure in the knowledge economy finds a robustly non-monotonic result across all deployment modes: span of control contracts first and expands later as the technology advances. If that shape is right, organizations flattening hardest today are optimizing for a steady state they have not reached, and will need coordination capacity back precisely when they have removed the people who provided it. Second, and more concrete, Meta's retreat from AI-managed mega-teams is a live example of span becoming chaos at the upper bound. The specific structural change underneath all of this is worth stating plainly, because it is the part that survives whichever way the curve goes: the junior-does and senior-reviews pyramid is inverting into agent-does and human-supervises. That inversion breaks the apprenticeship mechanism, since the junior work that AI now absorbs was also how people learned judgment. Organizations cutting the middle layer in 2026 are, in the same motion, dismantling the apparatus that produces their senior leaders in 2028. The defensible move is not to refuse the flattening, it is to make the development pathway explicit and funded rather than leaving it as a byproduct of a work structure that no longer exists.

#Question worth asking your team this week

Which numbers in our performance reporting are produced by a party that gets paid based on what those numbers say, and what would it cost us to verify one of them independently this quarter? Ask for the list before the answer. The exercise fails usefully, because the list is almost always longer than the executive team expects and the verification cost is almost always lower.

#Try this now

  • Give your agent a playbook file instead of a longer prompt: The Agentic Context Engineering paper presented at ICLR 2026 makes a structural argument that is easy to act on. Instead of rewriting a system prompt every time an agent fails, represent context as a collection of structured, itemized bullets and update it incrementally. The paper splits the work across three roles: a Generator that produces the reasoning trajectory, a Reflector that extracts a concrete lesson from what worked and what failed, and a Curator that folds that lesson back into an evolving playbook. The 20 minute version does not require the full architecture. Take the agent or assistant your team uses most, pull ten runs where the output was wrong, and write one bullet per failure describing the specific condition and the correct handling. Store those bullets in a file the agent reads at every run. What changes is the failure mode. A prompt that gets rewritten loses the reason for every previous edit, and teams end up relitigating the same fix quarterly. A playbook accumulates. The related framing worth adopting is that agent failures are usually context failures rather than model failures, which redirects debugging effort away from swapping models and toward what information reached the window and when.
  • Write your context hints before you buy assistant inventory: AdRoll opened a pilot on August 11 letting a selected group of its customers buy sponsored placements inside ChatGPT, aimed at small business, mid-market, and B2B advertisers, roughly six months after OpenAI began selling advertising inside the assistant. The structure is the part worth studying even if you are not in the pilot. Placements are labelled sponsored and visually separated from organic output, and the buying hierarchy is campaign, ad group, ad, where the campaign carries objective, budget, dates, and targeting, and the ad group carries context hints. That middle object is new. In search you bought a keyword, a string the user typed. Here you are describing the conversational situation in which your ad should be considered relevant. Spend an hour writing the ten situations where your product is genuinely the right answer, phrased as a person's circumstance rather than as a query. Not "loyalty program software" but "a mid-size airline whose points expire faster than customers can earn them." That list is the asset. It is also, usefully, the same artifact your positioning work should already have produced, and most teams discover they cannot write it.
  • Check whether an agent can actually transact with you: Instant Checkout in ChatGPT runs on the Agentic Commerce Protocol, an open standard co-developed by Stripe and OpenAI. Etsy sellers are live, over a million Shopify merchants including Glossier, SKIMS, Spanx, and Vuori are being onboarded, and PayPal's ACP server is set to bring tens of millions of small businesses onto the pattern this year. The protocol is deliberately not Stripe-exclusive, so businesses on other payment providers can adopt it, and many Stripe merchants enable agentic checkout with a configuration change. The practical audit takes an afternoon. Ask an assistant to complete your highest-value transaction end to end and watch where it stalls. Most orgs fail well before payment, at product data that is not machine readable, at inventory or availability that only exists behind a session, or at a policy that cannot be expressed as a rule. Note that this applies past retail. Any transaction with a catalog, an availability check, and a price, which includes a seat, a room, a policy, or an appointment, is inside this pattern.

#Case study worth stealing

Shopify, treating AI referral as a measured channel rather than a narrative: In its Q2 2026 results Shopify reported revenue up 34 percent year over year to $3.58 billion, and management specifically called out that AI-attributed traffic and orders tripled year over year. The Q1 disclosure was sharper still: AI-referred orders ran nearly 13 times higher year over year, converting at roughly 50 percent above organic search, while fully agent-executed transactions accounted for about 3 percent of orders. Read those three numbers together, because the pairing is the lesson. Agent-driven traffic is small in absolute terms and growing at a rate no other channel matches, and it converts far better than the channel most marketing budgets are still built around. The tactic worth stealing is not the technology, it is the instrumentation. Shopify could say this because it separated agent-referred sessions from general traffic early enough to build a trend line, which means it can now defend investment in that channel with evidence instead of conviction. Most organizations cannot answer the equivalent question about their own funnel today, and the reason is prosaic: assistant referrals arrive with thin or missing referrer data and get bucketed into direct traffic, where they are invisible and unarguable. The counterweight to hold alongside this is that independent August data shows AI-referred shoppers arriving with high purchase intent and then converting poorly on the destination site, which suggests the gap is often in what happens after the handoff rather than in the channel itself. Either way, the diagnosis requires the same first step. Segment the traffic, then argue.

#Framework or mental model

Write, Select, Compress, Isolate: A four-operation model for how information moves through an agent workflow, useful because it turns a vague complaint about an agent being unreliable into a question with four possible answers. Write covers persistent task state, and the decision is whether that state lives in the context window or in an external store. Select covers what enters the window when retrieval is noisy, which is the operation most teams skip entirely by dumping everything relevant and hoping. Compress covers reducing context, with the important sequencing constraint that compression is safe only after the decisive facts have been extracted and structured, never before. Isolate covers separating contexts when domains, tools, or sub-agents would otherwise collide. Where digital leaders can apply it: the next time a team says the AI is not working, ask which of the four is failing. In practice most failures are Select problems presented as model problems, and the fix is a retrieval decision rather than a vendor decision. The framework also has a non-AI use. Substitute a new hire for the agent and the same four questions describe onboarding, which is a fair test of whether the model is real or just vocabulary.

#Rabbit hole

The evidence on organizational flattening is more complicated than the headlines. Span of control moved from 8.1 in 2013 to 12.1 in 2025, with projections toward 25 by 2028 and roughly 41 percent of companies cutting management layers. But a 2026 arXiv paper on generative AI and organizational structure in the knowledge economy finds a non-monotonic result across every deployment mode studied: the span of control contracts first and expands later as the technology advances. If that holds, the organizations cutting layers fastest right now are acting on the second half of a curve whose first half they have not yet lived through. The companion argument worth reading alongside it is the succession problem, that eliminating middle management in 2026 also dismantles the apparatus that produces senior leaders in 2028. Two hours, and the useful output is not a position on flattening but a clearer question about which specific coordination work your middle layer currently performs that nothing else in the system does.

#Vendor & platform moves

  • AdRoll opens a reseller channel into ChatGPT inventory: A pilot announced August 11 lets a selected group of AdRoll and AdRoll ABM customers buy sponsored placements inside ChatGPT, positioned explicitly at small business, mid-market, and B2B advertisers, roughly six months after OpenAI began selling ads in the assistant. Placements are labelled and visually separated from organic output, and the buying object model is campaign, ad group, ad, with the ad group carrying context hints rather than keywords. Signal: assistant inventory has entered the classic channel structure, which is the moment a media property stops being an experiment. Direct sales to large advertisers proves demand exists. A reseller tier proves the inventory needs volume, and volume comes from the long tail. Two consequences follow for anyone planning 2027 budgets. First, the pricing discovery period is ending, because a channel with thousands of small advertisers bidding sets a clearing price a handful of direct deals never could. Second, the targeting primitive is genuinely new. Buying a described situation rather than a typed string means the creative brief and the media brief converge into one document, and the org structure that separates those two functions gets awkward fast.
  • DeepLumen crosses 1,000 merchants on Agentic Page and moves to cost per sale: The August 13 announcement reports more than a thousand merchants live, AI-driven orders attributed back to participating brands, and a performance-based cost-per-sale model entering commercial rollout, where merchants pay only when a sale is attributed to AI-driven discovery. Signal: the pricing model is the story, not the merchant count. A vendor moving to cost per sale is making a claim it can be held to, that it can attribute an agent-originated sale credibly enough to invoice against it. That is a much stronger assertion than a SaaS subscription for the same capability, and it puts commercial pressure on the attribution question the whole agentic commerce category has so far deferred. Watch how the attribution window and the counterfactual are defined in the contract, because performance pricing without an incrementality test is a mechanism for charging a toll on demand that would have arrived anyway, which is exactly the criticism that took a decade to stick to branded search and affiliate.
  • The neutral middle of adtech continues to be bought: Publicis agreed to acquire LiveRamp for roughly $2.2 to $2.55 billion depending on the reported structure, with close expected by the end of 2026, folding identity and clean room technology into Epsilon's existing identity assets. Vista Equity Partners and Quinti Capital have a takeover offer out for Criteo. IAS went to Novacap at $1.9 billion. Nielsen's $2.15 billion acquisition of DoubleVerify was announced this week. Signal: four independent measurement and identity vendors changed or are changing hands inside a single year, and three of the four buyers are financial sponsors or agency holding companies rather than platforms. That specific buyer mix matters. A platform buying a verification vendor would raise an obvious conflict and probably fail review. A holding company buying identity raises a quieter one, which is that the entity resolving your customers to profiles now also sells you the media those profiles are targeted with. The independence that made these vendors purchasable is the same property their new owners cannot preserve indefinitely.

#Category watch

Identity resolution, and the disappearance of the neutral option: The structural shift is that identity assets are consolidating into agency holding companies rather than into platforms. Publicis holds Epsilon and Lotame and is adding LiveRamp, which itself absorbed Habu for clean room capability in 2024. Omnicom's merger with IPG inherits Acxiom and its roughly 2.6 billion global identities. WPP's GroupM took InfoSum. The identity market now spans thirty-plus vendors across data bureaus, adtech identity providers, CDPs, clean rooms, and specialists, with more than $25 billion in M&A since 2018, and the segment shrinking fastest is the genuinely independent middle. For anyone building a martech org, this changes a buying decision that used to be routine. Selecting an identity layer used to be a technical evaluation of match rates and coverage. It is now a question of whose commercial interests sit behind the match. If your identity provider is owned by the holding company that also plans your media, you have consolidated two functions that exist to check each other. The defensible responses are limited and worth naming: run identity in-house on your own authenticated base, keep a second independent measurement source you pay for separately, or accept the consolidation and negotiate for contractual data portability and audit rights before the close rather than after.

#Stack intelligence

Agentic commerce readiness is turning out to be a data-quality project wearing a protocol's clothes. The Agentic Commerce Protocol is an open standard co-developed by Stripe and OpenAI, adoptable by businesses using other payment providers, and many Stripe merchants can enable agentic checkout with a configuration change. Etsy is live, over a million Shopify merchants are being brought on, and PayPal's ACP server extends the pattern to tens of millions of small businesses. The integration is therefore not the hard part, and treating it as an engineering roadmap item misreads where the work is. What actually gates an organization is whether its product, availability, and policy data can be read and trusted by a system that will not tolerate ambiguity and cannot ask a clarifying question of a merchandiser. Agents fail on attributes that are missing, inconsistent across channels, or expressed only in prose on a page. The practical consequence is that the product information layer, historically the least glamorous system in the stack and the first one deferred in a replatform, has become the constraint on a new revenue channel. Enterprises with a mature PIM and a clean policy model will connect in weeks. Enterprises whose catalog truth lives in three systems and a spreadsheet will spend a year on remediation and describe it as a protocol integration. Budget accordingly, and note that the same readiness work pays out twice, since the data quality that lets an agent transact is the data quality that lets an assistant cite you.

#Foundations concept of the day

Marketing mix modeling: A statistical method for estimating how much each marketing input contributed to a business outcome, using aggregate historical data rather than tracking individuals. The mechanics: you assemble a time series, usually two to three years of weekly data, containing spend by channel, plus the outcome you care about such as revenue or bookings, plus the external factors that also move that outcome, price, promotions, seasonality, competitor activity, weather, macro conditions. A regression then estimates a coefficient for each input, answering how much incremental outcome each additional dollar in each channel produced, with adjustments for two effects that make marketing unlike other spend: adstock, meaning advertising keeps working after the week it ran, and saturation, meaning the tenth million in a channel does less than the first. The output is a decomposition of results by driver and a set of response curves you can optimize a budget against. Why it matters at scale: MMM needs no cookies, no device identifiers, no user-level data at all, which makes it structurally immune to the privacy changes, platform walls, and consent losses that have been degrading click-based attribution for years. It is also the only method that can price the channels that were never trackable in the first place, television, out of home, sponsorship, and now assistant-referred demand that arrives with no usable referrer. Its weaknesses are equally structural. It is slow, typically refreshed quarterly, it cannot tell you which creative or which audience worked, and it is highly sensitive to whether you included the confounding variable that actually explained the lift. Cross-industry example: a hotel group finds its last-click model crediting branded search with a large share of direct bookings, while an MMM built on the same period shows branded search almost entirely capturing demand generated weeks earlier by a broadcast campaign the attribution model valued at nearly zero. The budget shift that follows is not a small optimization, it is a reallocation between teams. The reason this decades-old technique is having a revival is that it is the measurement approach least affected by who owns the identity layer, which, given how much of that layer was bought this year, is now a governance property rather than a statistical one.

#Watch list

  • Whether the ChatGPT ad channel opens beyond pilot resellers this year: the pilot is deliberately narrow, restricted to selected AdRoll and AdRoll ABM customers and explicitly not broadly available. The date that matters is general availability, because that is when the assistant becomes a planned line in mid-market budgets rather than a test, and when comparable cost data starts existing. If that happens before Q4 planning closes, a meaningful share of 2027 performance budget moves before anyone has a benchmark to defend the move with.
  • Whether cost per sale becomes the default price of agent-originated demand: one vendor moving to performance pricing is a competitive tactic. Three would be a category norm, and it would reframe agentic commerce from a technology purchase into a media buy, complete with the take rate question that follows. The number to look for in any such contract is not the rate, it is the attribution window and whether an incrementality holdout is permitted. A channel that refuses holdouts is pricing correlation.

#Macro pulse

Markets are closed for the weekend. The read below is Friday's close, which is what carries into Monday.

Canada: The S&P/TSX Composite finished at 36,730.27, down 29.02 points or 0.08 percent, effectively flat. USD/CAD fell to 1.3872, a 0.40 percent move in the Canadian dollar's favour on the week's benign US inflation data, with both CPI and PPI landing below expectations. A stronger loonie is a mixed input for a Canadian carrier. It reduces the CAD cost of USD-denominated fuel, leases, and aircraft, and it compresses the CAD value of US and international point-of-sale revenue. At 1.39 the move is not yet large enough to change hedging posture, but the direction is now working against translated international revenue for the first time in several weeks.

United States: All three indexes slipped from Wednesday's record. The S&P 500 fell 13.23 points or 0.2 percent to 7,785.76, the Dow fell 107.58 points or 0.2 percent to 53,732.41, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 73.86 points or 0.3 percent to 26,729.16. Despite the down day, the S&P advanced 0.4 percent on the week for a third consecutive weekly gain and the Nasdaq eked out a third weekly gain of its own at 0.1 percent, while the Dow closed the week 0.6 percent lower. The data was the story rather than the tape. July retail sales fell 0.6 percent, the steepest drop in more than a year, and preliminary August University of Michigan sentiment came in at 51.0 against a 54.5 estimate and 55.2 prior, with one-year inflation expectations ticking up to 4.3 percent. Both missed. Index levels near records sitting on top of a consumer print that weak is the tension to carry into next week.

Asia: Divergent. The Nikkei 225 rose about 0.6 percent to 68,713.80. The Hang Seng fell 279.66 points or 1.10 percent to 25,116.85 as offshore investors trimmed China exposure. The Shanghai Composite fell roughly 0.5 percent to around 3,908. The pattern is a preference for technology-weighted markets over China-exposed equities rather than a regional risk move.

Oil: WTI rose 1.42 percent to $82.40 on Friday and gained close to 5 percent on the week, comfortably past the 2 percent flag. The driver is the Strait of Hormuz, where talks remain deadlocked and Washington has signalled unprecedented economic measures alongside a continued naval blockade of Iranian ports, with further announcements expected next week. This is the single most consequential line in today's brief for an airline. Jet fuel in Europe has roughly doubled year over year to around $187 per barrel, European airports have warned of a systemic jet fuel shortage if the strait does not reopen, Lufthansa has already cut 20,000 short-haul flights through October in part on fuel costs, and United has said publicly it expects oil above $100 through the end of 2027.

#Today's industry move

The measurement and identity layer of adtech consolidated further, with the week's transactions now forming a pattern rather than a sequence. Nielsen agreed to acquire DoubleVerify for $2.15 billion all cash at $13.60 per share, closing between Q4 2026 and Q1 2027 and creating a company with more than $4 billion in expected annual revenue. That sits alongside Publicis acquiring LiveRamp at roughly $2.2 to $2.55 billion, IAS taken private by Novacap at $1.9 billion, and a Vista Equity and Quinti Capital takeover offer for Criteo at around a 30 percent premium. Four independent verification and identity vendors changed hands or agreed to in one year, and the buyers are agency holding companies and financial sponsors rather than platforms. The valuation signal is that independent measurement is being priced as a scarce strategic asset at the exact moment machines take over the buying decision.

#AI infrastructure watch

Two financing signals worth separating from the capex headlines. Nvidia has assembled more than $500 billion in AI data center financing with six major institutions including BlackRock and Goldman Sachs, structured around GPU-backed lending, which makes the chips themselves the collateral for the buildout they enable. Separately Nvidia put $3 billion into Lancium, tying compute capacity directly to power infrastructure. On the manufacturing side TSMC approved a $29.44 billion capital budget, raised 2026 capex guidance to $60 to $64 billion, at least $4 billion above prior, lifted revenue growth guidance above 40 percent, committed a further $100 billion to Arizona, and announced a $6.4 billion image sensor joint venture with Sony in Japan. The binding constraint continues to migrate down the stack toward power. The US Department of Energy projects data centers reach up to 12 percent of national electrical demand by 2028, which is why compute investment now routinely arrives packaged with an energy transaction.

#Signal or noise?

Signal on both. Oil at $82 and rising on a blockade with no diplomatic path is a structural cost input for aviation, not a weekly fluctuation, and the consumer prints are the second consecutive month of evidence that discretionary demand is softening in the cohorts that fill leisure cabins. Those two moving together is the combination that compresses airline margins from both ends at once.

#Watch tomorrow

The promised US announcement on additional economic measures against Iran. Anything that hardens the blockade puts WTI through $85 and makes the European jet fuel shortage a scheduling problem for the autumn rather than a pricing one, and any credible signal that the strait reopens takes several dollars out of crude in a session.
