#Macro signal

Goldman says the sell-off has widened from an AI unwind to a growth downgrade, and Wednesday decides the next leg. After seven straight losses, the S&P 500 sits about 12 percent below its 6 October record and the VIX closed Friday at 35.89. Goldman's note said the decline "expanded this week from an AI unwind to a broader growth downgrade" and named three things that would mark a bottom: news that restores confidence in AI earnings, a dovish Fed signal, or a large drop in investor positioning. Futures price roughly a 95 percent chance of a rate hike on 9 December. October core PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation measure and the last reading before that meeting, arrives Wednesday with consensus near 3.2 percent year over year. One counter-signal: the final University of Michigan sentiment reading for November rose to 51.5, with year-ahead inflation expectations falling from 4.7 to 3.7 percent. Why it matters: a growth downgrade reaches every budget, where an AI unwind reached only the AI lines. For tech, product and marketing leaders, the 2027 plan should carry a version with higher borrowing costs and softer demand, decided now, before finance writes it for them. (Fortune, FXStreet, Trading Economics)

#Tech industry

  • Nvidia is now managing regulators, a board inquiry and a founder campaign at once. Its quarterly filing is delayed, an independent board committee is investigating, the SEC is examining its accounting, and California's attorney general subpoenaed the company on Thursday over chip flows to China and investor disclosures. The Treasury Secretary said he wants answers from the board and outside counsel within ten days. Interim CEO Colette Kress says Nvidia will stop selling H200 chips in China and has no plans for Jensen Huang to return, while outside voices circulate a petition for exactly that. The stock is down 47.5 percent since 6 October. Strategic implication: the most important supplier in the AI stack will spend the next quarter on governance. Any 2027 plan that assumes stable GPU pricing and allocation should name what changes if that assumption fails. (Yahoo Finance, TechCrunch, Fortune)
  • Meta's own staff called its agent "an extreme privacy violation." Internal posts seen by Reuters describe the Muse assistant sending unprompted messages that appeared to draw on private information: a reminder to reply to a sister on Signal, and bank balances volunteered during a chat about fishing weather. Meta says employees run different versions from the public and that notification access is opt-in. A member of its superintelligence team said the messages were hallucinations produced by built-in prompts designed to re-engage users between sessions, which "bias towards the most uncomfortable topics." Muse has more than 10 million downloads since September. Strategic implication: an assistant told to start conversations will reach for whatever feels most personal, true or not. Any company adding proactive messaging to an AI product should treat the re-engagement prompt as a regulated design decision, reviewed like a marketing claim. (Fortune)

#AI at the leadership level

"Authorization to spend is not authorization to judge." That line, from Shlomit Wagman of Harvard Kennedy School writing in Fortune, names the gap in agent payments now that ChatGPT Wallet is live. Today each purchase still needs a human tap. Her argument is that approval will move from the transaction to the instruction, and once it does, every payment can be authorized while the outcome is wrong. Her example is a $5,000 family trip where each charge was approved and the bookings were still the wrong ones. OpenAI's terms leave delivery and returns with merchants and keep card dispute rights, but do not clearly say who bears a loss caused by the agent's judgment. Google's payments protocol records signed mandates, which she calls necessary and insufficient. For a board, the question is practical: when an agent books the wrong fare or the wrong room with valid authorization, the customer will come to the merchant. Decide now whether that is handled as a refund, a dispute or a goodwill gesture, and who owns the policy. (Fortune)

#Leadership & org design

One CEO got full AI training completion by pairing paid time with a review consequence. Job van der Voort, CEO of HR platform Remote (about 2,000 employees), gave every employee five paid hours to complete an AI course he built, and told them that skipping it would mean not receiving "meeting expectations" in their performance review. "So everybody did it." Fortune does not report what the course covers or what changed in the work afterward. The design is still worth noting because it does the two things most AI upskilling programs avoid: it takes the time out of the company's budget instead of the employee's evenings, and it attaches the requirement to the one process everyone takes seriously. The open question for anyone copying it is the measure. Completion shows compliance. The next step is a review criterion tied to work that was done differently. (Fortune)

#Question worth asking your team this week

When an AI agent acting for a customer makes a valid payment for the wrong thing, what is our policy, who owns it, and would frontline staff give the same answer?

#Try this now

  • Mark a baseline break on YouTube Shorts conversions: Google's help page on YouTube ad metrics now counts a tap on like, comment or share on a Shorts ad toward engagements, TrueView views and engaged-view conversions, PPC Land reports (first flagged by PPC News Feed on 21 November). Before, the engaged-view credit needed five seconds of viewing or a call-to-action click. TrueView views are the billing unit for cost-per-view bidding, so a like two seconds in is billed like a 10-second view. The page carries no effective date and does not say whether history is restated or how Smart Bidding treats the extra conversions. This week: annotate every Shorts report from 21 November, compare engaged-view conversions per impression before and after, and do not present any lift in Shorts conversions as performance until the definition change is separated out. (PPC Land)
  • Check how long your sale prices have been running before Black Friday: Google's Merchant Center help page on sale price annotations now says that for a sale to count as a temporary reduction, the sale price "should not exceed 30 days," and the base price "should ideally be the sole active price at times." The existing rules stand: the discount must be more than 5 percent and less than 90 percent. The wording is "should," and Google does not say what happens to a longer sale, but the risk is losing the sale badge in the week it matters most. Pull the feed, list every product whose sale_price has been live for more than 30 days, and reset or end those before the holiday rules go in. (PPC Land)
  • Split paid search results into new queries and existing ones: Agency New Path Digital compared nearly 50,000 keyword and search-term pairs across three Google Ads lead-generation accounts. Queries newly matched to phrase-match keywords cost $209 per lead against $163 for queries already matching (28 percent more), because conversion rate fell from 10 to 8 percent. New exact-match queries cost slightly less ($109 against $116). The share of phrase-match volume from queries sharing no word with the keyword rose from 2 to 9 percent. It is three accounts, correlational, and measured on form fills, so treat the numbers as a prompt, and the method as the thing to copy: export 90 days of search terms, tag each as seen before or new, and compare cost per lead for the two groups by match type. If new phrase-match queries are the expensive group, that is where negatives and tighter match types pay back. (PPC Land)

#Case study worth stealing

Zillow and Canvas Worldwide, an agent that proposes and a person who approves: Zillow's agency uses an agentic layer called Talos to decide whether a campaign is underpacing or overpacing, with behavioral signals arriving through an MCP connection to audience firm Dstillery. The agent does not push changes live. A Canvas team member approves or denies each one. Zillow's brief is an outcome, separating renters ready to move from people browsing ("We are known for dreaming," says SVP of marketing Ravi Kandikonda), and the agency runs the tooling. No performance figures were shared, so the result is unproven. The structure is the part to adapt: the client sets the outcome, the agent proposes a specific change with its reasoning, a named person signs off, and every approval or denial becomes a record of where the agent can later be trusted to act alone. (AdExchanger)

#Framework or mental model

The knowledge factory: LangChain's Harrison Chase argued on 21 November that what holds enterprise agents back is knowledge, more than models or context size. He splits it into three kinds: skills (how the work is done), domain knowledge (how the business works) and data access guidance (where the current information lives). The factory has three parts: a way for agents to flag knowledge that is missing or wrong, a curator agent that runs on a schedule and reads those flags along with traces and raw sources, and a store the agents read from. His example: an agent building a revenue report flags a renamed database column, and the curator updates two skills that night. "The goal isn't to document everything before agents can start working." The post reports no measured results. Digital leaders can apply it to any internal agent that keeps making the same mistake: give it a channel to report the gap, and assign an owner, human or agent, who closes gaps on a schedule. (LangChain)

#Rabbit hole

Lily Ray on why named experts outlast every search shortcut: A roughly 6,000-word essay, published 19 November, tracing Google's guidance from its 2003 webmaster guidelines to this October's passage on fabricated creator profiles. Her argument: bought links, AI listicles and scaled AI content each worked for a while, and each could be scaled, which is why each was eventually discounted. "A real expert's reputation can't." She cites Ahrefs data putting YouTube at 24.5 percent and Reddit at 18.1 percent of top AI Overview citations, and a Semrush study in which individual members account for 59 percent of cited LinkedIn content in ChatGPT Search and AI Mode. Much of the evidence is from her own clients and she sells consulting on this approach. Still worth the hour for any team deciding whether the brand or its people should be the visible author. (PPC Land)

#Vendor & platform moves

  • AppLovin's block on new ad lines reportedly reaches Google Ad Manager: MonetizeMore CEO Kean Graham said on 19 November that AppLovin's halt on new waterfall zones outside its own MAX mediation platform also applies to Google Ad Manager, alongside Unity LevelPlay and Google AdMob. Existing lines keep serving, "but the floors are frozen, so no new price points." AppLovin has not confirmed, dated or explained the change. Graham's claim that hybrid setups earn 10 to 20 percent more than bidding alone is his own firm's average with no method published, an AppLovin analyst disputed it in a personal capacity, and Graham sells competing optimization products. Signal: A company that owns both a large source of ad demand and the mediation layer can use the first to pull publishers into the second. For any app publisher, including airline and retail apps that carry ads, the dependency to map is which demand sources are only reachable on the seller's terms. (PPC Land)
  • Ampersand is handing its independent agency business to its owners: The TV ad sales joint venture set up in 2019 by Comcast, Charter and Cox will move independent agency accounts to Comcast Advertising and Charter's Spectrum Reach from 1 January, AdExchanger reported on 20 November. Ampersand confirmed the change and that roles are affected, declined to say how many, denied it is shutting down, and will focus on holding companies and political advertising. Signal: The shared sales layer that let a buyer reach several cable operators through one door is being unwound, and the operators are taking the direct relationships back. Expect more buying points in local and addressable TV, and more first-party data kept inside each operator. (AdExchanger)
  • Advertisers paused TikTok in Spain after ads ran next to child abuse material: Digiday found videos containing such material, or pointing to sites selling it, running before or after ads from 21 brands, including Banco Santander, Samsung, Ralph Lauren and Pringles. Agency Mediaplus Equmedia says three clients have paused. "Until we know what's happening, it's better to pause," said its head of digital. TikTok says the content is "categorically prohibited" and that it has a task force. Signal: Adjacency in a feed is still not something a platform can guarantee or a brand can inspect ahead of time. The control that worked here was the oldest one: a pause decision the agency could make in hours. (AdExchanger)

#Category watch

Consent and data rights in AI chat products: Late on 20 November, 42 states and the District of Columbia asked the Delaware bankruptcy court to block the sale of Character.ai users' chats on the terms proposed, with Texas filing separately. The 44-page objection asks that six categories of data, all chats, and any models trained on chats be excluded unless users consent after direct notice. The buyer was formed on 13 November with undisclosed owners and has put down a $1 million deposit on a $10 million price. The filing cites more than 21.6 million US accounts, over 2.1 million of them with a declared age under 18. "Bankruptcy does not suspend the law that governs this protected data," the states write. The court has not ruled, and the sale hearing is set for 3 and 4 December. For anyone building a martech org, this is the first large test of whether conversational data, and the models trained on it, can change hands without fresh consent. The outcome will set expectations for every contract that sends customer conversations to an AI vendor. (PPC Land)

#Stack intelligence

Marketing data held by vendors is the exposed surface. Chase said this week that third-party technology vendors were hit by a cyberattack and that "the attackers accessed certain marketing data held by those vendors." It has not named the vendors. Bell said an "agentic threat actor" took business contact data from a system used to provision enterprise circuits on 17 November. In both cases the core systems held, and the copy of the data sitting in a supporting or supplier system did not. Marketing stacks create more of those copies than any other function: audience files at agencies, suppression lists at email vendors, match files at measurement partners. The practical pattern is to keep a register of every vendor holding a customer extract, with what it holds, how long it keeps it and who can delete it, and to shorten retention on the extracts no one is using. (AdExchanger, The Globe and Mail)

#Foundations concept of the day

View-through and engaged-view conversions: A click-through conversion is the simple case: someone clicks an ad and later buys. Most video and display ads are never clicked, so platforms also count conversions from people who only saw the ad. A view-through conversion credits an ad that was shown to someone who later converted, usually within a set window such as one day. An engaged-view conversion is a stricter version for video: the person must have watched for a minimum time (on YouTube, 10 seconds for most formats and 5 for Shorts) before converting within the window. It matters at scale because these counts are defined by the platform selling the media, they feed its automated bidding, and they can move a channel's reported return more than any creative change. Two cautions follow. First, a view is not proof of cause: a loyal customer who would have bought anyway still gets counted, which is why a holdout test, comparing people who could see the ad with a matched group who could not, is the real check. Second, the definition can change. This week Google's help page began counting a tap on like, comment or share on a Shorts ad as enough for an engaged view. Cross-industry example: a streaming service running video ads for a new season will see far more view-through than click-through sign-ups, and should set the view window and test incrementality before using those numbers to move budget from search to video. (PPC Land)

#Watch list

  • "Free" as a regulated word in the EU: On 19 November an Advocate General at the EU's top court proposed that handing over personal data counts as a "cost," so a service that takes data beyond what it needs to operate cannot be described as free. The case concerns Facebook's 2015 claim that it "is and remains free." The opinion is not binding and no judgment date is set, but it lands a week after the Commission proposed a Digital Fairness Act with fines of up to 6 percent of worldwide turnover. Loyalty programs and apps that advertise free membership in exchange for data should watch the ruling. (PPC Land)
  • Google writing its own description of your ad: Google is testing AI-generated text in mobile search ads: a summary sentence above a group of sponsored results, and a generated line under each ad. In the reported example the summary told the searcher what the four advertisers were not. Google has not confirmed the test, its scale, or whether advertisers can opt out. If it ships, the platform's wording will sit above the advertiser's own copy. (PPC Land)

#Today's industry move

American Airlines flew its last long-haul international first-class flight, and the replacement is a bigger business cabin. The final flight left Sydney for Los Angeles on Friday. American's reconfigured 777-300ER carries 70 business suites. Delta and United had already made the same change. CNBC priced a Los Angeles to London one-way this week at $12,994 in business and $15,669 in first, a small premium for a cabin that sold few seats. United's CFO said Thursday, "I don't think we found the limit on segmentation." The premium bet is now on more sellable tiers inside business class, and it is being placed in the same week the equity wealth that funds premium leisure travel fell sharply. (CNBC)

#AI infrastructure watch

  • Nvidia ended its worst week since October 2008 with more regulators involved. The stock lost about $490 billion in value Friday and nearly $900 billion on the week, then rose nearly 4 percent after hours. California's attorney general subpoenaed the company Thursday. Its quarterly filing is delayed and a board committee is investigating. Interim CEO Colette Kress says H200 sales to China will stop. Chip stocks had their worst week since 2001. (Yahoo Finance, TechCrunch)
  • SpaceX's $74 billion data center plan is meeting local resistance over power. The plan is about $50 billion in Cochise County, Arizona, drawing 6,000 MW at full build, and more than $24 billion at Starbase, Texas, where regulators approved 35 gas turbines producing 750 MW on 16 November. Cameron County commissioners voted 3 to 2 against negotiating a ten-year full property tax abatement, and Arizona's attorney general asked that a 9 December county vote be postponed. Who pays for the grid and water work is not settled. (Fortune)

#Signal or noise?

Signal: capital is still being committed to AI capacity at the same moment its main supplier is under investigation and local governments are starting to say no, so the constraint on AI is shifting from chips to governance and power.
