#Macro signal

Stocks had their best day since April 2025, and the price of money did not move. The S&P 500 rose 4.73 percent on Monday and the Nasdaq 6.51 percent after Nvidia reinstated its CEO. The 10-year Treasury yield stayed at 4.92 percent. CME FedWatch put the odds of a quarter-point Fed hike on 9 December at about 98 percent, up from 88 percent on Friday, and New York Fed President John Williams said inflation near 3.5 percent means the Fed has "work to do." The flash US composite PMI slipped to 54.6 from 55.9, and futures were lower again before Tuesday's open. One company's leadership news repaired sentiment. It did nothing to borrowing costs, the consumer, or inflation. Leaders finalizing 2027 plans should keep the rate and demand assumptions they held on Friday, and should expect the PCE inflation print on Wednesday to matter more to those plans than Monday's rally. (CappNotes, CNBC)

#Tech industry

  • Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang, and said Washington was consulted. The board voted unanimously on 23 November, eleven days after Huang left following reports of affairs, one with a woman US officials reportedly consider a Chinese intelligence asset. Its statement said it had "received input from senior levels of the United States government" and that the return "poses no national security risk." President Trump said he spoke with directors and "convinced them." The board's independent investigation continues, and Huang testifies to the Senate Banking Committee on 3 December. The same day, the Commerce Department blacklisted Megaspeed, the Singapore company at the center of the chip diversion probe, and six affiliates. Shares rose 31.48 percent, adding about $952 billion in value. The strategic point for any board: when a company is treated as national infrastructure, the government becomes a participant in its governance, and the directors' room to decide alone shrinks. (CNBC, Globe and Mail, Fox Business)
  • CrowdStrike agreed to buy Zscaler for $43.9 billion in stock. Each Zscaler share converts into 1.135 CrowdStrike shares. The combined company expects more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue at closing, which is roughly a year away and needs regulatory approval. The pitch is one platform covering devices and network access so customers can "safely adopt AI." CrowdStrike's shares fell 8.92 percent. Security is consolidating into a few platforms at the moment companies are giving AI agents access to their systems, so the security vendor decision is becoming a ten-year platform choice of the same weight as the cloud provider. (CrowdStrike, TechCrunch)

#AI at the leadership level

Customers' AI agents have started transacting, and impostors arrived with them. Cequence's first Agentic AI Security Index, covering 16 May to 15 November at the businesses it protects, found the share of agent visits that signed in, booked, bought or managed an account rose from 1.4 percent to 13 percent. In travel it was 59 percent. Over the same period, bots posing as agents to hunt for credentials and configuration files grew 2,600 percent, and 63 percent of visits claiming to be Perplexity were doing that. Cequence sells security for this problem and gives no total visit counts, so the direction matters more than the exact figures. The governance consequence is that "block or allow AI agents" is no longer one decision. A company now has customers arriving through software, and it needs a named owner for which agents are admitted, how each is verified, and what each may do, with the commercial and security leads deciding together. (Cequence)

#Leadership & org design

"Too big to fire" is an org design failure, and Nvidia showed its cost in both directions. On the earnings call days after Huang left, chair Harvey Jones said the board did "not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." Within a week it reversed. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon called the back and forth "clown-like" and said, "We all knew that there was key man risk here." Dow Jones's Angela Mancini asked what it means to have "a CEO too big to fire" and noted that Huang has 60 direct reports. Former Nvidia executive Jeff Herbst urged the board to add directors who have run large companies and to develop successors formally. The lesson is specific. Sixty direct reports suggests no layer of general managers was built, which would leave no one below the CEO who had run enough of the company to take over. A board that cannot replace a leader cannot hold that leader to account either. The test for any leadership team is whether each top role has someone who has already run a meaningful share of it. (CNBC, Yahoo Finance)

#Question worth asking your team this week

If our most important leader were gone tomorrow, who has already run a large enough part of their job to take it over, and if the answer is no one, what would we have to restructure in 2027 to change that?

#Try this now

  • Split your agent traffic into "read" and "acted" before Black Friday: Cequence's first Agentic AI Security Index, drawn from six months of traffic (16 May to 15 November) at the businesses it protects, found that the share of AI agent visits that signed in, booked, bought or managed an account rose from 1.4 percent to 13 percent. In travel, 59 percent of agent visits took an action, mostly bookings. The share ending in a purchase at retailers went from 0.2 percent to 3.0 percent during early holiday deals. The same data shows bots that only claim to be agents: 63 percent of visits presenting as Perplexity were hunting for keys, credentials and admin pages. The 20-minute job is to ask analytics or security for one table: visits by claimed agent name, split into read-only and acted, with a column for requests to config or admin paths. That table tells a product team which agents are customers and which are intruders using a familiar name. Cequence sells agent security and does not publish total visit counts. (Cequence)
  • Check that this week's promo email survives the AI inbox summary: Inbox assistants now summarize an email before the person opens it, and if the offer and the code are inside an image, the summary can miss them. Stensul shipped a pre-send check for this on 23 November, along with Gmail Promotions annotations that show a promo code in the tab itself. The manual version needs no tool: send the Black Friday email to a test Gmail and a test Apple Mail account, read only the generated summary, and confirm the discount, the deadline and the code are all there. If any is missing, move it into live text in the first lines. Stensul cites a survey in which 22 percent of millennials bought from an email they never opened, with no sample size given. (Business Wire, Agile Brand Guide)
  • Ask your streaming TV buyer one question about authorized sellers: HUMAN Security disclosed on 23 November that a botnet on cheap Android TV boxes sent about 40 billion fake bid requests a day at its peak, each naming a well-known streaming app. HUMAN says those requests came through sellers that the real apps never listed in their app-ads.txt and sellers.json files. The question for the agency or the buying platform is: are we bidding only on connected TV supply where the seller is listed in the app's app-ads.txt, and can you show last week's spend that was not? Holiday budgets are live now, so ask today. (PPC Land, ExchangeWire)

#Case study worth stealing

Target, reporting its shopping assistant as a conversion number: Target launched Ask Target, an AI shopping assistant, in beta in October. On the 18 November earnings call, CEO Michael Fiddelke told analysts that customers move to purchase at "about twice the rate" through Ask Target compared with elsewhere, and said, "It's important for us to be at the forefront of any changes to the consumer purchase funnel." Target did not define the comparison group, the period or the sales involved, so the figure is a claim to test. The part worth adapting is the choice of metric. Within weeks of a beta, the assistant was being described to investors by purchase rate, where most teams report sessions or satisfaction scores. Any team with an assistant in market can do the same with more rigor: pick the purchase or booking rate, compare assistant sessions against similar sessions without it, and state the baseline in the same sentence as the result. (CX Dive)

#Framework or mental model

The working media waterfall: Start with a media budget and subtract, in order, everything that does not become an ad a real person could see on a quality site. The ANA's third-quarter Programmatic Transparency Benchmark, dated 20 November, does this for programmatic buying. As AdExchanger reports it, of every $1 million spent, $285,000 goes to transaction costs (the fees of the platforms in between) and $284,000 to lost media productivity (fraud, unviewable placements, sites made for advertising), leaving 43.1 percent reaching quality media. In connected TV, 24 percent of spend went to inventory that could not be measured at all. The model applies to any budget that passes through intermediaries. A loyalty team can run the same subtraction on points issued versus points that changed behavior, and a product team on engineering hours versus features customers used. (AdExchanger, ANA)

#Rabbit hole (optional)

The quiet edit to ChatGPT's shopping rules: AI Visibility Journal compared archived and live copies of OpenAI's "Shopping with ChatGPT Search" help page and found that a sentence saying results were not "influenced by any OpenAI partnerships" disappeared between 4 and 15 November. The piece lays the before and after text side by side and links the archive capture. It is a short read and a useful method: the policies that decide how a brand appears in AI answers live on help pages, and the only way to know when they change is to keep copies. (AI Visibility Journal)

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Microsoft puts ads beside Bing's AI Overviews: Microsoft Advertising now shows ads next to AI Overviews on Bing in every market except China. The placement runs through existing campaigns, so advertisers are opted in without building anything new. Microsoft has announced no separate placement controls, no separate reporting, and no performance benchmarks for the slot. Signal: AI answer inventory is being sold as an extension of search campaigns before it can be measured on its own. Buyers will pay for the placement inside a blended number, and the first platform to break it out in reporting will set the benchmark everyone else is judged against. (ExchangeWire)
  • OpenAI removed the sentence saying partnerships do not influence ChatGPT shopping results: AI Visibility Journal found that the "Shopping with ChatGPT Search" help page changed between 4 and 15 November. The earlier text said product results were "selected independently by ChatGPT and are not ads, nor influenced by any OpenAI partnerships." The page now reads "Product results are not ads; how products and merchants are selected is described below." A promise to link to sites where a shopper can "learn more or purchase" became "show available ways to purchase." The listed ranking inputs are unchanged (availability, price, quality, and whether the seller is the maker), and OpenAI gave no reason for the edit. Signal: the line between an ad and a commercial arrangement is being drawn in help-page wording, with no announcement. Any brand relying on organic placement in an AI shopping surface should archive the selection policy and check it on a schedule, the way search teams track algorithm updates. (AI Visibility Journal, AdExchanger)
  • Sierra raised $1.75 billion at a $30 billion valuation: The customer service agent company doubled its valuation in six months, with Greenoaks and Lightspeed leading. It says outcome-based pricing took run-rate revenue to $400 million in five months and that it serves almost half of the Fortune 50. It is also working on agent protocol definitions with Meta, Stripe and Walmart. All figures are company-reported. Signal: the service channel is being rebuilt by a vendor that is paid per resolved outcome and sits outside the CRM suite. The customer conversation, and the data it produces, is moving to whoever runs the agent, which is a problem for any stack that assumed the CRM would stay the system of engagement. (TechCrunch)

#Category watch

Connected TV supply quality: Two reports in four days say the same thing about streaming TV inventory from different directions. On 23 November HUMAN Security disclosed BADBOX 3.0, a botnet running on cheap uncertified Android TV boxes that at its peak sent about 40 billion bid requests a day, more than 4 percent of the connected TV traffic HUMAN sees and roughly three times the previous record. The boxes played real video ads in a window the owner could not see, named well-known streaming apps in the ad request, and routed the traffic through residential proxies so it looked like household viewing. HUMAN gave no estimate of money lost and says the catalog of fraud modules is still maintained. Separately, the ANA's third-quarter programmatic benchmark, dated 20 November, reported that 24 percent of connected TV spend went to inventory that could not be measured, and that only 43.1 percent of programmatic spend reached quality media. Connected TV is the fastest-growing line in most media plans and the one with the weakest verification. A team building a media practice should treat supply verification as a named job with an owner. (PPC Land, AdExchanger, ANA)

#Stack intelligence

Marketing tools are publishing themselves into the data warehouse's assistant, under the warehouse's permissions. On 23 November Attentive, the SMS and email platform, listed an MCP server on Snowflake Marketplace. Joint customers can use Snowflake's AI tools to pull campaign and journey performance, build segments, edit campaigns and draft copy, with access governed by Snowflake's role-based controls. Two days earlier AWS did the same with Meta's ads server inside Quick. The pattern is that the execution tool becomes a set of actions callable from wherever the data and the access rules already live. That shifts two decisions to the data team: which roles may trigger a send or a budget change, and where those actions are logged. Attentive published no pricing or performance figures. (Business Wire, Agile Brand Guide)

#Foundations concept of the day

ads.txt, app-ads.txt and sellers.json: Three public files that let a buyer check who is allowed to sell an ad slot. A publisher posts ads.txt on its website, or app-ads.txt for its mobile and TV apps, listing every company authorized to sell its inventory and each one's account ID. An ad exchange posts sellers.json, listing every seller it pays and whether each is the publisher itself or a reseller. A buying platform can then compare an incoming bid request against both files: if the request claims to come from a streaming app but arrives through a seller that app never listed, it is unauthorized. The files exist because programmatic buying passes through several intermediaries and anyone can type a famous app's name into a bid request. They matter at scale because checking them is free and automatic, yet many buys still skip the check. In the BADBOX 3.0 case, HUMAN noted that the fake apps were absent from the real apps' app-ads.txt and sellers.json files "where such files exist." The same logic applies outside media: a bank's list of authorized payment processors, or an airline's list of accredited ticket agents, is a public register that lets a counterparty confirm a seller is real before money moves. (PPC Land)

#Watch list

  • Meta's Muse agent as a source of transactions: Cequence's first Agentic AI Security Index, built from six months of traffic at the businesses it protects, says Meta Muse produced 40 percent of all agent visits that took an action in its first 30 days, and that 51 percent of Muse visits signed in, booked or bought, against 9 percent for Google's shopping crawler. Cequence sells agent security, so read the numbers as a vendor's sample. (Cequence)
  • Google's patent filing for a personalized, always-on AI TV channel: 404 Media reports Google is seeking a patent on a 24/7 generated channel built around the individual viewer. A patent filing is no product plan, but generated video supply with a known viewer would be a new kind of ad inventory. (404 Media)

#Macro pulse

Canada: The TSX closed Monday up 2.69 percent at about 34,082, recovering Friday's loss, led by technology and gold stocks. The loonie traded at 69.69 US cents during the session against 69.81 on Friday, which puts USD/CAD near 1.435. The currency did not join the rally, because the driver was a single US stock and the rate gap with the US is unchanged. (Canadian Press via MoneySense, CappNotes)

USA: The S&P 500 rose 4.73 percent to 7,237.17 and the Nasdaq Composite rose 6.51 percent to 24,089.56, the best day for both since April 2025. The Dow gained 3.01 percent to 49,595.31. Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO and the stock rose 31.48 percent. The VIX fell from 35.89 to 26.62. Rates did not move with stocks: the 10-year Treasury held at 4.92 percent, and CME FedWatch put the odds of a quarter-point hike on 9 December at about 98 percent, up from 88 percent on Friday. New York Fed President John Williams said inflation near 3.5 percent means the Fed has "work to do." The flash US composite PMI slipped to 54.6 from 55.9. Before Tuesday's open, S&P 500 futures were down about 1 percent and Nasdaq-100 futures about 2 percent, with Nvidia down more than 3 percent. (CappNotes, CNBC)

Asia: Tokyo reopened after Monday's holiday and the Nikkei 225 closed Tuesday up 3.91 percent at 58,644.63. South Korea's KOSPI rose 6.32 percent to 5,006.03, with SK Hynix up 8.6 percent and Samsung up 8.2 percent. In Taiwan, TSMC rose almost 10 percent. China did not follow. The Hang Seng fell about 0.5 percent to its weakest level since April 2025 after its AI index gave up an early 6 percent gain, and the Shanghai Composite edged up about 0.5 percent to around 3,555 from Monday's 16-month low, helped by state fund buying. (CNBC, investingLive)

Oil: WTI fell 3.88 percent on Monday to $80.17, its lowest close since 26 August, and Brent fell 3.46 percent to $89.37. Libya restarted its Sharara pipeline and Middle East flows rose. Early Tuesday WTI was near $80.03 even after reports that three tankers hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz. For airlines this is the one input cost moving the right way heading into the holiday peak. The US dollar index rose 0.52 percent and EUR/USD fell to 1.0904, a 19-month low for the euro. (CappNotes, CNBC)

#Today's industry move

Sierra raised $1.75 billion at a $30 billion valuation, double the $15 billion of its round six months ago. Greenoaks and Lightspeed led, and Benchmark, Sequoia, GV and Tiger Global returned. Sierra builds AI agents for customer service, charges by outcome, and says that model doubled its run-rate revenue to $400 million in five months. It says it serves almost half of the Fortune 50, with BNY, HSBC, Wells Fargo, JPMorganChase and Singtel among named clients. The figures are the company's own and unaudited. The round was announced in the first session after an 8.6 percent weekly drop in the S&P 500, which shows private money for customer-facing agents did not pause with the public market. Separately, CrowdStrike agreed to buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal valued at $43.9 billion, pitched as security for companies adopting AI. CrowdStrike fell 8.92 percent and Zscaler rose 17.67 percent. (TechCrunch, TechCrunch)

#AI infrastructure watch

  • Nvidia: Shares closed at $165.05, up 31.48 percent, adding about $952 billion in market value in one session. The board voted unanimously to reinstate Huang eleven days after he left, said it had received input from senior levels of the US government, and said its independent investigation will continue. President Trump said he spoke with directors and "convinced them." Huang testifies publicly to the Senate Banking Committee on 3 December. (CNBC, Globe and Mail)
  • The rest of the chain moved with it: TSMC's US shares rose 14.08 percent, Arm 16.08 percent, Micron 19.04 percent, Dell 20.46 percent and CoreWeave 22.32 percent. Before Tuesday's open, Micron, Marvell, AMD, Arm and Broadcom were each down 2 to 4 percent. (CappNotes)
  • Export controls tightened the same day: The Commerce Department added Megaspeed and six linked companies in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand to its trade blacklist, effective 23 November, with licences presumed denied. One is an Alibaba unit, and Alibaba called the listing "erroneous." Morningstar's Brian Colello pointed to proposed licence requirements for chip sales to several Southeast Asian countries and Brazil and said, "We don't believe Nvidia is back to where it was before this saga started." (Fox Business, Globe and Mail)

#Signal or noise?

The size of the rally is noise, since Nomura's Charlie McElligott called it a "massive surge of MECHANICAL buying" and futures are already giving part of it back. The signal is that a 98 percent chance of a rate hike and a tighter export regime both survived the best day for stocks in nineteen months.
