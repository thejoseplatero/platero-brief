#Macro signal

Inflation rose, growth was revised up, and Washington proposed a new round of tariffs, all before the opening bell. The October PCE price index, the Fed's preferred measure, rose to 3.8 percent from 3.6 percent, the highest annual reading since 2023. Core PCE came in at 3.3 percent against a 3.2 percent forecast. Third-quarter GDP was revised to 4.3 percent annualized from 3.4 percent, personal income rose 0.7 percent, and jobless claims fell to 189,000. The same morning the US Trade Representative proposed Section 301 tariffs of up to 20 percent on 14 trading partners including China, the EU, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, after an eight-month investigation into excess manufacturing capacity. The duties need hearings and a comment period and are unlikely to take effect before January. The current trade truce with China expires on 10 January. Markets fully price a quarter-point Fed hike on 9 December and two more in 2027. Stocks opened sharply lower, with the S&P 500 down more than 2 percent and the Nasdaq more than 3 percent in the first minutes of trading. Chris Rupkey of Fwdbonds said, "Inflation is too hot and it will not be brought under control as long as economic demand is also too hot." For leaders closing 2027 plans, the consumer is still earning and spending, so the demand case holds. The cost case does not: borrowing gets more expensive in two weeks, and imported hardware and goods may get more expensive in two months. Budgets with hardware, devices or imported merchandise in them need a tariff line before they are signed. (CNBC, Yahoo Finance, CNN)

#Tech industry

  • AI hardware demand set records, and the suppliers said they cannot fill it until 2028. Dell reported revenue of $54.9 billion on Tuesday, more than double a year earlier, with $60.2 billion in new AI server orders in the quarter and a backlog of $132.3 billion. It raised its full-year revenue forecast to $202 billion from $192 billion. HPE reported record revenue of $14.55 billion the same day and its shares fell about 16 percent, because it guided its Cloud and AI operating margin down to about 14 percent. CEO Antonio Neri said, "We expect supply of memory, NAND, and many other critical components to remain constrained to at least the second half of calendar 2028." The strategic point: enterprise AI capacity is sold out at the component level for two years, and the vendors' own margins are being squeezed by the parts they cannot get. Any 2027 plan that assumes AI compute gets cheaper or easier to reserve is assuming against the suppliers' guidance. (The Next Web, Benzinga)
  • Nvidia's board added controls, and its returning CEO declined a succession plan. The company's 23 November filing creates its first chief compliance officer, reporting to the board's nominating and governance committee, alongside a National Security and Export Control Committee made up of independent directors. At an all-hands on Tuesday, Jensen Huang apologized to employees and, according to reports citing CNBC and the Financial Times, said Nvidia does not need a conventional succession plan because capable leaders already run its divisions. The shares fell 5.86 percent on Tuesday and about 8 percent at Wednesday's open. The board has built oversight around the role and left the question of who could fill it unanswered, which is the question the last two weeks raised. (Nvidia 8-K, Korea JoongAng Daily, CappNotes)

#AI at the leadership level

Telling employees what AI is for is worth about five times as much as giving them better tools. BCG's second annual Asia-Pacific AI at Work report, released on 24 November, surveyed 6,349 employees in nine markets. Eighty-five percent use AI regularly, up from 78 percent a year ago. Only 20 percent say they have strong clarity on their company's AI strategy. Among regular users, strong clarity raised measurable business impact by 22 points, and better access to tools raised it by 4. Where leadership communicates very clearly about AI, 61 percent of employees fully trust leadership. Where communication is unclear, 3 percent do. Seven percent of employees with strong clarity are anxious about AI, against 19 percent without it. The survey is self-reported and regional, so the size of the gap matters more than the exact figures. The governance consequence is that the next unit of AI budget is better spent on a leader explaining which work AI should change and why than on another licence. (BCG)

#Leadership & org design

A team's judgment gets written down when people correct a system, and rarely when they are asked to describe it. Matthew Finlayson, CTO of ActivTrak, built an agent to route engineering escalations and found that none of his seven managers could state the routing rules in advance. They could all say why a specific assignment was wrong. "Building the agent meant turning the group's fragmented judgment into explicit rules." Corrections logged on tickets took accuracy from about 60 percent to about 95 percent. He also learned what the automation costs to keep: when one team's responsibilities changed, the rules had to change with it. His test before building the next one is whether the workflow is stable or whether the team behind it is about to be reorganized. "Low operating costs mean little if the system needs constant intervention to stay useful." The practice for any leadership team: before automating a decision, have the people who make it review a first attempt, and treat their objections as the documentation the org never had. (CIO)

#Question worth asking your team this week

If we asked ten employees what AI is supposed to change in their work next year, how many would give the same answer, and who on this team owns making that answer consistent?

#Try this now

  • Tighten the target before lifting the cap on any Google campaign that bids to a target: Google's 2027 search guidance, a Think with Google Q&A with Scott Sinclair, tells advertisers to set the daily budget at roughly four times projected average daily spend. The stated reason is that Target ROAS and Target CPA campaigns now optimize to the target whatever budget is entered, across Search, Shopping, Performance Max, Demand Gen and Travel, so a budget near average spend runs out on the highest-intent days. Take the advice with one check first. Google Ads Help still says a campaign can spend up to twice its daily budget in a day and 30.4 times the daily budget in a month, so a fourfold cap also quadruples the most a campaign can bill in a month. That is an inference from the two documents, since Google has not explained how they interact. In 20 minutes: list the campaigns marked limited by budget, confirm each target is one finance would accept at four times the volume, then raise the cap on one campaign and watch the target for two weeks. (Think with Google, ExchangeWire)
  • Find out which deal sites describe your prices to ChatGPT: Digiday reported on 24 November that because Amazon blocks OpenAI's crawlers, ChatGPT takes products, discounts and prices for Amazon from third-party deal-tracking sites. Any brand whose main retailer blocks AI crawlers is in the same position: the assistant's version of this week's offer comes from a site the brand does not run. Ask ChatGPT for the best Black Friday price on your top five products, open the sources it cites, and compare each price and stock status with the live listing. Where a deal site is wrong, send it the correction this week, since that page is what the assistant will repeat through Cyber Monday. (Digiday)
  • Schedule December as separate product deals with end dates: The Harris Poll's 2026 holiday report found 79 percent of shoppers say sales and discounts are worse than they used to be, and more than half say the longer sales window makes it harder to know when to buy. Marketing Brew reports the response from Jonathan Cohen, CMO of Aquasonic and Pure Daily Care: he staggers deals product by product through the month on Amazon and TikTok. "You're not programming it as a monthlong sale." For the rest of the season, replace any sitewide banner that has run more than a week with a calendar of single-product offers, each with a stated end. (Marketing Brew)

#Case study worth stealing

ActivTrak, using managers' corrections as the specification for an agent: CTO Matthew Finlayson built an agent to route the roughly 100 customer issues a week that support escalates to engineering. The first version assigned about 60 percent correctly and sometimes invented people by combining one employee's first name with another's last name. He did not ask the seven engineering managers to write routing rules, because none of them could. "I couldn't have asked any of the managers to write the complete set of routing rules upfront." The agent instead posts its reasoning on each Jira ticket, managers give a thumbs down and say where it should have gone, and those corrections become the rules. Accuracy reached about 95 percent and each manager gets back about two hours a week. He then stopped. Managers redirect the last 5 percent by hand, because each further refinement saved less time than it cost. Any team automating a judgment call can copy the sequence: ship a version that explains itself, collect corrections where the work already happens, and name the accuracy at which the build ends. (CIO)

#Framework or mental model

The double toll: Amazon's new ChatGPT ads send shoppers to an Amazon search results page, because Amazon does not let those ads link to a product listing. A search results page is itself sponsored inventory. eMarketer's Arielle Feger named the consequence: "For brands, that creates a risk of paying twice for the same customer," once for the click that brings the shopper in and again for the placement that wins them on arrival. The test for any paid touchpoint is one question: is the page the customer lands on also ad space that brands in this category buy? Apply it to retailer display ads, affiliate links, app deep links and marketplace storefronts, and count both tolls when calculating the cost of a sale. (Digiday)

#Rabbit hole (optional)

How Gossip Harbor passed Candy Crush on ads built from distress: AdExchanger's roundup today points to two long reads on the mobile game Gossip Harbor, whose monthly in-app revenue passed Candy Crush's roughly $100 million by November according to Sensor Tower and GameRefinery data. Its ads are AI-generated scenes of infidelity, child neglect and animal abuse that have little to do with the game, a merge puzzle with a running story. Matej Lancaric covers the revenue and marketing mechanics, and Segwise breaks down the creative. Worth the hour for anyone writing rules for generated creative, because it shows what a creative system optimizes toward when the only measure is the install. (AdExchanger, Matej Lancaric, Segwise)

#Vendor & platform moves

  • Amazon is buying ChatGPT ads while blocking OpenAI's crawlers: Digiday reported on 24 November that Amazon is running Black Friday ads in ChatGPT in the US, its first round since Prime Day. The ads land on Amazon search results pages, because Amazon does not allow ChatGPT ads to link to product listings. Since OpenAI cannot read Amazon's product pages, ChatGPT takes Amazon prices and discounts from third-party deal-tracking sites. It is unclear whether Amazon bought direct or through its own DSP, which has resold ChatGPT inventory to select US advertisers since September. Both companies declined to comment. Amazon's US search ad revenue is forecast at $40.57 billion this year, close to a quarter of all US search ad spending. Signal: the largest retail media seller is now a buyer on the surface that threatens it. It wants the assistant's traffic and refuses the assistant its data, and the price of that refusal is that someone else's site describes Amazon's offers. (Digiday, AdExchanger)
  • Google told advertisers to set daily budgets at about four times average spend: In a Think with Google Q&A on 2027 planning, Scott Sinclair, head of Search+ for EMEA, said campaigns on Target ROAS and Target CPA now optimize to the stated target whatever daily budget is entered, across Search, Shopping, Performance Max, Demand Gen and Travel. Google's argument is that a budget-limited campaign used to overshoot its target and then look less efficient when the budget rose, which gave a false picture of diminishing returns. The guidance arrives a month after Alphabet's chief business officer said AI Mode was eating into revenue from ads beside traditional results. Signal: the budget is being retired as the advertiser's spending control and the target is taking its place. An advertiser who accepts this holds one number, and the platform's model decides how much inventory that number buys. (Think with Google, ExchangeWire)
  • TikTok signed its first UK news publisher to Pulse Premiere: Mail Metro Media, the sales arm of DMG Media, sells advertising for the Daily Mail, Metro, The i Paper and New Scientist and has more than 40 million followers across nine TikTok channels. Until now a brand could reach that audience on TikTok only by commissioning branded content. Under the deal, reported by Press Gazette, advertisers can buy ads that play directly after Mail and Metro videos, and revenue is shared between TikTok and the publisher. Signal: publisher adjacency is becoming a line item inside the platform's own buying tool. The publisher earns a share on an audience it could not monetize directly, and the platform keeps the advertiser relationship and the data. (ExchangeWire)

#Category watch

Retail media attribution: Retail media grew on one property: the ad and the purchase happen on the same site, so the retailer can show which ad led to which sale. Mike Shields argued on 24 November that AI assistants break that link, because shoppers research in the assistant and buy somewhere else, and that Amazon and OpenAI "may be on a collision course" over who owns the transaction. He notes Amazon's ad business shows no dent so far, and that eMarketer forecasts retail media at $300 billion by 2030. The IAB published an AI attribution framework on 13 November to account for the ways an assistant can influence a purchase. Shields's conclusion is that "brands have to give up some of the magic, perfect attribution they enjoy today." For someone building a martech org, the practical consequence is that retail media reporting will show a shrinking share of the path to purchase, and incrementality testing has to carry more of the budget case. (Next in Media, AdExchanger)

#Stack intelligence

Where the data lives is choosing the agent platform. ActivTrak's CTO, Matthew Finlayson, described rebuilding one internal agent four times in a year: first in Claude Code with a Jira connector, then in the low-code tool n8n, then on Anthropic's hosted agent platform, and finally on Google's agent platform. The last two moves had the same cause. The agent needed the codebase and the issue tracker, and those sat in the cloud where the company runs production. He wrote that he had not expected data access to decide the platform. He also found that swapping the model changed the agent's behavior with the prompts, connectors and guardrails held constant, so testing has to continue after launch. This is the same pull seen this week when Attentive and Meta published their tools into Snowflake and AWS: agents and the tools they call are settling next to the governed data. A stack owner should expect the cloud and warehouse contracts to set the agent platform, and should plan model changes as releases that need a regression test. (CIO)

#Foundations concept of the day

Budget pacing: Pacing is how an ad platform spreads a budget across time. An advertiser gives a daily or monthly amount, and the platform decides hour by hour how much of it to commit, because demand is uneven and spending evenly would miss the moments when buyers are most active. Two controls sit side by side. The budget is a ceiling on how much money goes out. The target, such as a cost per acquisition or a return on ad spend, is a rule about which opportunities are worth buying. When the budget is tight, the ceiling ends the day early and the campaign misses late demand. When the budget is loose, the target does all the deciding and spend rises and falls with the number of opportunities that qualify. Most platforms also allow overdelivery: Google can spend up to twice the daily budget on a busy day and evens it out so the month does not exceed 30.4 times the daily figure. It matters at scale because the control an advertiser relies on determines who carries the risk. A ceiling protects cash and gives up sales. A target protects efficiency and makes monthly spend a forecast. A hotel chain that caps daily spend will go dark by mid-afternoon on the day a storm cancels flights and stranded travelers search for rooms. One that paces to a target will buy that demand and report a spend figure nobody planned. (Think with Google)

#Watch list

  • Generated creative optimized on installs alone: The mobile game Gossip Harbor passed Candy Crush's roughly $100 million in monthly in-app revenue by November, per Sensor Tower and GameRefinery data cited by AdExchanger, on AI-generated ads showing infidelity, child neglect and animal abuse that bear little relation to the game. It is the clearest current example of what an automated creative loop produces without a brand rule, and platform policy teams will be asked about it. (AdExchanger)
  • Platforms removing generated supply: Variety reports, via AdExchanger, that Tidal will ban AI-generated music and remove 29 million AI songs, saying 96 percent of AI tracks get zero plays. It had cut their royalties five months ago. If a streaming service decides generated supply costs more to host and police than it earns, ad-supported platforms carrying generated video and audio face the same sum. (AdExchanger)

#Macro pulse

Canada: The TSX closed Tuesday down 0.35 percent at 33,962.25, with materials off 2.5 percent as gold fell 2.64 percent to $3,555. The loonie closed at 70.08 US cents, up 0.47 of a cent, which puts USD/CAD near 1.427. That is a move of about 0.7 percent in the Canadian dollar's favor, large enough to flag, and it helps a Canadian carrier's US-dollar costs for fuel and aircraft. The US dollar index fell 0.47 percent the same day, and Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem spoke in Toronto on keeping the 2 percent inflation target for another five years. The Bank's next rate decision is 9 December. (Baystreet, CappNotes, Bank of Canada)

USA: Tuesday split the market. The Dow rose 0.85 percent to 50,018.70, closing above 50,000. The S&P 500 fell 0.50 percent to 7,201.09, its eighth loss in nine sessions, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.48 percent to 23,733.54. Technology lost 3.26 percent while health care gained 2.95 percent, and 346 of the S&P 500's stocks rose. Conference Board consumer confidence jumped 11.4 points to 103.3 against a 92.2 forecast. Wednesday's data arrived hot. October PCE inflation rose to 3.8 percent and core to 3.3 percent against a 3.2 percent forecast. Third-quarter GDP was revised to 4.3 percent from 3.4 percent, personal income rose 0.7 percent, and jobless claims fell to 189,000. The US Trade Representative also proposed tariffs of up to 20 percent on 14 trading partners. CME pricing puts a quarter-point Fed hike on 9 December at about 98 percent. In the first minutes of Wednesday trading the S&P 500 was down more than 2 percent, the Nasdaq more than 3 percent and the Dow about 700 points, and the VIX was above 31. (CappNotes, CNBC, Yahoo Finance)

Asia: Tuesday's rebound reversed on Wednesday. The Nikkei 225 fell about 2.8 percent to 57,011.62, and South Korea's KOSPI dropped close to 6 percent during the session, with Samsung and SK Hynix each down more than 8 percent. Reuters reported foreign investors as net sellers, and the tariff proposal names Japan, South Korea and Taiwan. China moved the other way at the index level: AP reported the Hang Seng up about 1 percent to 22,449.86, recovering from Tuesday's lowest close in more than a year, and the Shanghai Composite down 0.9 percent to 3,515.05. Published closing figures for the two Chinese indexes vary between reports. (AP via Yahoo Finance, investingLive)

Oil: WTI rose 2.35 percent on Tuesday to $82.05 and Brent 2.27 percent to $91.40 after attacks on vessels near the Strait of Hormuz, which takes back most of Monday's fall. WTI was near $82.70 early Wednesday. For airlines, the fuel relief of Monday lasted one session, and it reversed during a week in which the TSA expects to screen about 17.6 million passengers. (CappNotes, The Hill)

#Today's industry move

Anthropic closed below its IPO price. The shares fell 6.54 percent on Tuesday to $81.90, under the $83 offering price, according to the CappNotes closing recap, which is AI-assisted and worth checking against an exchange quote. It is the first time the only pure AI model company on a public market has traded below what IPO buyers paid, and it happened on a day when the Dow set a record. Public investors are now marking the model layer down with the chip layer, which will weigh on how private rounds at OpenAI and xAI are priced. (CappNotes)

#AI infrastructure watch

Full-year revenue guidance rose to $202 billion from $192 billion and AI server revenue guidance to $78 billion from $74 billion. The stock fell 11.83 percent in Tuesday's session before the report and gained a few percent after hours. (The Next Web)

(Benzinga, Motley Fool)

Nvidia fell about another 8 percent at Wednesday's open. The proposed tariffs exclude certain semiconductors and do not stack on existing chip duties. (CappNotes, Yahoo Finance)
  • Energy: US natural gas rose 15.30 percent on Tuesday to $3.671 per million BTU on a cold snap and short covering. Gas is the marginal fuel for much of the US grid, so a sustained move feeds into data center power costs. (CappNotes)

#Signal or noise?

Signal. HPE fell 16 percent on record revenue and Dell fell 12 percent ahead of a record quarter, because the parts to fill the orders are short until 2028 and margins are falling. The market has moved from doubting AI demand to doubting who profits from supplying it.
